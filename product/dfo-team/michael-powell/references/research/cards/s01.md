### S055 · Recent research at Cambridge on radial basis functions (New Developments in Approximation Theory 1999, DOI 10.1007/978-3-0348-8696-3_14)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none. The acknowledgements credit Goodsell (calculations and the residual-reuse idea), Faul, Gutmann and Bejancu (p. 17). · **Position in trajectory**: DAMTP 1998/NA05 (June 1998), a talk at the 2nd International Dortmund Meeting on Approximation Theory (p. 1). It is a status report on the Cambridge RBF group, written between COBYLA (1994) and UOBYQA (2002). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–18.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Three open topics are surveyed (p. 1, p. 3).
  1. The thin-plate-spline (TPS) interpolation equations when n is very large, up to 10^5. The matrix has no useful sparsity (p. 3).
  2. Where to place the next evaluation of an expensive, derivative-free f in global optimization. This topic was triggered by Jones (1996) (p. 3, p. 8).
  3. Loss of TPS accuracy near the edges of finite grids, studied in one dimension (pp. 3–4).

  Each topic is entered through a numerical fact: the need for O(n log n) work per iteration (p. 7), or orders of accuracy seen in experiments that theory has not confirmed (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *§2 (iterative solver).* Interpolation is rewritten as Newton's method with nested Lagrange functions on {x_k, …, x_n}, with coefficients (2.10) (pp. 4–6). The practical method replaces each Lagrange function by the one built on the q ≈ 30 nearest points, then corrects the result by iterative refinement. Each iteration cuts the largest residual by about a factor of 10 (pp. 6–7). Goodsell's variant reuses the residuals from the start of the iteration. This cuts the work by about a factor of q at similar iteration counts. The link to Newton's method was noticed only while seeking a theoretical explanation of that experiment (p. 7).
  - *§3 (global optimization).* Choose x_{n+1} to minimise the RBF semi-norm of the interpolant that also takes a target value f* at x_{n+1} (p. 8). The new Lagrange function is orthogonal to s, which gives the closed form (3.27) (p. 13). A very low f* pushes points into large gaps, and f* just below min s refines near the minimum (p. 9).
  - *§4 (edge accuracy).* Replacing the side conditions (4.3) by end-derivative conditions, or by minimising Σλ_j², raises the observed edge accuracy from O(h^{3/2}) to O(h^{5/2}) (pp. 15–16). Minimising Σλ_j² loses invariance under scaling of x (4.12), and adding a quadratic polynomial restores it (pp. 16–17).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: P has full rank (for d = 2 TPS, the points are not collinear) (p. 2). φ is conditionally positive definite of order m0 (p. 2). In §3, f is expensive and has no derivatives (p. 8). In §4, the grid on [0, 1] is uniform and f is smooth (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique** (brief): One full proof. The RBF interpolant minimises the semi-norm (3.18) among all interpolants in the space. The proof pads the centre set with zero coefficients and uses conditional positive definiteness, (3.10)–(3.15) (pp. 10–11). This generalises Duchon's TPS variational property and is Schaback's Theorem 4 (pp. 9–12). §2 and §4 are driven by experiments and end in conjectures (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: Tables of |e(h/2)| for n = 80–640 under several f and side conditions, with rates read off by halving h (Tables 1–3, pp. 14–16). Point sets were constructed deliberately to make the fast §2 variant diverge, e.g. n = 1000 on two concentric quarter circles of radii 1 and 1+10^{-5} (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Beatson–Powell (1992, 1994), Powell (1997), Duchon (1977), Schaback (1993) and Jones (1996) (pp. 3, 8, 9, 12). It criticises no one.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A group report whose open questions are marked as open (pp. 1, 7, 14). Each student's contribution is named precisely in the acknowledgements (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A status report, not finished results.
  - The cheaper variant fails on constructed examples, so an intermediate version is under study (p. 7).
  - Global-optimization algorithms are only hoped for (p. 9).
  - The edge orders are conjectures (pp. 14–15).
  - The most accurate 1-D variant is still not recommended over cubic splines (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. Rates are read from halving tables and kept as conjectures (pp. 14–16). Failures of the cheaper variant are reported (p. 7). A recommendation is withheld when the new method does not beat the standard one (p. 17).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. Per-iteration work is O(n log n) through Laurent expansions (p. 7), and the residual-reuse trick is valued for its q-fold saving (p. 7).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (outside DFO). The robust old version and the cheap new version lead to an intermediate version that keeps the old reliability with part of the new savings (p. 7).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (same principle, different norm). The interpolant is the minimum semi-norm element subject to the interpolation conditions (pp. 9–12). The effect of adding one data value is expressed through a Lagrange function (3.25)–(3.27) (p. 13). This is the minimum-norm-subject-to-interpolation principle that NEWUOA later applies with the Frobenius norm of the Hessian change. Here it uses an RBF native semi-norm and serves point selection (pp. 8–13). The paper shows no causal link to the later work.
- Bridge to DFO: §3 is RBF-model optimization of an expensive derivative-free f (p. 8). This matches the memoir's account that RBF models came first (card c06, R007 p. 17).
- New-pattern candidate: *adversarial test-case construction for one's own fast method*. Recent work tries to build point sets that make the procedure diverge (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *numerics → conjecture → proof or counterexample* ✅ (existing candidate). The tables yield conjectured orders, and analytic work on them has begun (p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *invariance as a veto on a more accurate variant*. The Σλ_j² variant is repaired because it loses scaling invariance, even though it is more accurate (pp. 16–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D5] Estimate an order from a halving table (a factor of about 2√2 per halving suggests O(h^{3/2})). Then construct an f that cancels the leading error term, and check whether the order rises (Tables 1–2, pp. 14–15).
- [D2] Before adopting a more accurate variant, test it for invariance under a simple rescaling of the variables. Repair or reject it if invariance is lost (4.10)–(4.12) (pp. 16–17).
- [D2] Turn a rule for where to sample next into a closed-form merit function by using the orthogonality of the new Lagrange function to the current interpolant (3.24)–(3.27) (p. 13).
- [D5] When a cheaper variant of a working method appears, spend effort building data that make it diverge, and report what you find (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "The year old version seems to be very robust, but some examples of failure have been found for the newer version that usually requires much less computation." (p. 7)
- "We do not recommend, however, that the new method should be used in practice instead of cubic spline interpolation." (p. 17)

### S075 · Proof of convergence of an iterative technique for thin plate spline interpolation in two dimensions (Advances in Computational Mathematics 1999, DOI 10.1023/a:1018923925800)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. C. Faul (her 2001 Cambridge PhD dissertation on iterative RBF techniques is cited in S057 p. 23; the supervisor is not named in the files read) · **Position in trajectory**: DAMTP 1998/NA08 (September 1998) (p. 1). It supplies the proof that was missing for the iterative method of Beatson, Goodsell and Powell surveyed in S055. **Read**: full text, pp. 1–10.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The direct solution costs O(n³), which is prohibitive for n > 1000 (p. 3). The algorithm of Powell (1997) worked in practice (10 decimals in fewer than 10 iterations for n around 1000; the relation symbol is garbled in the text file, p. 7), but its convergence analysis was thought to be very hard. The earlier route, through the spectral radius of the matrix R that maps residuals across one iteration, had not succeeded (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: This is a generalisation of the algorithm.
  - Each iteration has n − q + 1 stages.
  - Stage k uses q of the last n − k + 1 equations (a set L_k that contains k, with the other points not collinear) to build an approximate Lagrange function. It then adds (μ_k / λ̂_kk) times that function to s, with μ_k from the current residuals (2.5). The paper calls this analogous to forward substitution (pp. 3–4).
  - A final stage interpolates the last q residuals exactly (2.6) (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The data points are distinct and not collinear, and each L_k \ {k} is not collinear (p. 3). §4 extends the method to any conditionally positive or negative definite function, with q > dim Π_m and no L_k \ {k} lying in the zero set of a polynomial of degree ≤ m (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique** (brief):
  - *Lemma.* Every stage is an exact minimisation of the native semi-norm (s* − s, s* − s) along the approximate Lagrange function. Integration by parts shows that the algorithm's coefficient is exactly the minimiser (3.1)–(3.2). The last stage is an orthogonal projection onto the subspace T (3.4) (p. 5).
  - *Theorem.* The semi-norm values are monotone and bounded below, so identity (3.6) forces the inner products with each approximate Lagrange function to 0. Induction over the stages and back-substitution through the triangular system (3.9), whose diagonal is nonzero, then give s_m(x_i) → s*(x_i). Finite dimension makes all norms equivalent (p. 6).
  - No rate is proved.
- **D5 Experiments**: Four point sets, with n = 400 and 900, q = 10–50, random right-hand sides and tolerance 10^{-8}. They are a circle, a square grid, a random disc, and Problem D, which was found by seeking slow convergence (two concentric eighth-circles of radii 1 and 1+10^{-5}). The table reports iteration counts beside the largest normalised scalar product (5.1), including three runs stopped after 1000 iterations (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: Contrasts its proof with the spectral-radius approach of Beatson, Goodsell and Powell (1995) (pp. 4–5). It credits Beatson with the original idea and Goodsell with the programs that shaped the algorithm (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Short and centred on the proof. The expectation about speed (orthogonality of the approximate Lagrange functions) is stated before the experiments (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Convergence is proved, with no rate. The method performs well when (5.1) < 0.1. This correlation is offered to guide the choice of L_k in future work (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. Non-converged runs are kept in the table, with their final residuals (p. 9).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant. The proof rests on the variational property of TPS interpolation (least (s, s) subject to interpolation, (1.5)–(1.6), p. 2), the same kind of minimum-norm principle as least-change updating.
- New-pattern candidate: *native-norm descent proof*. Prove that each step of a heuristic solver exactly minimises the error in the norm for which the solution is optimal. Monotonicity plus finite dimension then give convergence (pp. 5–6). The same device appears in S085 and S074.
- New-pattern candidate: *adversarial test-case construction for one's own fast method* ✅ (Problem D, p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate: *numerics → conjecture → proof or counterexample* ✅. The method worked in practice (p. 7), then the proof followed (pp. 5–6). A conjecture about speed was then tested numerically (pp. 9–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] When the residual-iteration matrix is intractable, look for a norm in which each stage is an exact line search. Monotone decrease then needs no spectral estimate (pp. 4–5).
- [D4] Order the stages so that the limits (inner products → 0) form a triangular system with a nonzero diagonal. Back-substitution then converts them into residuals → 0 (3.9) (p. 6).
- [D5] Tabulate, next to the iteration counts, the quantity your analysis says controls the speed (the largest normalised scalar product (5.1)), so the correlation can be seen (pp. 9–10).

**Quotes**:
- "The analysis of convergence for the algorithm was thought to be very hard." (p. 4)
- "Problem D was found by seeking a case where the convergence of the method of Section 2 is very slow." (p. 9)

### S085 · Krylov subspace methods for radial basis function interpolation (DAMTP report 1999/NA11; published in Numerical Analysis 1999, eds. Griffiths and Watson, Chapman & Hall 2000, pp. 115–141, per the reference list of S057 p. 23; no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. C. Faul · **Position in trajectory**: August 1999, presented at the 18th Dundee Biennial Conference (p. 1). It adds a convergence safeguard to the S055/S075 line and becomes the base of S074. **Read**: full text, pp. 1–25. Figure 1 (p. 20) is garbled in the text file; only its caption was readable.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The O(n³) direct method limits n to about one thousand (p. 4). Goodsell's cheaper method occasionally fails to converge, so a Krylov subspace technique was added to it (p. 5). The paper then reframes the whole line of work: the Krylov construction becomes the main part, and five years of development reduce to the choice of one self-adjoint operator A (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Iteration k finds the element of the Krylov space S_k = span{A^j s*} that is closest to s* in the native semi-norm (3.1) (p. 8). It is implemented like conjugate gradients, (3.2)–(3.3) (p. 9).
  - The enabler is identity (2.6): ⟨s, s*⟩ can be computed from the data before s* is known (p. 7).
  - The recommended A sums projections onto approximate Lagrange functions built on about q = 30 nearest points, plus an exact q-point interpolation operator H, (4.10)–(4.11) (pp. 14–16). The design goal is an A close to the identity (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The data points are distinct and polynomially unisolvent. φ is conditionally definite with sign σ (Table 1, pp. 3–4). For TPS every L_j \ {j} must be non-collinear. This is enforced by putting three far-from-collinear points into every L_j (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique** (brief):
  - Four sufficient conditions on A (3.6): nonsingularity, polynomial reproduction, ellipticity and self-adjointness (p. 9).
  - Induction gives conjugacy, strict decrease of the error semi-norm, and termination within n − m̂ + 1 iterations (pp. 10–12).
  - The role of each condition is explained, including a Gram–Schmidt form (3.21) for an A that is not self-adjoint (pp. 12–13).
  - Ellipticity of the recommended A is shown by a greatest-index argument (4.19)–(4.20) (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: Four point sets (circle, grid, random disc, and two arcs whose gap is 10^{-5} of the radius), random data, ε = 10^{-8}, n = 400 and 900, q = 10, 30, 50, with φ = r and φ = r² log r. Iteration counts are in Tables 2–3 (p. 21). They are compared with the plain iteration (5.1), whose rate is the spectral radius of I − A (p. 21). Random orderings were also tried (p. 23).
- **D6 Positioning**: Like GMRES, except that A need not come from the equations and the semi-norm need not be a norm (p. 8). It follows Dyn–Levin–Rippa conjugate gradients and Beatson–Powell (1994) (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Candid about its own theory: the termination proof is called misleading about practice (p. 13). It narrates how each test problem shaped the algorithm (pp. 19–20).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A convergence guarantee, with large gains in difficult cases, for O(n) extra work per iteration (p. 22). Open issues are the slow Problem 4 with TPS (p. 22), the data ordering (pp. 22–23) and d ≥ 3 (p. 23). Software was promised only after that future work (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (outside DFO). The sequence Beatson–Powell 1994 → Powell 1997 → Goodsell's cheaper variant → Krylov safeguard keeps the machinery at each step and removes one limitation: cost first, then occasional divergence (p. 5).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence, including a negative result. The Krylov technique pays off only when the spectral radius of I − A exceeds 0.5. On the easy problems it saves at most a third of the iterations (pp. 21–22).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence.
  - The extra cost of the technique is O(n), against O(nq) and O(n²) for the rest of the iteration (p. 22).
  - The product is formed with the search direction rather than the new iterate, to avoid inconsistencies that rounding errors could cause between residuals and coefficients (p. 18).
- New-pattern candidate: *native-norm descent proof* ✅ (pp. 8–12).
- New-pattern candidate: *safeguard designed so that the proof goes through* ✅ (existing candidate). The Krylov step turns an occasionally divergent heuristic into a method with guaranteed termination, and it is cheap enough to include always (p. 22).
- New-pattern candidate: *recast a working heuristic into a principled framework after the fact* (p. 5, p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *adversarial test-case construction for one's own fast method* ✅. Problem 4 came from looking for divergence (p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Abstract the method into an operator A and list only the properties the proof needs (3.6). For each concrete A, only those need checking (p. 9).
- [D4] Find a quantity computable before the solution is known, here ⟨s, s*⟩ from the data (2.6), so that an exact line search on the error norm becomes possible (p. 7, p. 19).
- [D5] Report the price of a safeguard: when it helps, when it does not, and its marginal cost. Argue for including it because hard cases cannot be recognised in advance (p. 22).
- [D7] Tell readers plainly when a worst-case bound misrepresents practice (p. 13, p. 20).

**Quotes**:
- "The proof of termination in the previous section is misleading, because it suggests that the number of iterations of the Krylov subspace method may be close to n." (p. 13)
- "These remarks suggest correctly that there is little advantage in including the Krylov subspace technique when solving most radial basis function interpolation problems." (p. 22)

### S170 · An optimal way of moving a sequence of points onto a curve in two dimensions (Computational Optimization and Applications 13 1999, DOI 10.1023/a:1008617208706)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none · **Position in trajectory**: DAMTP 1997/NA21 (December 1997, revised May 1998). It is dedicated to Olvi Mangasarian (p. 1), and the work was done at the University of Canterbury, Christchurch (p. 26). **Read**: §§1–2 in full (pp. 2–11); §3 on pp. 11–14 and its last paragraph on p. 21; §4 in full (pp. 21–26). **Not read**: pp. 15–20, the bookkeeping of the splittings and the analytic sub-calculations.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The pull came from an application. Image registration by mapping curves into curves (Powell 1996) needs good initial parameters t_i (p. 2). The problem is to minimise the largest distance ‖x_i − s(t_i)‖ subject to 0 ≤ t_1 ≤ … ≤ t_n ≤ T, with ties broken by Chebyshev's lexicographic ordering (p. 3). The curve, built from arcs and segments, may fluctuate and intersect itself, so local minima occur. The algorithm nevertheless finds the global solution (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The design exploits the fact that the objective is a maximum.
  - For a trial h, a greedy forward sweep decides whether h ≥ h* or h < h* (Lemma 1, p. 4). A backward sweep gives upper bounds (Corollary 2, pp. 4–5).
  - Bisection on h keeps certified bounds on every t*_i (2.4) (p. 5).
  - When the bounds separate, the problem splits into independent subproblems (Lemma 3, p. 5). One-sided splittings are also possible (Lemma 4, p. 6; Lemma 6, p. 10).
  - A cheap starting h0 comes from the discrete joins of the curve (pp. 11–12).
  - Bisection occasionally tries h = h+ so that the bounds stay useful (pp. 13–14).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The curve is made of circular arcs and straight segments with a continuous direction (pp. 2–3). The analysis also holds in higher dimensions (p. 11). The bracket tolerance is at least h0 times the relative machine precision (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique** (brief):
  - A greedy sweep plus induction (Lemma 1, p. 4).
  - An exchange argument for splitting, which is proved formally even though it looks obvious (Lemma 3, p. 5).
  - Pathological degenerate examples show that the cheap final choice t_p = τ_p(h1) can move a point further than necessary. Instead of treating those cases, Lemma 5 proves that the choice is exactly optimal for the perturbed problem (2.8), whose perturbation is the bracket width h1 − h2 (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Timing tests.* Rounded-hexagon curves with one cycle or n/125 cycles, n = 125–8000, data perturbed by 0, δ or 10δ; CPU seconds on a Sparc 5/170 (Table 1, p. 22). Random arc angles were also tried (p. 22).
  - *Hard case.* A constructed case (data on a circle well inside the curve) costs O(n²) work. This is explained analytically (4.5)–(4.8) and measured (Table 2, pp. 23–24).
  - *Accuracy check.* Each problem is also solved with the direction and the ordering reversed, and the two answers are compared (4.10) (p. 25).
- **D6 Positioning**: The minimax objective is set against a sum-of-squares formulation as a very different problem (p. 3). The image-registration literature is cited for motivation (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Surprisingly small errors are called incredible. The hypothesis that most parameters were set analytically is then checked with extra program output (pp. 25–26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A global solution with typically O(n) total work (p. 1, p. 22). The limits are stated concretely:
  - the work is O(n²) when the data are far from the curve relative to their spacing (pp. 23–24);
  - the bisection route has only square-root-of-tolerance accuracy in theory (p. 25);
  - the author knows of no application that needs high accuracy (p. 26).

  Further analytic techniques are the stated future work (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. A self-constructed bad case is reported with a table and an analysis (pp. 23–24). Accuracy is self-tested by symmetry (p. 25).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The tolerance is tied to machine precision (p. 12), and total work is stated as O(n) versus O(n²) (p. 3, p. 24).
- New-pattern candidate: *justify a practical choice by exact optimality for a nearby problem* (Lemma 5, pp. 7–8).
- New-pattern candidate: *adversarial test-case construction for one's own fast method* ✅ (pp. 23–24).
- New-pattern candidate: *explain a surprise with a designed follow-up experiment* ✅ (existing candidate) (pp. 25–26).
- Problem choice: the closing belief that a new algorithm can create its own applications (p. 26) fits the interview statement that he seeks fields that may benefit from an algorithm he has in mind (card c02, X002 p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] For a minimax problem, replace the optimisation by feasibility tests (is h ≥ h*?), each answered by a greedy sweep, and bisect on h (pp. 4–5).
- [D2] Keep certified two-sided bounds on each variable from every trial. When the bounds of neighbouring variables separate, split the problem (Lemma 3, p. 5).
- [D4] If a cheap choice is hard to prove optimal because of degenerate cases, prove instead that it is exactly optimal for a problem perturbed at the level of the bracket width (pp. 7–8).
- [D5] Test accuracy without knowing the answer: solve the mirrored problem (reversed curve and ordering) and compare the two solutions (p. 25).

**Quotes**:
- "Although it seems obvious that this splitting is valid, we present a formal proof." (p. 5)
- "he has found in similar situations that the development of a new algorithm can create applications, because many computer users restrict attention to numerical computations that can be solved routinely by available software." (p. 26)

### S121 · On the convergence of the DFP algorithm for unconstrained optimization when there are only two variables (Mathematical Programming 87(2) 2000, DOI 10.1007/s101070050115)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none · **Position in trajectory**: June 1999, dedicated to W. C. Davidon for his 70th birthday (p. 1). It returns to the DFP method of 1963 while the DFO codes were being developed. **Read**: §1 (pp. 2–4); §2 (pp. 4–9); the overview of §3 with Lemma 3.1 and its proof (pp. 9–12); the conclusion of §4 and Theorem 4.3 (pp. 17, 21–22); §5 (pp. 22–23). **Not read**: the proofs of Lemmas 3.2–3.5 (pp. 12–17) and of Lemmas 4.1–4.2 (pp. 18–21).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: DFP convergence theorems for general smooth functions began around 1970 and assumed convexity (p. 2). The target setting is a bounded level set with continuous second derivatives (1.1), and exact line searches that stop at the first local minimum (pp. 2–3). Powell admits that this line search cannot be used in practice. The motivation is to help explain why DFP succeeds, not to improve software (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - The key reduction is that for n = 2 the exact line search and the quasi-Newton equation fix the direction d_{k+1} through (1.5). The matrices B_k can therefore be removed from the analysis (p. 3).
  - Polak–Ribière directions satisfy the same conditions. Powell's own counterexamples for that method (1984) break monotone decrease along the line search. This identifies the monotonicity in Step 3 as the hypothesis the proof must use (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: n = 2; conditions (1.1); each step length is the largest α for which F decreases monotonically along the line (Step 3, p. 2). Convexity is not needed (p. 1).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique** (brief): The proof is by contradiction, assuming ‖g_k‖ > ε for all k (1.7) (p. 4).
  1. *The limit set of the iterate path.* The set T of limit points of the piecewise-linear path through the iterates is closed, connected, free of stationary points and collinear (Lemmas 2.1–2.4, pp. 5–8). The argument uses the known bound Σcos²θ_k < ∞ (2.7) (p. 7).
  2. *Normalisation.* Without loss of generality T is the segment from (−1, 0) to (1, 0), and the search directions become parallel to it (pp. 8–9).
  3. *The contradiction.* ‖d_k‖ → ∞ (3.4) (p. 9). The iterations are split by sign into the sets K_same and K_opp (3.6). The ratio ‖d_k‖/‖g_k‖ is shown to be non-increasing along K_opp, which contradicts (3.4) (p. 10).

  The intricate bound (1.9) is isolated, and readers are advised to study §4 first (p. 4, p. 10). The result is Theorem 4.3 (pp. 21–22).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Powell (1972, Theorem 2), Dixon (1972), Wolfe (1970) and Powell's 1984 CG counterexamples (pp. 2–4, 7).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A personal opening: Davidon's work helped his career greatly (p. 2). The paper closes by calling its result a small contribution (p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The theorem is limited to n = 2, because in three dimensions the path can pass around the line T (p. 22). Dai and Powell looked for an n = 3 counterexample as follows (pp. 22–23):
  - fix a self-similar iterate sequence (5.1);
  - write the DFP and line-search conditions on it as nonlinear constraints;
  - search for a feasible point.

  Months of work had failed, so the paper was deferred (p. 23).

**Method links**:

### S057 · Radial basis function methods for interpolation to functions of many variables (HERCMA 2001; published as HERMIS 3 (2002) 1–24 per the reference list of S074 p. 29; no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none. The algorithm is credited to collaboration with Faul and Goodsell (p. 23). · **Position in trajectory**: DAMTP 2001/NA11 (October 2001, revised July 2002), a talk at the Fifth Hellenic-European Conference on Computer Mathematics and its Applications (p. 1). **Read**: §1 (pp. 2–7), §2 (pp. 7–12), §3 on pp. 12–13 and 17–19, and §4 (pp. 19–22). **Not read**: the details of the conjugate-gradient-like method on pp. 14–16.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - *Why RBFs.* In two or more dimensions, any fixed space of continuous basis functions has point positions that make the interpolation matrix singular (exchange two points continuously). The RBF spaces avoid this (p. 2).
  - *The question.* When the data lie on a low-dimensional nonlinear manifold in R^d, as in data mining (e.g. life expectancy from answers to d questions), does the number of points needed for good accuracy depend on d (p. 1, p. 6)?
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: A review of nonsingularity, polynomial terms and accuracy on infinite grids, where the order m equals d for linear and multiquadric φ (pp. 2–6). It is followed by experiments that use the Faul–Goodsell–Powell iterative solver, a method like conjugate gradients built on approximate Lagrange functions on q points (pp. 12–13, 17).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Linear or multiquadric φ plus a constant (2.1) (p. 7). The Gaussian test function has its width fixed by min f(x_i) = 0.01 (2.4) (p. 7). The manifolds are built from a distorted sine with random phases, so that they are nonlinear, bounded and without edges (2.5)–(2.7) (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique** (brief): At the level of a review. Nonsingularity follows from conditional negative definiteness (p. 3). TPS without a polynomial term fails at the vertices of a regular simplex, where Φ = 0 (p. 4). No new theorem.
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Accuracy.* d = 5–40 and n = 64–4096, on regular grids with and without edges, on random points, and with c > 0 (Tables 1–4, pp. 8–12).
  - *Iteration counts.* q = 10, 30, 50, random right-hand sides, residual tolerance 10^{-8} (Tables 5–6, pp. 18–19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Draws on Hardy, Micchelli, Buhmann and Bejancu (pp. 2–6). It acknowledges that fast multipole methods (Greengard–Rokhlin; Cherrie–Beatson–Newsam) are missing from his own code (p. 21).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A concrete application example motivates the manifold question (p. 6). Caveats are explicit: the random-point figures only indicate accuracy (p. 10). Surprises are flagged as such (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Both the accuracy on the manifold and the iteration counts are nearly independent of d (Tables 1 and 6, pp. 8–9, 19). The limits are reported in the same paper:
  - for random points in the ball with d ≥ 5, the iteration count grows like √n (p. 18);
  - the multiquadric needs more iterations than the linear φ (p. 19);
  - the code is slow beyond 5000 points on a Sun Sparc 10, because of the O(n²) product and the storage it needs (p. 21);
  - an attempt to improve the Lagrange approximations returned the same coefficients (p. 22).

  The Fortran program is available from the author (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence, with a ⚠ variant: the tests are randomised families swept over d and n, not a few hand-picked problems (pp. 7–12, 18–19). Unfavourable results are reported (pp. 18–19).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. Costs are given as O(nq) per iteration plus the O(n²) product, and O(nq³) for preliminary work (p. 17). The slowdown caused by storage is quantified as a multiple of n²d (p. 21).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the program is offered by the author (p. 22).
- New-pattern candidate: *test the regime users actually have (low-dimensional manifolds in high d), not only the worst case* (p. 6, Tables 1–2 and 6).
- New-pattern candidate: *close a design avenue with a theorem*. The q = 50 → q = 30 coefficient idea turns out to reproduce the existing coefficients (p. 22). S074 Theorem 2 proves this.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D5] Build synthetic data on a bounded, edge-free nonlinear manifold (sums of distorted sines with random phases), so that the ambient dimension d can be varied without edge effects (2.5)–(2.8) (pp. 7–8).
- [D5] Tie the test function's width to the data (min f(x_i) = 0.01) so that the test stays meaningful as n and d change (2.4) (p. 7). Switch test functions when the data can no longer resolve the old one (the Gaussian at d = 40, p. 11).
- [D5] If an ordering-dependent method slows down when the points track gradually through the region, randomly reorder the data first (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "It is remarkable that most of the iteration counts become smaller when d is increased from 20 to 40, which may happen because tiny distances between data points are less likely to occur in higher dimensions." (p. 18)
- "The lack of other methods for interpolation to values of functions of large numbers of variables should make the new software useful to many applications." (p. 22)

### S074 · A Krylov subspace algorithm for multiquadric interpolation in many dimensions (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2005, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drh021)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. C. Faul, G. Goodsell · **Position in trajectory**: DAMTP 2002/NA05 (April 2002, revised January 2004) (p. 1). It is the full specification and analysis of the solver used in S057, written in the same years as NEWUOA. **Read**: §§1–3 (pp. 2–9); the statement of Theorem 1 and the start of its proof (pp. 13–14); the opening of §5, the linear-rate argument, and Theorem 2 with its proof (pp. 16–18, 21–23); §6 (pp. 23–29). **Not read**: the details of §4 (pp. 9–12), the rest of Theorem 1's proof (pp. 14–16), and pp. 17 (second half) and 19–20.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The Cambridge iterative procedures (since 1993) need about ten iterations for d = 2 and c = 0, but they lose efficiency as d and c grow. Convergence can be guaranteed by a Krylov technique (p. 1). Fast multipole partitions become hard for d > 3 (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Steps 0–9 (pp. 3–6):
  - a random ordering of the points (Step 2);
  - each L_ℓ is the q nearest remaining points, with an exchange rule (2.7) when two of them are unusually close (Step 3);
  - the approximate Lagrange functions come from a Cholesky factorisation of −Ψ (Step 4; §3, pp. 8–9);
  - each iteration forms a conjugate-gradient-like direction, does one O(n²) product, and chooses the step and the constant term to minimise the largest residual (Steps 5–8).

  The algorithm is read as preconditioned conjugate gradients, with a preconditioner Ξ that approximates an exact operator Ξfull (p. 3, p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Distinct data points and c ≥ 0. Micchelli's result that w^TΦw < 0 for nonzero vectors w with zero sum (p. 2, p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique** (brief):
  - *Theorem 1*: termination within κ iterations, conjugacy and a positive step length (p. 13).
  - *Linear rate*: ‖s* − s^(k+1)‖ ≤ ρ^k ‖s*‖, where ρ is the spectral radius of I − H for a normalised Gram matrix H (5.9)–(5.12) (p. 18).
  - *Expected cost*: the cost of building the L_ℓ under the random ordering (3.3)–(3.4) (pp. 7–8).
  - *Theorem 2*: the algorithm's coefficients already minimise the largest normalised scalar product V, so the improvement suggested in Powell (2002) brings nothing. The proof uses Cauchy–Schwarz and an orthogonal decomposition, (5.29)–(5.39) (pp. 21–23).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Problems A–D* (random disc, points pushed far out, points on 2 or on 10 line segments): n = 200–10000, q = 10–50, ε = 10^{-10}, ten random instances per setting, reported as min–max ranges (Table 1, p. 24).
  - *Higher dimensions*: d = 5–40 (Table 2, p. 26), and averages for c > 0 (Table 3, p. 27).
  - *Timings*: against a direct method on a Sun Ultra 10 (Table 4, p. 28).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues Beatson–Powell (1994), Powell (1997), Faul–Powell (2000), Faul (2001) and Powell (2002) (pp. 2–3). Compared with Powell (1997), it needs about 25% more iterations, except at q = 10, where it needs fewer (p. 25).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The specification comes first and the reasons later (p. 3). An unexplained finding is reported as unexplained, after a test designed to explain it had failed (p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The Fortran 77 code is free from the third author and has been run on up to 10000 points (p. 29). Its limits:
  - for d = 40 and c > 0 the iteration count grows almost in proportion to n (p. 27);
  - the best q depends on the application (p. 28);
  - one run hit a nonpositive Cholesky pivot and was replaced by another random instance, and the paper says so (pp. 24–25).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (outside DFO). It removes one limitation (poor efficiency at large d and c > 0, and no guarantee) while keeping the Lagrange-approximation machinery of the earlier versions (pp. 1–3).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence with a ⚠ variant. The variant is ten random replications reported as min–max ranges (p. 24). The unexplained d = 40 result is probed with a designed experiment and still reported as unexplained (p. 26).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. Expected operation counts (3.3)–(3.4) (pp. 7–8); O(q³n) preliminary work (p. 9); timings (Table 4, p. 28); storage limits (p. 29).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. The code is available free of charge from the third author (p. 29).
- New-pattern candidate: *native-norm descent proof* ✅ (pp. 13–18).
- New-pattern candidate: *close a design avenue with a theorem* (Theorem 2, p. 21).
- New-pattern candidate: *explain a surprise with a designed follow-up experiment* ✅ (existing candidate). Small point distances are suppressed by (6.2) to test an explanation of the good d = 40 counts (p. 26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Randomise the data ordering to get multigrid-like coarsening and cheap nearest-neighbour updates in expectation, and prove the expected cost (3.3)–(3.4) (pp. 7–8).
- [D4] Get a linear rate from a Krylov optimality property: substitute (I − Ξ)^k into the minimum (5.11) and identify ρ with the spectral radius of a normalised Gram matrix (pp. 18–19).
- [D4] Before publishing an improved variant, check whether your current choice already solves the variant's optimisation problem (Theorem 2, p. 21).
- [D5] Run each configuration on ten random instances and report min–max ranges. Narrow ranges show that the cost is insensitive to the randomness (p. 24).
- [D5] Time the preliminary work and a single iteration separately, next to a direct-method baseline (Table 4, p. 28).

**Quotes**:
- "We found, however, that the changes to the iteration counts due to this modification are negligible, so the relatively good performance of the algorithm for d = 40 is unexplained." (p. 26)
- "The use of such coefficients, however, is not an improvement over the present algorithm" (p. 21)

### S078 · Fast evaluation of polyharmonic splines in three dimensions (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2007, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drl027)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. K. Beatson, A. M. Tan (University of Canterbury) · **Position in trajectory**: DAMTP 2006/NA03 (April 2006) (p. 1). It is one of the last RBF papers, written after his 2001 retirement (card c06, R007 p. 24), in the years between NEWUOA (2006) and BOBYQA (2009). **Read**: the abstract and §1 (pp. 1–3); the opening of §2 (pp. 3–6); the statements of Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 (pp. 9, 12) and Lemma 5.1 (p. 14); the uniqueness remark on p. 17; §7 in full (pp. 18–26); the opening of Appendix A (pp. 27–28). **Not read**: the derivations in §§2–6 (pp. 6–18, apart from the pages listed) and the appendix formulae (pp. 28–30).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Evaluating a polyharmonic spline costs O(N) at one point and O(N²) for a matrix–vector product. This is the bottleneck of iterative interpolation methods such as that of Faul, Goodsell and Powell (p. 2). Applications include surface reconstruction from laser and lidar scans and modelling ore grade from drill-hole data (p. 2, p. 19).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Far-field (outer) expansions for (ν+1)-harmonic splines, from a generalised Legendre expansion (Theorem 4.1, p. 9), with a truncation error bound (Theorem 4.2, p. 12).
  - A uniqueness lemma for truncated outer expansions (Lemma 5.1, p. 14). It lets a parent panel's coefficients be computed cheaply from its children's (p. 17).
  - A binary-tree hierarchical evaluator. Each panel stores the distance at which each truncation order reaches the tolerance, and evaluation uses a stack instead of recursion (pp. 18–22).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Distinct centres, unisolvent for π_ν (p. 2). Real coefficients, which give a symmetry that saves work (p. 23).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique** (brief): The coefficients of Theorem 4.1 were first found by straightforward algebra for ν = 1–4. Iserles then proved the general formula with special functions, and the authors later found an induction proof, which is posted on a web page (p. 12). The uniqueness lemma certifies the translation formulae indirectly (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: Biharmonic matrix–vector products for N = 4000–128000, with centres uniform in a cube or on the sphere. Relative accuracy targets τ = 10^{-3} and 10^{-6}, 10 replications, fast versus direct times (Tables 1–2, pp. 24–25). The achieved accuracy never exceeded τ (p. 25).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Barnes–Hut, Greengard–Rokhlin, Epton–Dembart and Powell's 1993 two-dimensional evaluator for thin-plate splines (p. 3, p. 19).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The engineering choice is stated plainly. They use a hierarchical code rather than a full fast multipole code, because it is much simpler and the full code wins only for very large N (p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The mathematics of the expansions plus a hierarchical code; a similar C code was used for surface fitting with hundreds of thousands of points (Carr et al., p. 24). The limits are admitted:
  - the adaptive error estimate could be fooled by contrived data (p. 23);
  - the per-panel tolerance is a heuristic (p. 23);
  - a hierarchical code costs an extra log N compared with a full fast multipole code (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence.
  - The evaluation formula is chosen by operation count, O((ν+1)p²) against O((p+ν)³) (p. 24).
  - Exploiting symmetry halves the coefficient storage (p. 23).
  - The hierarchical and full-multipole options are compared by operation count, with and without the log N factor (p. 27).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence with a ⚠ variant. The variant is a relative-accuracy protocol with replications (p. 25). The authors also admit that their error estimator can be defeated (p. 23).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant. No free-distribution statement was seen. A C implementation served an application paper (p. 24), and a proof is distributed through a web page (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: *numerics → conjecture → proof* ✅ (existing candidate). Formula found from small cases, then proved in general (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: *prefer the simpler code when the asymptotically better one pays off only at extreme sizes* (p. 27).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Use a uniqueness lemma for truncated expansions to certify that a cheap indirect computation equals the direct one (Lemma 5.1, p. 14; p. 17).
- [D2] Precompute, for each tree node, the distance at which each truncation order meets the tolerance, so that every evaluation uses the shortest adequate series (pp. 19–20; §7.1, pp. 22–23).
- [D5] When the output magnitude grows with N, set the absolute tolerance to τ‖s‖∞ and report the achieved relative accuracy averaged over replications (p. 25).
- [D4] Guess a coefficient formula from small cases worked by hand (ν = 1–4), then prove it by induction (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "the error estimation method described above could fail for contrived input data. However, in our computational experience, it has never failed." (p. 23)
- "Firstly, a hierarchical code is an order of magnitude simpler to write." (p. 27)
