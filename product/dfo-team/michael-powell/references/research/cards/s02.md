Batch intent: See how Powell places his derivative-free work inside the whole of nonlinear optimization, using his own retrospective (S137, written for the 2007 Leuven symposium), and how late in his career he enters another community's method with theory alone (S167, SAO from structural optimization). ### S137 · On nonlinear optimization since 1959 (The Birth of Numerical Analysis, World Scientific 2009, pp. 141–160, 10.1142/9789812836267_0009)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: written in January 2008 for the symposium on the Birth of Numerical Analysis, Leuven, October 2007 (p. 1, footnote). It falls between NEWUOA, which the paper calls five years old (p. 19), and BOBYQA (2009). It is Powell's own account of the field from 1959, the year of Davidon's report and the year he began research (p. 2). He says it leans strongly towards his own contributions (p. 19).
**Read**: the whole report, §§1–6 and the references (pp. 1–20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The essay is organized around one practical demand: algorithms that work without good initial estimates of the variables (abstract, p. 1). §1 sets up the disadvantages of the classical methods, each shown by a small case (pp. 2–4):
  - Newton's method needs a starting point close to the solution (p. 2).
  - A line search on ‖f‖² can converge to a point where J is singular and ∇F ≠ 0 (Powell 1970, p. 3).
  - Steepest descent is intolerably slow on F = m x₁² + M x₂² (p. 3).
  - Newton–Raphson applied to ∇F = 0 seeks a stationary point and pays no attention to minimization (p. 4).

  The date in the title works as a personal hook: Davidon's 1959 report is the source of the field's key ideas, and 1959 is also when Powell started (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open a survey by listing the concrete failure of each classical method on a two-line example. The reader can then measure every later advance against a named defect (pp. 2–4).

- **D2 Core idea (of the essay)**: each advance is presented as the removal of a named disadvantage of its predecessor.
  - Variable metric (DFP/BFGS) needs no good start and no second derivatives, and costs O(n²) rather than O(n³) per iteration (p. 5). Conjugate gradients also drop the n×n matrix (p. 6).
  - Barrier and penalty functions bring in constraints (pp. 7–9) but create cliffs when µ is tiny (p. 9). The augmented Lagrangian adjusts λ without driving µ → 0 (pp. 9–11).
  - SQP replaces the sequence of unconstrained subproblems. It needs only an approximation to the Hessian of the Lagrangian combination W, not every ∇²cᵢ (pp. 11–13).
  - Trust regions cure many consecutive short line-search steps and allow an indefinite Bₖ (pp. 14–16).
  - The symmetric Broyden update takes up the freedom in Bₖ₊₁ by least Frobenius change (p. 16). It carries over to derivative-free work (p. 17). What is kept from quasi-Newton: the least-change principle. What is changed: gradient conditions become interpolation conditions (6.4), gₖ becomes an estimate, m stays about 2n+1, and one point is replaced per iteration.
  - Transferable: when you port a method to a new information regime (for example, no derivatives), keep its variational principle and swap only the constraints that encode the data (gradient secant → interpolation). Powell does this for symmetric Broyden (pp. 16–17).

- **D3 Assumptions and model**: This is a survey, so the assumptions are stated method by method:
  - Quadratic termination needs exact line searches and a strictly convex quadratic F (p. 5).
  - Barrier theory needs continuity and strictly interior points near x* (p. 8).
  - The penalty error estimate needs ∇F(x*) ≠ 0 and linearly independent constraint gradients (p. 9).
  - The augmented Lagrangian needs second-order sufficiency, so that x* minimizes Λ(x, λ*, µ) for small µ (p. 10).
  - The monotonicity argument of §6 assumes F is quadratic (p. 17).

- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: The essay gives expository arguments rather than new proofs.
  - Closed-form steepest-descent iterates on m x₁² + M x₂² from (M, m)ᵀ contract by θ = (M−m)/(M+m) (p. 3).
  - Invariance of DFP under z = Sx shows that scaling does not matter and lets the analysis take ∇²F = I without loss of generality (p. 5).
  - Comparing the penalty stationarity condition with KKT gives cᵢ(x[µ]) ≈ −½µλ*ᵢ, so the error is of order µ, with the exception x₁⁴ + x₁x₂ + x₂ subject to x₂ = 0 (p. 9).
  - The dual bound (3.11) motivates maximizing over λ (p. 10).
  - The Powell (1978) damping γₖ = θγ̂ₖ + (1−θ)Bₖδₖ takes the largest θ ∈ [0,1] with δᵀγ ≥ 0.1 δᵀBδ (p. 13).
  - The central device is the least-squares projection identity (6.6), ‖∇²F − Bₖ₊₁‖²_F = ‖∇²F − Bₖ‖²_F − ‖Bₖ₊₁ − Bₖ‖²_F (pp. 17–18). It gives monotone error decrease and ‖Bₖ₊₁ − Bₖ‖_F → 0. Together with the secant condition (6.3), this yields the Broyden–Dennis–Moré condition (6.7), and hence superlinear convergence, even when Bₖ does not approach ∇²F (p. 18).
  - Transferable: if your update is a Frobenius-norm projection onto an affine set that contains the true matrix, write down the Pythagorean identity at once. It gives monotone error decrease and summable changes for free (pp. 17–18).
  - Transferable: when a secant update needs positive curvature, use the θ-damped γ with a 0.1 threshold rather than skipping the update (p. 13).

- **D5 Experiments**: There are no new experiments. Powell recalls programming Davidon's method on a Ferranti Mercury in 1962 and minimizing a periodic function of 100 variables when n = 20 counted as large (p. 4). He reports NEWUOA on the trigonometric test function (6.8) with random instances for each n: average #F of 931, 1809, 3159 and 6013 for n = 20, 40, 80 and 160 with m = 2n+1. He sets these against the more than n²/2 evaluations a carefully constructed quadratic model would need (p. 18).

- **D6 Positioning (one line)**: He credits Davidon as the source of the ideas (p. 2), Fletcher as co-author of DFP (p. 5) and Hestenes as independent co-inventor of the augmented Lagrangian (p. 9). He calls Broyden–Dennis–Moré (1973) an important breakthrough (p. 18) and admits that he does not properly understand Davidon's relativity-style argument (p. 5). For details he defers to textbooks and to Kolda–Lewis–Torczon (2003) (p. 19). The reference list cites no other group's model-based DFO work (pp. 19–20).

- **D7 Writing (one line)**: Short first-person anecdotes (1962 at p. 4, the 2n+1 preference at p. 17) sit inside first-person-plural exposition. Every method gets a formula and then a disadvantage shown by a tiny example (pp. 3, 8, 9, 13). The one explicit maxim comes last: the question most useful to him in developing unconstrained algorithms is whether the method works well when F is quadratic (pp. 16–17).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is exposition and history, with no new theorem. The limit is stated plainly: this is a personal view of an active researcher, weighted to his own work and not comprehensive (p. 19). The future direction is specific: derivative-free optimization with constraints needs new algorithms that give high accuracy efficiently (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: Powell states his preference for m ≈ 2n+1 with one interpolation point changed per iteration (p. 17). He reports NEWUOA counts roughly proportional to n, against the more than n²/2 a careful quadratic model would need (p. 18). The paper also supplies the theory behind the least-change choice: the projection identity (6.6) plus the Broyden–Dennis–Moré condition (6.7) (pp. 17–18).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: he names O(n²) rather than O(n³) work as a key advantage of variable metric methods (p. 5), and Cholesky updating preserves positive definiteness under rounding errors (p. 6). He calls the damping device necessary in order to provide software (p. 13).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (field level): the history is written as a chain in which each method removes a named disadvantage of the previous one (pp. 4, 9, 11, 14, 16).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: his first response to Davidon's report was to program it and run experiments (p. 4). The NEWUOA evidence is averages over random instances for each n (p. 18).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the trust region he describes has one radius, with thresholds 0.2 and 0.8 and factors 2 and ½, offered only as an example (p. 15). The RHO/DELTA split of his DFO codes is not mentioned, so the two-radius discipline looks specific to the DFO codes rather than to his general view of trust regions.
- New-pattern candidate: **quadratic-case test as the first design question** (pp. 3, 5, 6, 16–18).
- New-pattern candidate: **minimal analytic counterexample per failure mode** (pp. 3, 8, 9–10, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ask of every new update or method, before any general theory, what it does when F is quadratic: termination, monotone error, rate (pp. 5, 16–18).
- Expose each failure mode with the smallest closed-form example: x³ subject to x = 1 for an unbounded penalty (p. 8); x₁⁴ + x₁x₂ + x₂ subject to x₂ = 0 for a stationary point that is never a minimum (pp. 9–10); F = −½‖x‖² with linear constraints for δᵀγ̂ < 0 (p. 13).
- Report evaluations against n as averages over random instances, and set them beside the model's parameter count (n²/2) to make the case for underdetermined models (p. 18).
- Use an invariance argument (iterates transform as zₖ = Sxₖ) both to claim scale independence and to simplify the analysis to ∇²F = I (p. 5).

**Quotes**: "A device of this kind was necessary in order to provide software." (p. 13) · "Some successes of the symmetric Broyden method in minimization without derivatives are stunning." (p. 18)

### S167 · The Lagrange method and SAO with bounds on the dual variables (Optimization Methods and Software 29(2) 2014, 10.1080/10556788.2013.822497)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: DAMTP 2011/NA16, revised March 2012, presented at the 8th International Conference on Numerical Optimization and Numerical Linear Algebra, Xiamen 2011 (p. 1). The work began during a two-month visit to Stellenbosch in early 2011, where Albert Groenwold introduced Powell to SAO (pp. 4, 18). The acknowledgement says this renewed his interest in Lagrange functions (p. 18), a return to the theme of his 1969 augmented Lagrangian paper (see S137 p. 9). It is a theory-only side paper from the LINCOA period.
**Read**: the whole report, §§1–5, acknowledgements and references (pp. 1–19).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is another community's method. SAO (Groenwold, Etman et al.) solves a sequence of approximate NLPs whose functions are separable (1.6), with simple bounds (1.7). Minimizing the Lagrangian then splits into n one-variable problems (1.8), which allows n to be very large (p. 3). The augmented Lagrangian remedy of adding squares of constraints is unacceptable here because f₀ would lose separability (p. 3). When no multiplier makes x(λ*) a solution, λ can grow without bound. The bounded dual method of Wood, Groenwold and Etman therefore confines λ to the box L (1.9) (p. 3). Powell's question is what maximizing the dual over the box actually delivers, under minimal assumptions (continuous fⱼ, compact X) (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable: when importing a method from an applied community, first name the structural property it cannot give up (here separability) and use it to rule out the textbook fixes (p. 3).

- **D2 Core idea**: Theorem 4 (pp. 4, 7–8). If λ† maximizes φ over the box L and x(λ†) is unique, then x(λ†) is the unique minimizer of the ℓ1 exact penalty Ψ = f₀ + Λ Σ|fⱼ| over equalities + Λ Σ max[0, fⱼ] over inequalities, (1.11)/(3.2). So the dual bound Λ is the penalty parameter, and Ψ is the natural merit function for the SAO outer loop: set x⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = x∞⁽ᵏ⁾ if and only if Ψ*(x∞⁽ᵏ⁾) < Ψ*(x⁽ᵏ⁾) (5.7) (p. 16). The design suggestions for SAO follow (pp. 15–17):
  - Quadratic approximations match value and gradient at x⁽ᵏ⁾ (5.6). A multiple of ‖x − x⁽ᵏ⁾‖² may be added for uniqueness (pp. 15–16).
  - An ∞-norm trust region intersected with the bounds keeps the subproblem separable (5.8). After a rejected step, ρ⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = ½‖x∞⁽ᵏ⁾ − x⁽ᵏ⁾‖ (p. 16).
  - Majorization, also called conservatism, gives monotone decrease of Ψ* but tiny steps under high curvature (p. 17). Powell prefers to choose curvatures so that fⱼ(x∞) ≈ f*ⱼ(x∞), and to judge success by actual against predicted reduction (p. 17).

  What is kept: SAO's separable subproblems and the bounded dual. What is changed: the acceptance rule and a trust region around it. Minimizing Ψ directly is rejected because it destroys separability (p. 16).

- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - The fⱼ are only continuous, and X is bounded and closed; the generality of X admits integer constraints (pp. 2, 4).
  - Uniqueness of x(λ) is assumed throughout §3, and may need a modification such as shrinking X (p. 7).
  - §§2–4 assume global minimization of L(·, λ) over X. §5 relaxes this to local minimization (p. 12).
  - The SAO part assumes that the gradients of f*ⱼ are available (p. 15).
  - Majorization needs m′ = 0 (no equality constraints) and bounded second derivatives (pp. 16–17).
  - Powell reports that he first assumed differentiability and later found continuity sufficient, which made the proofs much shorter (p. 4).

- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**:
  - Theorem 1: swapping minimizers gives φ(λ) − φ(µ) ≤ Σ(λⱼ − µⱼ) fⱼ(x(µ)), so φ is Lipschitz with constant c_max in the ℓ1 norm (2.1)–(2.4) (p. 5).
  - Theorems 2–3 (concavity, and gradient fⱼ(x(λ)) when x(λ) is unique) are cited to Bazaraa, Sherali and Shetty (2006) in place of his own proofs (pp. 4–5).
  - Corollary 1: one-sided directional derivatives at the sign-constrained maximizer give feasibility and complementarity (2.6)–(2.7). The chain f₀(x∞) = L(x∞, λ∞) ≤ L(x*, λ∞) ≤ f₀(x*) (2.8), plus uniqueness, finishes the proof (pp. 5–6).
  - Theorem 4 reuses this pattern. The box maximizer's components sit at −Λ, 0 or Λ according to the sign of fⱼ (3.3), so L(x†, λ†) = Ψ(x†) (3.5) while L(x, λ†) ≤ Ψ(x) for all x (3.6)–(3.7) (pp. 7–8).
  - Localization device: replace X temporarily by X ∩ N so that a strict local minimizer becomes the unique global one, then apply Theorem 3 to get a local dual gradient (pp. 12, 17).
  - Majorization gives Ψ(x⁽ᵏ⁾) > Ψ(x∞⁽ᵏ⁾) ≥ Ψ*(x∞⁽ᵏ⁾), hence strict monotone decrease of Ψ* (p. 17).
  - Transferable: to link a box-constrained dual with an exact penalty, write the sign pattern of the maximizer's components (3.3). The two inequalities L(x†, λ†) = Ψ(x†) and L(x, λ†) ≤ Ψ(x) then give the result in three lines (pp. 7–8).

- **D5 Experiments**: There are no numerical experiments, and Powell says so (p. 17). In their place is a hand-computed 2-D example: f₀ = x² + y², a piecewise-quadratic f₁ with continuous first derivatives, and three choices of X. He gives closed-form paths of x(λ) and dual functions φ in two figures (pp. 8–12) and a walk-through of local minimization (pp. 12–15). For numerical results of four SAO implementations he points to Groenwold and Etman (2010) (p. 17).

- **D6 Positioning (one line)**: The paper builds on the Stellenbosch SAO papers (pp. 3–4, 17). It notes that Gill and Robinson (2010) found a similar penalty relation in the differentiable case (p. 4). It discloses that Burdakov pointed to Bazaraa et al. ch. 6, which contains the same proofs as Powell's (p. 4).

- **D7 Writing (one line)**: Powell writes of himself in the third person, as the author, and tells the story of the work: the differentiability assumption dropped, the prior art found (p. 4), and the example changed because its first version had an unexpected degeneracy (pp. 14–15). The claim of practical value is hedged as likely rather than certain and is backed only by argument (p. 17).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory (short continuity-only proofs, and the new Theorem 4) plus design suggestions for SAO. No particular SAO version is specified (p. 17). The limits are concrete:
  - Uniqueness of x(λ) is required (p. 7).
  - With global minimization, every x(λ) can stay far from the constraint (case X±, p. 12).
  - Local minimization can make φ discontinuous (5.2) (p. 14).
  - The Lagrange method with local minimization fails on a nonconvex QP with linear constraints: every x(λ) lies on the boundary of X and violates a constraint (p. 18).

  The future direction: SAO whose quadratic approximations give the Lagrangian positive curvature for every λ, with bounds on the dual variables (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: cost structure drives the design. Separability is what allows huge n (p. 3). The augmented Lagrangian squares and direct minimization of Ψ are both rejected because they destroy it (pp. 3, 16). The ∞-norm trust region is chosen to keep it (p. 16).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: a single-radius trust region is transplanted to the SAO outer loop. The radius is set to half the rejected step (p. 16) and success is judged by actual against predicted reduction (p. 17). There is no RHO/DELTA split.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the move is to keep the working machinery (separable subproblems, bounded dual) and change only the acceptance rule (pp. 3, 16), but it is applied to another group's method, not to his own solver lineage.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: this is a theory-first paper with no experiments, which departs from experiment-first practice. The honest-scope half is kept: he declines to specify an SAO version because he has run no experiments (p. 17), and he names a problem class where the method fails (p. 18).
- New-pattern candidate: **strip hypotheses to the minimum to shorten proofs** (differentiability → continuity, p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate: **merit function derived from the subproblem's own duality theorem** (Theorem 4 → Ψ as acceptance test, pp. 7, 16).
- New-pattern candidate: **disclose prior art and replace own proofs by citations** (p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate (shared with S137): **minimal hand-built example chosen to be typical, not degenerate** (pp. 8, 14–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Once a proof works, reread it step by step and delete every hypothesis that no step uses. Here continuity plus compactness replaced differentiability and shortened the proofs (p. 4).
- To show that a min-value function φ(λ) = min_x L(x, λ) is Lipschitz, evaluate L at the other point's minimizer. This gives two one-sided bounds and a constant equal to the bound on the constraint functions (pp. 5).
- Use box bounds on dual variables as an exact-penalty parameter. Then use the penalty Ψ as the merit function that accepts or rejects outer iterates (pp. 7–8, 16).
- To apply a theorem that needs a unique global minimizer at a strict local minimizer, intersect the feasible set with a small neighbourhood temporarily (pp. 12, 17).
- If a subproblem must stay separable, use an ∞-norm trust region so that it intersects the simple bounds componentwise (5.8) (p. 16).
- Build the illustrative example to be typical: the simplest quadratic choice gave a singular Lagrangian Hessian (p. 8). Check it for accidental degeneracy, and replace it if it has one (pp. 14–15).

**Quotes**: "By removing the unnecessary assumptions, his proofs became much shorter than before." (p. 4) · "The author is not trying to specify a particular version of the SAO method, because he has not run any numerical experiments himself that investigate possible options." (p. 17)
