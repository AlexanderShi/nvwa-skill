### S093 · On the convergence of trust region algorithms for unconstrained minimization without derivatives (Computational Optimization and Applications 2012, 10.1007/s10589-012-9483-x)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: presented at the Erice 2010 workshop on nonlinear optimization (p. 1, footnote). Written after NEWUOA, which it cites as the far more efficient practical relative (p. 2). This is Powell's own convergence theory for the COBYLA-style regime of one new F value per iteration (p. 4). The closing remarks point to linear and nonlinear constraints as the next use (pp. 30–31).
**Read**: whole report, §§1–8 and references (pp. 1–31). There is no appendix.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The published theory for derivative-free trust-region methods (Conn, Scheinberg and Vicente 1997, 2009a, 2009b) keeps two geometry conditions: all points within c1·ρ (1.5) and |det D_k| ≥ c2·ρⁿ (1.6). It enforces them with a model-improvement algorithm that may spend many F values without taking a trust-region step (pp. 3–4). Powell asks whether the same guarantees can be had when, as in COBYLA, exactly one new F is computed per iteration and exactly one interpolation point is replaced (p. 4). The problem statement assumes F is bounded below with bounded ∇²F, no derivatives are available, and the models are linear or quadratic interpolating F at n+1 points. The goal is to prove ‖∇F(x_k)‖ → 0 (abstract, p. 1; (1.8), p. 4).

- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The key idea is to maintain geometry on the fly with two cheap kinds of alternative step, each costing one F value (pp. 6–7):
  - Alpha steps maximize the simplex volume. One is taken when the smallest distance σ_t from a point to the opposite face is below α·ρ (2.5).
  - Beta steps pull in a point that is farther than β·ρ from the centre (2.6).
  - An alternative step is orthogonal to the face opposite y_t and has length ρ (p. 6).

  Point selection and acceptance rules:
  - After a trust-region step, the point dropped is the one with the largest barycentric coordinate |θ_t|. This multiplies the volume by exactly |θ_t| (2.8)–(2.9), pp. 7–8, 10.
  - A trust-region step is evaluated only if two conditions hold (2.7), p. 7: its predicted reduction exceeds γ times η_k, the largest model error seen at the current ρ, and its length is at least ρ/2.
  - ρ is reduced, by a factor of 10, only after an unsuccessful trust-region step followed by a beta attempt that changes nothing (pp. 8–9).

  What is kept from COBYLA: one new F per iteration, one point replaced, and n+1 interpolation points (pp. 2–4). What is changed to make the theory work (pp. 4–5):
  - There is a single radius ρ that is never increased.
  - There is no sufficient-decrease condition in the acceptance rule (1.2).

  The model Hessian G_k may be any bounded symmetric matrix. The one exception: the model must be kept unchanged when it has predicted every new F exactly (p. 8). In §8, G_k is updated by the symmetric Broyden (least Frobenius norm) method, at O(n²) cost using Z = Y⁻¹ (p. 28).

- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - F is bounded below, ‖∇²F‖ ≤ Φ and ‖∇²Q_k‖ ≤ Ω (p. 19). F is twice differentiable (Lemma 1, p. 12).
  - Arithmetic is exact and the number of iterations can be infinite (p. 2).
  - The initial interpolation matrix D_1 must be nonsingular (p. 5).
  - Simplifications admitted for proof convenience (pp. 5–6):
    - ρ_k = ρ, i.e. M = 1, although Powell notes that ρ_k > ρ is needed for efficiency in practice.
    - Every reduction is exactly a factor of 10, where any factor in [M1, M2] would do.
    - The exact-step threshold is κ+5, where any κ+M3 would do.
    - The constant 0.1 in (2.4) could be any number in (0,1).
  - §1 lists three differences between practical and theoretical algorithms (exact arithmetic, generality, practical techniques that have no proofs). It concedes that NEWUOA is much more efficient in practice than the methods analysed (p. 2).

- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The proof runs through five results (pp. 4, 12–27):
  1. Lemma 1: the model gradient error is at most c3·ρ under (1.5)–(1.6).
  2. Lemma 2: condition (1.5) always holds.
  3. Lemma 3: condition (1.6) always holds.
  4. Weak convergence, plus Lemma 4: only finitely many iterations use each ρ.
  5. Theorem 1: lim ‖∇F(x_k)‖ = 0.

  The devices used are:
  - (i) **Determinant as volume via Gram–Schmidt.** |det D_k| = ∏‖š_i‖, which gives the sandwich ∏σ_i ≤ |det D_k| ≤ ∏‖y_i − y_0‖ (3.3)–(3.7), pp. 9–10.
  - (ii) **Column-replacement determinant identity.** Replacing a column by D_kθ gives |det D_{k+1}| = |θ_t|·|det D_k| (3.9), p. 10.
  - (iii) **Lagrange functions as determinant ratios** (3.13). This gives Σ|Λ_j| ≤ c4 = 1 + 2n·c1^{n−1}/c2 (pp. 11–12). Taylor-expanding F − Q inside the identity Σ(F − Q)(y_j)Λ_j ≡ 0, with a test vector along ∇F − ∇Q, then gives ‖∇Q_k − ∇F‖ ≤ c3·ρ (3.17)–(3.21), pp. 12–13.
  - (iv) **Counting with an index set.** The index set B shrinks monotonically and is reset on success. Every 3n+3 consecutive iterations without a ρ reduction therefore contain a successful trust-region step (pp. 13–14).
  - (v) **Potential Γ_k = Σ‖y_i − y_0‖.** It grows by at most n·ρ per iteration (4.5). Whenever Γ_k > c5·ρ, a beta step cuts it by more than (2τβ+1)·n·ρ (4.6)–(4.7), pp. 15–16.
  - (vi) **Determinant recovery.** If |det D_k| < c7·ρⁿ, an alpha step raises it by more than the worst-case loss over the next τ̂α iterations (5.8)–(5.9). Induction over successive ρ values then gives the explicit constant c2 (5.13), pp. 17–19.
  - (vii) **Explicit success threshold.** c8 = (19/9·c3 + 5/4·Ω + 5/9·Φ)·max[γ, 1] makes every trust-region attempt successful whenever ‖∇F(x_k)‖ > c8·ρ (6.1)–(6.9), pp. 20–21.
  - (viii) **Counterexample for the exact-step rule.** F = ξ² + 3η² + 0·ζ² on R³ shows that, without exact trust-region steps, the iterations with one ρ can be infinite (6.10), p. 22. It is followed by a case analysis on the eigenvalues of ∇²Q_κ (Lemma 4, pp. 22–24).
  - (ix) **Upgrade from lim inf to lim.** Pairs of indices (ℓ, m) are formed with ‖∇F‖ > 10ε at ℓ and < ε at m+1. The summed decreases (7.10) would then contradict F being bounded below (pp. 25–27).

  No rate or complexity bound is given; the result is purely asymptotic (Theorem 1, p. 25).

- **D5 Numerical experiments**: §8 runs the same algorithm with a switch set to off (linear models) or on (symmetric-Broyden quadratic models) (p. 28).
  - Parameters α = 0.1, β = 5, γ = 0.01, τα = 1, τβ = 5 were fixed before any calculation, and §§1–7 were written before the experiments (p. 27).
  - Test functions are the author's trigonometric sum of squares (8.6) and the chained Rosenbrock function (8.7) (p. 28).
  - n ∈ {20, 40, 80, 160, 320}, with five random instances per n, and ρ going from 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁶ (pp. 28–29).
  - Tables 1–4 give min–max ranges over the five instances of #F and of ‖x_f − x*‖∞ (pp. 29–30).
  - Quadratic models usually reduce both by more than a factor of five (p. 29).
  - No other solver is compared and no failures are reported (pp. 28–30). Powell suspects the test functions may be too easy (p. 30).

- **D6 Positioning**: He credits Conn, Scheinberg and Vicente with most of the published theory of derivative-free trust-region methods (p. 3). He reuses their conditions (1.5)–(1.6) and notes that COBYLA also employed them (p. 3). He calls the contrast "a major strategic difference" (p. 4) and describes their approach in neutral terms (reader's reading; the text has no disclaimer): his approach may be much more efficient when a small change to the model suffices for the next trust-region step to succeed (p. 4). There are only five references: three by Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente and two of his own (p. 31).

- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The main theorem is stated in the abstract (p. 1), and a section-by-section roadmap closes §1 (p. 4).
  - He suggests the reader first take every G_k to be zero (p. 3). He marks the proofs of Lemmas 2 and 3, and the structure of §6, as skippable without loss of coherence (pp. 13, 16, 20).
  - The purpose of each parameter is stated where it is introduced: α (p. 6), β and γ (p. 7). Parsimony in parameters is made an explicit value (pp. 5–6).
  - The algorithm is given in prose paragraphs, not pseudocode (pp. 5–9).
  - Hedging is almost absent in the theory. §8 is candid about surprise (p. 30).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Theoretical contribution: global convergence, lim ‖∇F(x_k)‖ = 0, for a parameterized family of derivative-free trust-region methods that use one evaluation per iteration and no sufficient-decrease test (pp. 4, 25).
  - Empirical contribution: inaccurate quadratic models beat linear ones by more than a factor of 5 with the same geometry machinery (p. 29).
  - Stated limits: exact arithmetic (p. 2). ρ is never increased, which causes long sequences of unnecessarily small changes to the variables (p. 30). The test set may be too easy (p. 30).
  - Future work: adopt NEWUOA's radius adjustment, so that ρ becomes a lower bound on the trust-region radius (p. 30). Extend to linear constraints, which he calls straightforward, and hopefully to nonlinear constraints (pp. 30–31).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 27–30): with the same n+1 points, alternative steps and trust-region steps, switching G_k to the least-Frobenius symmetric Broyden update cuts #F by more than a factor of 5 up to n = 320 (Tables 1–2, p. 29). This is direct evidence that underdetermined models with curvature memory are the right design bet.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (pp. 5, 8–9, 30): for the proof, the RHO/DELTA pair collapses to a single ρ that is never increased. The rule of not lowering the resolution until the model has been checked survives as the termination test for the current ρ: an unsuccessful trust-region step plus a beta attempt that changes nothing (pp. 8–9). That test is also evidence for Heuristic 4. Powell himself flags the single radius as inefficient and plans to restore the two-radius scheme (p. 30).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (p. 11): keeping Z = Y⁻¹ updated in O(n²) gives the alternative step, all σ_i and all θ_i by closed formulas.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 27, 30): parameters were fixed and the theory written before any experiment. His surprise at the results, and the possible weakness of the test set, are both stated.
- Research Taste mark 5 ⚠ variant (p. 2): here the theory is written for a simplified family that is explicitly less efficient than the shipped code (NEWUOA), not to enable a better code. Theory and code are deliberately decoupled.
- new-pattern candidate: **one-evaluation geometry maintenance** (pp. 4, 6–8): keep the geometry conditions with single-point alternative steps interleaved with trust-region steps, instead of a separate model-improvement phase.
- new-pattern candidate: **prove for a parameterized family, run one untuned member** (pp. 5–6, 27): every constant can be any value in an interval, and the experiments use one member fixed in advance.
- new-pattern candidate: **tiny explicit counterexample to justify an odd rule** (p. 22): example (6.10) shows why exact trust-region steps are needed when η_k = 0.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) **Bounding the gradient error of an interpolation model.** Write the Lagrange functions as determinant ratios, and sandwich the determinant between ∏σ_i and ∏‖y_i − y_0‖. This turns an abstract well-poisedness assumption into two geometric quantities that the algorithm can control (pp. 9–13).
- (D4) **Keeping a quantity bounded when some steps increase it.** Define a potential Γ_k with a per-step increase bound (4.5). Then show that a periodically forced repair step removes more than the accumulated increase whenever Γ_k is large (4.6)–(4.8), pp. 15–16.
- (D4) **Counting repairs.** Use an index set that shrinks monotonically and resets on success. This turns the claim that repairs cannot go on for ever into an explicit count, here 3n+3 (pp. 13–14).
- (D2) **Choosing the point to drop.** Drop the interpolation point with the largest barycentric coordinate |θ_t| of the new point. The volume ratio is then exactly |θ_t|, which yields a proof-ready lower bound (pp. 7–8, 10, 17).
- (D2) **Gating evaluations by observed model error.** Evaluate a trust-region step only if its predicted reduction exceeds γ times the largest model error observed at the current radius. This saves evaluations, and it gives a lower bound on the actual decrease without a sufficient-decrease test ((2.7), p. 7; Lemma 4, p. 23).
- (D5) **Fixing parameters in advance.** Fix all algorithm parameters, and finish the theory sections, before running any experiment, and say so in the paper (p. 27).
- (D7) **Skippable proofs.** Tell the reader which proofs can be skipped, and arrange the lemmas so that skipping them is genuinely safe (pp. 13, 16, 20).

**Quotes**:
- "These values were chosen before beginning the calculations reported below, so the parameters have not been tuned." (p. 27)
- "The author had not expected the linear and quadratic models to perform so well when there are hundreds of variables. Perhaps the test functions (8.6) and (8.7) are too easy." (p. 30)

### S108 · Beyond symmetric Broyden for updating quadratic models in minimization without derivatives (Mathematical Programming 2013, 10.1007/s10107-011-0510-y)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: much of the work was done January–March 2010 at City University of Hong Kong (p. 18), right after BOBYQA (2009, cited p. 4). It is implemented as an extension of BOBYQA (p. 4). This is Powell's own test of an alternative to the least-Frobenius-norm update behind NEWUOA and BOBYQA (pp. 1–2). S067 later cites it as Powell (2013) for model accuracy (S067 p. 3).
**Read**: whole report including Appendix A (pp. 1–25).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: He starts from a method that usually works well in practice, the symmetric Broyden update (abstract, p. 1), and from a theoretical gap.
  - For quadratic F, the least-squares projection identity (1.9) gives only ‖∇²Q_{k+1} − ∇²Q_k‖_F → 0.
  - Adding a gradient term with weight θ > 0 to the semi-norm would also give ∇Q_{k+1}(v) − ∇Q_k(v) → 0 (1.10). Powell calls this potentially very helpful to convergence proofs (p. 3).
  - This could give theory without any extra F values, unlike the model-improvement steps required by Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente (p. 3).

  A practical defect is shown in a two-variable, four-point example. With θ = 0, the Lagrange function's gradient at the origin has magnitude M, even though the bounded alternative 4x(1−x) also satisfies the constraints (2.4), (2.11)–(2.12), pp. 5–6.

- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - **New norm.** Replace the Frobenius semi-norm by ‖Q‖_θ = {‖∇²Q‖_F² + 2θ‖∇Q(v)‖²}^{1/2} (1.4). The new model is the closest point, in this norm, in the affine set of quadratics that satisfy the interpolation conditions (1.7). θ = 0 recovers symmetric Broyden (pp. 2–3).
  - **Why θ cannot be constant.** A scaling argument shows θ must scale like σ⁻² when the variables are scaled by σ (3.1), pp. 7–8.
  - **Choice of v.** v = x_{k+1}. A fixed v would make ‖∇Λ_t(x_{k+1})‖ of order Δ⁻² instead of Δ⁻¹ (3.2), p. 8.
  - **Choice of θ.** θ = η_k/(2δ_k) balances the estimated magnitudes of the two terms. The estimates follow damped recurrences (3.6)–(3.7). These use the θ = 0 Hessian norm, so a large θ cannot perpetuate itself. The stated principle is that θ should err on the small side in cases of doubt, because unmodified BOBYQA performs well (p. 9).
  - **Kept unchanged.** All of BOBYQA's point management, trust-region radii and stable inverse updates (p. 4).
  - **New implementation.** A truncated conjugate-gradient solve of the KKT system in O(n²) operations, followed by a symmetric-Broyden correction that restores the interpolation conditions exactly (Appendix A, pp. 19–25).

- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - No convergence theorem is proved. The motivating identity (1.9)–(1.10) needs F to be quadratic (p. 3).
  - The experiments run BOBYQA with bounds at ±10⁶⁰, which makes the bounds irrelevant (p. 10). They use m ∈ {n+6, 2n+1} and ρ_beg = 0.5 or 0.1 with ρ_end = 10⁻⁶ (p. 10).
  - §5 uses homogeneous quadratics (1.11). Rescaling x by λ and F by λ² then gives an equivalent problem and avoids underflow (pp. 4, 15).
  - The O(n²) claim for Appendix A assumes m = O(n) (p. 24).

- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - (i) **Pythagoras in an inner-product space of quadratics.** Projection gives ‖Q_{k+1} − F‖_θ² = ‖Q_k − F‖_θ² − ‖Q_{k+1} − Q_k‖_θ² (1.9). Errors therefore decrease monotonically and model changes tend to zero (p. 3).
  - (ii) **Closed-form example.** A three-parameter example is solved via first-order conditions (2.7)–(2.9), followed by an order-of-magnitude comparison in M (pp. 5–7).
  - (iii) **Scale-invariance argument** (3.1), used to decide how a parameter must depend on the current point spacing (p. 7).
  - (iv) **θ-independent KKT matrix.** In the system (A.9) the θ·g term is moved to the right-hand side. The matrix then does not depend on θ, and the stored inverse can be reused (p. 20).
  - (v) **Convexity and a CG error bound.** The identity ‖G‖_F² = 2μᵀAμ (A.24) shows A is positive semi-definite (pp. 23–24). Hence Υ_v has no positive eigenvalues and I − θΥ_v ⪰ I. The subproblem becomes a strictly convex quadratic Φ(g) (A.13), and the CG error is bounded by the gradient norm, ‖g^(ℓ) − g^(opt)‖ ≤ ‖∇Φ(g^(ℓ))‖ (A.15), pp. 20–21.
  - (vi) **Base-point shift formula** (A.18)–(A.19). It gives products with Υ_v in O(n²), without forming the inverse matrix for the new base point, which would cost O(n³) (pp. 21–23).

- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **Versions.** Four versions: m ∈ {n+6, 2n+1} crossed with θ = 0 or θ > 0. The θ = 0 runs use the new Appendix-A code rather than stock BOBYQA, so every number is new and the baseline shares the implementation (p. 10).
  - **Test set** (pp. 10–12): 75 problems in all.
    - The trigonometric sum of squares (4.1).
    - ARWHEAD (4.2), with five random permutations of the variables.
    - Chained Rosenbrock (4.3), with random log-uniform starting points.
    - n ∈ {20, 40, 80, 160, 320}, with five problems per n.
  - **Reporting.** Tables give min–max #F (pp. 11–13).
  - **Headline result.** θ > 0 is hardly ever advantageous on the sum of squares, and Powell calls this disappointing (p. 11).
  - **Outliers.** The raw five values behind suspicious ranges are listed (822, 845, 852, 870, 1401 and others) and read as a sign of instability (p. 12).
  - **Confirming the surprise.** The clearest positive cell (Rosenbrock, n = 160, m = n+6) is re-run with ten extra random starts. The ranges [9350, 11412] versus [7675, 8986] confirm it, and Powell says he is puzzled by the finding (p. 12).
  - **Asymptotic-rate experiments (§5).** These use homogeneous quadratics with eigenvalues from 1 to 100 (5.1). ρ_end = 10⁻⁵⁰⁰⁰ is reached with the rescaling identity (5.2) (pp. 14–15).
    - Table 4: at n = 320 the final Hessian error ‖E_fin‖_F is still about 96.8% of the initial ‖E_1‖_F (p. 14).
    - Table 5: θ > 0 reduces the number of iterations in most m = n+6 runs and in all n = 160 runs (p. 15).
    - One failure is marked with asterisks, excluded from the table, and its accuracy stated (pp. 15–16).
    - Table 7 was added at a referee's request to show that convergence speeds up as iterations proceed (p. 17).

- **D6 Positioning**: The paper extends his own derivative-free symmetric Broyden work: Powell 2004a,b, NEWUOA 2006 and BOBYQA 2009 (pp. 4, 18). It frames Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente (2009) neutrally, as theory that needs extra F values (p. 3). It invokes Broyden–Dennis–Moré (1973), which he calls a major breakthrough, as the analogy for fast convergence without accurate Hessian approximations (p. 13). There are nine references (p. 18).

- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract states the negative result outright (p. 1).
  - An algebraic example comes before any algorithmic detail (§2, pp. 5–7).
  - Self-deprecating verdicts about his own code appear (p. 14).
  - He marks which results are not relevant to practical computation (p. 13). He warns the reader to reread the negative §4 conclusion before trusting the favourable Table 6 (p. 17).
  - The conclusion is kept open: it is too early to say that going beyond symmetric Broyden will not be useful (p. 13).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions:
    - An algorithmic generalization with an O(n²) implementation (Appendix A).
    - A documented negative practical result (Tables 1–3, pp. 11–13).
    - Numerical evidence on asymptotic rates and on how Hessian errors behave (Tables 4–7, pp. 14–17).
  - Limits stated:
    - θ > 0 seldom helps in practice.
    - The θ > 0 update needs much more work than BOBYQA's own (p. 13).
    - No theory is proved.
    - The §5 setting is artificial (p. 13).
  - Future directions: automatic choice and adjustment of m, because Tables 1–2 show that efficiency can depend strongly on m (p. 13). Use θ > 0 in convergence theory (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 11–14): Powell tested the obvious alternative norm himself and kept θ = 0 for practice (p. 9, p. 13). Table 4 shows the Hessian error improving by less than 0.00025% per update at n = 320, while x_fin is accurate to about 1.5×10⁻⁵ (pp. 13–14). This supports the claim that least-change models work through curvature memory, not accurate curvature. It also resolves the ⚠️ unverified remark in Method 3's Limitations that later work explores other norms: the alternative norm here is Powell's own, and in practice it was disappointing.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (pp. 10, 17): ρ_end is described as a crude control of accuracy (p. 10). BOBYQA usually refuses to reduce ρ until at least three new F values have been computed at the current ρ, and Powell notes that this rules out superlinear convergence (p. 17). The resolution discipline deliberately gives up asymptotic speed for stability.
- Heuristic 2 ✅ evidence (p. 12): on ARWHEAD, m = n+6 often needs far fewer F values than m = 2n+1.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 1, 15–16, 19–25): the main difficulty was an accurate O(n²) implementation (p. 1). The paper adds a rescaling device against underflow (p. 15) and diagnoses accuracy loss when ratios ‖y_j‖/Δ_k exceed 10⁷ (p. 16). The stable factored updates are kept (p. 22).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 11–17): full min–max ranges, raw outlier values, extra runs to confirm a surprise, and a failure reported and excluded explicitly.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (p. 4): exactly one change (θ) inside otherwise unchanged BOBYQA machinery, with θ = 0 recovering the predecessor.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (p. 10): BOBYQA had been sent by e-mail to about 200 people, and the extension keeps the same user data.
- new-pattern candidate: **embed the incumbent as the θ = 0 member of a one-parameter family, and rerun the incumbent on the same new code** (pp. 2, 10).
- new-pattern candidate: **extreme-precision asymptotic experiment** (pp. 4, 15): use homogeneity to rescale on the fly, reaching 10⁻⁵⁰⁰⁰ so that rates of convergence become visible.
- new-pattern candidate: **publish the rejected alternative** (pp. 1, 13): the negative result goes in the abstract, together with the reasons the question stays open.
- new-pattern candidate: **tiny explicit counterexample to justify a design choice** (pp. 5–9): the 2-D examples (2.4) and (3.4) motivate θ > 0, and the lower bound Δ_k⁻² in (3.6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) **Scaling a free weight.** When a model-update rule has a free weight, work out how each term scales under x → σx. This tells you how the weight must depend on the current length scale, instead of tuning a constant ((3.1), pp. 7–8).
- (D2) **Guarding against self-reinforcement.** Estimate the next θ from the θ = 0 solution rather than the current θ, and damp the recurrences with max[0.7·old, new] ((3.6)–(3.7), p. 9).
- (D4) **Reusing a stored inverse.** Move a parameter-dependent term to the right-hand side of a KKT system so the stored factored inverse stays valid. Then solve the small fixed-point system g = Γe_t + θΥg by truncated CG with an a posteriori error bound ((A.12)–(A.15), pp. 20–21).
- (D4) **Approximate solve, then exact repair.** After an inexact solve, add the minimum-norm correction (here symmetric Broyden) that restores the hard interpolation constraints exactly ((A.32)–(A.36), p. 25).
- (D5) **Rate experiments beyond machine range.** On homogeneous test functions, store (σ, 10^σ·x) and rescale whenever the radius drops. This allows accuracies far beyond the floating-point range ((5.2), p. 15).
- (D5) **Confirming a surprise.** When one cell of a table looks surprisingly good, run ten extra random instances for that cell only, and report both ranges (p. 12).
- (D5) **Showing raw values.** Show the raw values behind a suspicious min–max range, so readers can tell a single outlier from a genuine spread (p. 12).
- (D7) **Placing the negative result.** Put the negative result in the abstract, and end with what could change the verdict, here automatic choice of m (pp. 1, 13).

**Quotes**:
- "A simple example suggests that the extension does bring some advantages, but numerical experiments on three test problems with up to 320 variables are disappointing." (p. 1)
- "Therefore, although BOBYQA achieves good accuracy in xfin, it may be the world’s worst procedure for estimating second derivatives of objective functions." (p. 14)

### S067 · On fast trust region methods for quadratic models with linear constraints (Mathematical Programming Computation 2015, 10.1007/s12532-015-0084-4)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: DAMTP 2014/NA02, August 2014 (p. 1). This is a design record for the trust-region step of LINCOA, written after the decision taken in 2013 that fixed LINCOA's step (p. 25). At the time of writing, LINCOA's own description had not been written (p. 30). It builds on S108, cited as Powell (2013) (p. 3).
**Read**: whole report (pp. 1–31). Figure 1 (p. 11) is partly garbled in extraction, but its labels and the accompanying worked example are readable. Figure 2 (p. 23) extracts as an unreadable cloud of glyphs, so only its caption and the surrounding prose were read.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The requirement comes from his own software. LINCOA must build a feasible x_k⁺ that makes Q_k small, subject to linear constraints a_jᵀx ≤ b_j and the trust region ‖x − x_k‖ ≤ Δ_k (pp. 1–2).
  - The cost target is O(n²) operations on most iterations, for hundreds of variables without exploiting sparsity. An average of O(n³) per iteration would not be practicable (p. 2).
  - The modest accuracy target is justified by model quality. Symmetric-Broyden Hessians stay far from ∇²F: ‖H_K − ∇²F‖ > 0.9‖H_1 − ∇²F‖ is not unusual for large n (1.4). So an accurate subproblem solution is not worth O(n³) (p. 3).
  - H_k is available only through O(n²) matrix–vector products (p. 3).

- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The step uses an active-set method with truncated conjugate gradients in the original variables, with gradients projected by Q̌Q̌ᵀ (5.7) (pp. 16–18). Three termination tests are used, with η1 = 0.01 (p. 5):
  - (2.4): the search direction is not a descent direction.
  - (2.5): the linear model predicts only a small reduction on the way to the boundary.
  - (2.6): the last step gave only a small relative decrease.

  The design choices are:
  - (a) **Near-active set.** Constraints within η2·Δ_k (η2 = 0.2) form the set J(x). The first direction minimizes ‖g + d‖ subject to a_jᵀd ≤ 0 on J, computed by Goldfarb–Idnani with warm starts (pp. 9–10). Any step later blocked by a constraint is then longer than η2·Δ_k. This device is taken from TOLMIN (p. 9).
  - (b) **Constraints on increments.** Active constraints are imposed as a_jᵀ(p_{ℓ+1} − p_ℓ) = 0 rather than a_jᵀx = b_j (3.2). This avoids expensive active-set changes after tiny steps (p. 9).
  - (c) **When to change the active set.** Only if the current point is at least η2·Δ_k from the trust-region boundary; otherwise the step calculation terminates (p. 12).
  - (d) **d_perp extension.** It moves the iterate onto the boundaries of active constraints, so they are not dropped and re-added unnecessarily (5.14)–(5.18), pp. 19–21.

  Two techniques were tried and rejected:
  - The Krylov-subspace method with active sets, using Arnoldi vectors, a tridiagonal reduced Hessian and Moré–Sorensen (§4, pp. 12–16). An early LINCOA used it (p. 14).
  - Searches round the trust-region boundary via a degree-2 trigonometric polynomial (§6, pp. 21–25).

- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - F can be computed at any x, including infeasible points. Some iterations deliberately evaluate infeasible points, for example when an equality constraint is written as two inequalities (p. 2).
  - The starting point x_1 must be feasible (p. 2).
  - Q_k is quadratic with a possibly indefinite H_k (p. 4). The gradient g_k is assumed nonzero; otherwise LINCOA changes an interpolation condition instead (p. 4).
  - How Q_k is updated, and how model iterations choose points, are explicitly out of scope (p. 30).

- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - (i) **Finite CG per active set.** While test (2.6) fails, the reduction Q_k(x_k) − Q_k(p_{ℓ+1}) grows by a factor greater than 1/(1−η1) at each step (5.9). This cannot go on for ever because Q_k is bounded on the trust region (p. 18).
  - (ii) **Finitely many active-set changes.** Each change multiplies Q_k(x_k) − Q_k(p_1) by at least (1 + η1η2/4). The case ℓ* ≥ 2 is handled by (5.10). The case ℓ* = 1 uses the guaranteed step length > η2·Δ_k and a one-dimensional quadratic argument (5.11)–(5.13), pp. 18–19.
  - (iii) **Sign lemma from QP multipliers.** d_perpᵀ∇Q_k = Σλ_j·r_j ≤ 0 (5.19)–(5.20), so the extended step is downhill (pp. 20–21).
  - (iv) **Hand-built counterexamples with exact numbers:**
    - A 2-D linear model in which the active-set update must both add and delete an index ((3.8)–(3.9), Figure 1, p. 11).
    - A 2-D quadratic (4.14) whose Krylov point (0.6, 0.8) is infeasible, and no feasible point on the line through p² and p³ improves on p² (pp. 14–15).
    - A variant (4.17) in which shortening a Krylov step without changing its sign gives Q = −31.688, against −53.7298 for conjugate gradients (p. 15).
    - A 3-D example (4.19)–(4.20) in which the Krylov method stops at −19.4 although −25 is attainable (pp. 15–16).
  - (v) **Geometric explanation of the failures.** When the active set is chosen at p_1 ≠ x_k, the trust region becomes a ball about a shifted centre x̂_k with radius Δ̂_k (7.3)–(7.4). The Krylov method is suitable only if p_1 = x̂_k (pp. 27–28).
  - (vi) **Dominance without constraints.** Without constraints, Krylov dominates CG with boundary searches: Q_k(p̂_{ℓ+1}) ≤ Q_k(p̌_{ℓ+1}) (pp. 25–26).

  No convergence theorem is given for the outer optimization algorithm.

- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **Test problem.** One problem, points in a triangle (6.8): n/2 points in a triangle, 3n/2 linear constraints and several local minima (p. 23).
  - **Settings.** n = 10·2^ℓ for ℓ = 0…5, five random starts, Δ from 0.1 to 10⁻⁶, NPT = n+6 and 2n+1 (pp. 23–24).
  - **Comparison.** Current LINCOA against the variant with boundary searches, reported as averages p/q (p. 25).
  - **Columns.** #F, #TRI (trust-region iterations), and a histogram of the number of H_k-vector products per iteration in the bins [1,3], [4,10] and [11,∞) (Tables 1–2, p. 24). The histogram is chosen because the product is the most expensive part of every step (p. 24).
  - **Accuracy check.** The largest KKT residual is about 3×10⁻⁵ (p. 25).
  - **Result.** Boundary searches raise #F by 27% at n = 320 with NPT = n+6. They raise it by about 7% and 35% at n = 80 and 160 with NPT = 2n+1. The only gain, 0.7%, is very expensive in products (p. 25).
  - **Regime weighting.** Small n is deliberately given less weight, because LINCOA targets hundreds of variables (p. 25).
  - **Local minima.** Two different local minima from different starts are reported with their F values, 0.15626737 and 0.15603890 (p. 23).

- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Classical sources are cited for each component (pp. 3, 7–10, 16):
    - active sets (Gill–Murray 1974, a technique more than 40 years old, p. 8);
    - Goldfarb–Idnani (p. 10) and Moré–Sorensen (p. 7);
    - the trust-region book of Conn, Gould and Toint (p. 3);
    - his own TOLMIN (p. 9) and NEWUOA's boundary search (p. 16);
    - Powell (2013), i.e. S108, for model accuracy (p. 3).
  - He recalls his 2003 finding that NPT = O(n) beat the full (n+1)(n+2)/2 (p. 29).
  - No competing derivative-free solver is discussed.
  - He states that the paper is not a description of LINCOA (p. 30).

- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract announces the two rejected techniques and ends with the modest verdict that LINCOA's techniques seem adequate in practice (p. 1).
  - The paper reads as a decision record:
    1. The Krylov method was attractive early in LINCOA's development (p. 4).
    2. Counterexamples exposed its defects (§4, pp. 14–16).
    3. Adopting conjugate gradients brought relief (p. 16).
    4. Tables supported the decision taken in 2013 (p. 25).
  - Worked examples are given to four decimal places (p. 15).
  - His personal stance is explicit: he is enthusiastic about models with NPT of magnitude n (p. 30), and calls the success of small NPT a welcome surprise (p. 29).
  - The reason for documenting failures is stated in the last paragraph (p. 30).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions:
    - Design knowledge for O(n²) linearly constrained trust-region steps, with finite-termination arguments (pp. 16–21).
    - A documented negative result for Krylov methods and boundary searches (pp. 12–16, 21–25, 30).
    - An explanation of why inaccurate NPT = O(n) quadratic models help (pp. 29–30). The set {Q_k < Q_k(x_k)} excludes neighbourhoods of worse interpolation points, unlike the half-space given by a linear model.
  - Limits:
    - Model updating and model-improvement iterations are out of scope (p. 30).
    - Only one test family is used (pp. 23–25).
    - LINCOA itself was undocumented at the time (p. 30).
  - Future directions:
    - A θ-regularized Krylov step, (7.1)–(7.2), to restore monotonicity (p. 26).
    - The Krylov method may still be useful in the many applications where active-set changes occur at the trust-region centre (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 1–2, 5, 9–11): the whole subproblem design is driven by an O(n²)-per-iteration budget. There is a named device against tiny steps (η2·Δ_k, from TOLMIN, p. 9). Powell also notes that cancellation in Q̌ᵀg is harmless, because a_jᵀd_1 = 0 holds for any computed product (p. 11).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 4, 9, 16): LINCOA adds active sets to NEWUOA's truncated CG and reuses TOLMIN's near-active set. NEWUOA's boundary-search feature was tried and then removed, not carried over (pp. 16, 21, 25).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 3, 29–30): a mechanism-level argument for low-accuracy NPT = O(n) models (their sub-level sets avoid bad interpolation points). Smaller NPT gave much lower #F than the full quadratic count, which Powell calls a welcome surprise (p. 29).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 23–25): the test problem has known multiple local minima, both are reported, and the target regime is stated (hundreds of variables; small n weighted less). This also supports Heuristic 5.
- Heuristic 8 ✅ evidence (p. 2): some iterations may evaluate infeasible points, including equality constraints written as two inequalities.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) **Constraints on increments.** Impose active constraints on step increments, a_jᵀ(p_{ℓ+1} − p_ℓ) = 0, not on positions. Nearly active constraints can then join the active set without first taking a tiny step onto them ((3.2), p. 9).
- (D2) **Near-active first direction.** Build the first search direction from all constraints within distance η2·Δ, by the small QP min ‖g + d‖ subject to a_jᵀd ≤ 0. Any later blocking constraint then allows a step of length ≥ η2·Δ. This guarantee is also the key to the finiteness proof (pp. 9–10, 18–19).
- (D4) **Finiteness of a nested procedure.** Show that each restart multiplies the achieved model reduction by a fixed factor greater than 1, here (1 + η1η2/4), while the total reduction is bounded ((5.10)–(5.13), pp. 18–19).
- (D4) **Diagnosing an attractive method that fails.** Find the smallest example, with exact numbers, that isolates the failure. Then find the geometric reason, here the shifted trust-region centre x̂_k, that predicts when it will fail (pp. 14–16, 27–29).
- (D5) **Histograms of expensive operations.** Report inner-solver cost as a histogram of expensive operations (products per outer iteration in [1,3], [4,10], [11,∞)) next to #F. Averages hide the tail that decides whether an extension is affordable (Tables 1–2, p. 24).
- (D5) **Reporting local minima.** Report the different local minima found from different random starts, with their F values. This shows that the differences come from the problem, not the solver (p. 23).
- (D7) **Writing a design paper as a decision record.** State what was tried, the counterexample or table that ruled it out, and when the decision was taken (pp. 4, 16, 25, 30).
