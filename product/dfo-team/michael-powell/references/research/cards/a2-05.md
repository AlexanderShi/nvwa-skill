### S095 · The minimum sum of squares change to univariate data that gives convexity (IMA J. Numer. Anal. 1991, DOI 10.1093/imanum/11.3.433)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: I. C. Demetriou
- **D1 Problem & entry**: The data are measurements of a convex function of one variable that lost convexity through measurement error. The task is the least sum-of-squares change that makes all second divided differences nonnegative (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: Treat this as a highly structured QP. An O(n) procedure first builds a starting point, and then a version of the Goldfarb–Idnani method is applied. Writing the fit as a combination of B-splines makes the matrices banded (abstract).
- **D8 Contribution**: The paper gives an algorithm with a convergence proof, obtained as a special case of Goldfarb–Idnani theory. Numerical results suggest it is usable for very large n, because the O(n) start already identifies most of the active constraints (abstract).
- **Method links**:
  - Method 4 ✅: cost is designed in through the O(n) start and the choice of a basis that makes the matrices banded (abstract).
  - New-pattern candidate: *cheap structural warm start that guesses the active set before an exact QP method* (abstract).
- **Transferable technique** [D2]: Choose the basis (here B-splines) that makes your QP matrices banded, and run a cheap O(n) heuristic to guess the active constraints before the exact active-set method starts (abstract).
- **Secondary**: The memoir describes [54, 55] as joint work with his research student Ioannis Demetriou (R007 p. 19). Its reference list gives this title as [55], IMA J. Numer. Anal. 11, 433–448 (R007 p. 31).
- **Quotes**:
  - "It is proved that the algorithm converges, the analysis being a special case of the theory of Goldfarb & Idnani." (abstract)
  - "They suggest that the algorithm can be used when n is very large, because the O(n) starting procedure identifies most of the convexity constraints that are active at the solution." (abstract)

### S113 · Univariate multiquadric interpolation: some recent results (Curves and Surfaces, Academic Press 1991, pp. 371–382, DOI 10.1016/b978-0-12-438660-0.50059-5)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none
- **D1 Problem & entry**: Multiquadric interpolation on an *infinite* regular grid in d dimensions is known to give O(h^{d+1}) accuracy. The paper asks what happens on a *finite* grid, for d = 1 (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: Numerical results come first. They suggest the order falls from O(h²) to O(h) near the grid boundaries. A theorem then guarantees O(h) for Lipschitz f. Two end conditions on f restore O(h²) uniformly, but the errors near the boundaries stay much larger, and strikingly so for large c (abstract).
- **D8 Contribution**: A partial theory, plus an explicit open direction: techniques that reduce the errors near the boundaries (abstract).
- **Method links**:
  - Existing candidate *numerics → conjecture → proof or counterexample* ✅: the numerics suggest the rate, and the theorem then bounds it (abstract).
  - New-pattern candidate: *finite-domain edge effects as the open problem that infinite-grid theory leaves behind* (with S043 and S134, below).
- **Transferable technique** [D1]: Frame the paper as one question that removes one idealisation from the known theory, here the move from an infinite grid to a finite one. Answer it with numerics first and a theorem second, and end by naming the remaining gap (abstract).
- **Secondary**: The memoir summarises [56] as a study of uniform convergence and its rate with equally spaced centres on the unit interval, with c equal to the spacing (R007 p. 22, list on p. 31). In 2003 Powell said his main theoretical interest was still the orders of convergence at edges on a regular grid. He called this frustrating because numerical experiments show the orders clearly (X002 p. 6).
- **Quotes**:
  - "But what happens when the grid is finite?" (abstract)
  - "Therefore the usefulness of multiquadric interpolation in the finite case could be improved greatly by the development of techniques that reduce errors near the boundaries of the range of the data." (abstract)

### S043 · Univariate multiquadric approximation: quasi-interpolation to scattered data (Constructive Approximation 1992, DOI 10.1007/bf01279020)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. K. Beatson
- **D1 Problem & entry**: Approximate f on [x_0, x_N] from the span of multiquadrics centred at given, possibly very irregular, points plus linear polynomials (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: Three explicit quasi-interpolants. ℒ_A and ℒ_B use function values only, and ℒ_C also uses f′ at the two ends. Their polynomial parts differ: zero for ℒ_A, a constant for ℒ_B (abstract).
- **D8 Contribution**: When c = O(h), the error is O(h²|log h|) away from the ends. Near the ends ℒ_A and ℒ_B give only O(h). Buhmann's (1988) infinite regular-grid properties are thus kept on a finite range without equal spacing (abstract).
- **Method links**:
  - New-pattern candidate *finite-domain edge effects* ✅: the end-of-range loss is traced to the polynomial terms (abstract).
  - Method 1 ⚠ (variant outside software): keep the known infinite-grid results and remove one idealisation at a time (finite range, unequal spacing) (abstract).
- **Secondary**: The memoir credits the first step from gridded to scattered centres, via univariate quasi-interpolants, to [52] (R007 p. 22). Its list gives [52] as the 1990 paper on reproduction of linear polynomials (R007 p. 31). The description fits this paper's title better, so the number may point to the wrong entry, a failure mode that card c06 records for R007.
- **Quotes**:
  - "These approximations are defined by quasi-interpolation formulas that are shown to give good accuracy even if the distribution of the centers in [x _0, x _N] is very irregular." (abstract)

### S134 · Univariate interpolation on a regular finite grid by a multiquadric plus a linear polynomial (IMA J. Numer. Anal. 1992, DOI 10.1093/imanum/12.1.107)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. K. Beatson
- **D1 Problem & entry**: Numerical calculations suggest that on a finite grid the second-order convergence falls to first order near the boundaries (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: First the conjecture is proved. Adding a linear polynomial can recover superlinear convergence, but the *usual* way of fixing that polynomial fails in general, so new procedures choose it automatically (abstract).
- **D8 Contribution**: A proof, plus new procedures that often cut the maximum error by a factor of 10³. They extend directly to several variables when the points are in general position (abstract).
- **Method links**:
  - Existing candidate *numerics → conjecture → proof or counterexample* ✅: the abstract states it outright. Numerical calculations suggest the rate, and the first quote below says the conjecture is proved (abstract).
  - Existing candidate *tiny counterexample against a popular default* ⚠: the standard polynomial choice is shown to fail in general, but the abstract does not say whether this is done by a small example.
  - New-pattern candidate *finite-domain edge effects* ✅.
- **Transferable technique** [D2]: When a standard fix (here, appending a polynomial) does not deliver the rate, keep the fix and replace the rule that pins down its free parameters. Report the gain as an error-reduction factor (abstract).
- **Secondary**: In 1999 Powell reused the idea for thin-plate/cubic splines, crediting Beatson and Powell (1992) for multiquadrics. He also cites its Theorem 4.2 for end conditions f(0)=f(1)=f′(0)=f′(1)=0 that give a higher order (S055 pp. 4, 15–16).
- **Quotes**:
  - "This conjecture is proved." (abstract)
  - "Making such additions is a standard technique, but we find that the usual way of choosing the polynomial fails to provide superlinear convergence in general." (abstract)

### S008 · A direct search optimization method that models the objective and constraint functions by linear interpolation (Advances in Optimization and Numerical Analysis, Kluwer 1994, pp. 51–67, DOI 10.1007/978-94-015-8330-5_4)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: core (only the abstract was available) · **Coauthors**: none
- **D1 Problem & entry**: Nonlinearly constrained optimization when no derivatives are available (abstract).
  - **Secondary**: Powell's own account starts with IMSL, which forced difference approximations onto his TOLMIN code, and with the popularity of simulated annealing and genetic algorithms. Then Westland Helicopters asked for help with a constrained problem in four variables, and he developed the COBYLA software in response (S029 pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea**: Linear interpolation at the vertices of a simplex models the objective and each constraint, and a trust-region bound limits each step. A new point can replace a vertex for either of two reasons: to improve the simplex shape, or because it is the best point so far under a merit function driven by the greatest constraint violation. ρ is never increased. It is reduced only when a *well-conditioned* simplex fails to improve, until it reaches the final value (abstract).
- **D8 Contribution**: Some convergence properties and several numerical results. The tested range is stated as at most 9 variables, and so is its reason: linear approximations can be highly inefficient. The abstract adds that the method is easy to use for small numbers of variables (abstract).
  - **Secondary**: Later Powell wrote that he could not give a favourable answer about COBYLA's convergence within the usual conventions of convergence theory, and argued instead from finite precision (S029 p. 8).
- **Method links**:
  - Method 1 ✅: the first member of the lineage, whose own abstract names the limitation (linear models are inefficient) that later solvers remove (abstract).
  - Method 2 ✅/⚠: the resolution parameter only decreases, and only after a well-conditioned model fails (abstract). Variant: COBYLA has *one* radius. S014 p. 34 says the algorithm of Powell (1994) has only one trust-region radius, and that the second radius came from his student Evan Jones. S047 p. 5 says COBYLA sets Δ = ρ on every iteration.
  - Method 5 ✅: the tested range and its reason are in the abstract.
  - Method 6 ✅: released as software (S029 p. 3).
- **Transferable technique** [D8]: Put the tested range *and the reason for it* in the abstract, as the second quote below does (abstract).
- **Transferable technique** [D2]: Reduce the resolution only after a *well-conditioned* model has failed. That way a bad geometry is never mistaken for convergence (abstract).
- **Secondary**: COBYLA stands for Constrained Optimization BY Linear Approximation (S047 p. 3). It computes one new function value per iteration, which S093 marks as a strategic difference from the methods of Conn et al. (S093 pp. 3–4).
- **Quotes**:
  - "The trust region radius ρ is never increased, and it is reduced when the approximations of a well-conditioned simplex fail to yield an improvement to the variables, until ρ reaches a prescribed value that controls the final accuracy." (abstract)
  - "Some convergence properties and several numerical results are given, but there are no more than 9 variables in these calculations because linear approximations can be highly inefficient." (abstract)

### S046 · The uniform convergence of thin plate spline interpolation in two dimensions (Numerische Mathematik 1994, 68:107–128, DOI 10.1007/s002110050051)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none
- **D1 Problem & entry**: Bounds on |f − s| for the thin plate spline interpolant in R², for x in the convex hull of the data or close to a data point (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: Inside any triangle of three data points, the error is at most a multiple of h, the longest side, with a multiplier independent of the points. Uniform convergence holds on any bounded set that is not contained in a line, as the fill distance h tends to 0 (abstract).
- **D8 Contribution**: A uniform convergence theorem under weak conditions on the domain (abstract).
  - **Secondary**: The memoir says earlier results needed Lipschitz boundaries, and that this one holds on any bounded domain, to within a log h factor, with the best constants for 2, 3 or 4 points (R007 p. 23, [65]).
- **Method links**: Existing candidate *native-norm descent proof* ⚠: the variational (native) semi-norm is used here for an error bound, not for descent (abstract).
- **Transferable technique** [D4]: To bound a pointwise interpolation error, integrate by parts and apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the scalar product of second derivatives that defines the variational problem (abstract).
- **Quotes**:
  - "Our method of analysis applies integration by parts and the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality to the scalar product between second derivatives that occurs in the variational calculation of thin plate spline interpolation." (abstract)

### S163 · An algorithm that straightens and smooths piecewise linear curves in two dimensions (DAMTP report 1994; Ulvik 1994 proceedings, Vanderbilt UP 1995, pp. 439–453; no DOI)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none
- **D1 Problem & entry**: Approximate a piecewise linear curve D by a curve A made of line segments and circular arcs. A must have continuous direction, radii at least Δ, and distance from D not much more than Δ (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: The freedom that the tolerance Δ allows is used to minimise the number of fluctuations of A, so kinks smaller than Δ are not inherited. The fluctuations are removed by another algorithm of his that is published elsewhere, giving an intermediate curve V. Building A from V is the new part (abstract).
- **D8 Contribution**: An algorithm, plus one numerical example over a range of Δ. The usefulness claim is hedged; see the second quote below (abstract).

- **Quotes**:

### S125 · Some convergence properties of the modified log barrier method for linear programming (SIAM J. Optim. 1995, 5(4):695–739, DOI 10.1137/0805035)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none
- **D1 Problem & entry**: The convergence properties of Polyak's modified log barrier method for LP (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: Each iteration minimises a multiple of the objective minus a weighted sum of logs of (residual + positive constant). The logs therefore stay finite at every feasible point, unlike in interior-point methods. The weights act as KKT multiplier estimates, updated from the zero gradient at the end of each minimisation (abstract).
- **D8 Contribution**: The iterates converge to a solution. Objective values and constraint violations converge at R-linear rates, even when there are many solutions. The only assumption is a bounded, nonempty feasible region (abstract).
- **Method links**:
  - Existing candidate *clarify–implement–prove someone else's idea* ✅: Polyak's algorithm, Powell's analysis (abstract).
  - Existing candidate *extend a quadratic-only theorem to the class practice already covers* ⚠: the same move of weakening the assumptions, applied to LP (abstract).
  - Possible trajectory link: the 1992 numerical study S152 included Polyak's shifted barrier (S152 summary). That this study motivated the paper is inferred, not stated.
- **Transferable technique** [D7]: State how many assumptions you use and compare that with the literature, as the second quote below does (abstract).
- **Secondary**: [66] is among his log-barrier contributions (R007 p. 17).
- **Quotes**:
  - "These techniques provide a simple and interesting algorithm for linear programming that was proposed by Polyak." (abstract)
  - "Indeed, our only assumption is that the feasible region of the linear programming problem is bounded and nonempty, which is far less restrictive than the assumptions that are usually made in theoretical investigations of the modified log barrier method." (abstract)
