# Paper cards · batch c2-02 (core)

Batch intent: Look at Powell in 1986–1991, before his derivative-free period, as he took on the two big constrained-optimization topics of the day: interior-point methods (Karmarkar, S115) and trust-region SQP with an exact penalty function (with Y. What does he use to validate a method: counterexamples, convergence theory or numerical runs? ### S115 · Karmarkar's algorithm: a view from nonlinear programming (University of Canterbury. Dept. of Mathematics 1989, no DOI/arXiv; report No. 53, November 1989)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: Written in November 1989 (p. 1), from a talk at the IMA Silver Jubilee Conference (pp. 3, 41). "Most of the work was done after the conference" at the University of Canterbury, Christchurch, where Powell was an Erskine Fellow; he thanks Ian Coope (p. 41). The paper belongs to his linearly-constrained period: in it he offers his own Fortran package TOLMIN (DAMTP 1989/NA2) as his alternative to interior-point methods (pp. 40–42). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–42, including the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry is a hyped method from outside his own work. Karmarkar's algorithm "hit the front page of the New York Times in 1984". Many researchers found the claims unbelievable but could not refute them, because the 1984 paper omits crucial implementation details. The topic became "the most active field of study throughout mathematical programming" (p. 3).
  - He calls his reason for writing "unusual". He agreed to speak on the algorithm at the IMA conference to force himself to study it. After reading "about one per cent" of the literature, he related the ideas to his own nonlinear programming knowledge rather than reading further (pp. 3–4).
  - The paper sets itself two concrete questions (p. 4). (i) Can the algorithm treat general inequality constraints directly, without the standard form? (ii) What happens when there are very many constraints? He tests (ii) on a two-variable semi-infinite problem.
  - A measurable motivation for (i) (p. 23): a minimax cubic fit to 100 data values has 5 variables and 200 inequalities, but its standard form needs at least 200 variables. He argues that no data-independent preliminary step can avoid this.
  - Transferable: when you enter a hot area, ask two questions: what does this method look like in the language I already know, and what does that view let me do that the standard presentation cannot? Make the payoff a number, such as 5 variables instead of at least 200 (p. 23).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Kept from Karmarkar*:
    - the homogeneous potential V(x) = n log cᵀx − Σ log x_i (2.13), which is constant on half-lines (p. 8);
    - the projected steepest-descent direction in the transformed space, where the Hessian of the barrier term is the unit matrix at e (p. 10);
    - the exact line search on the potential, using Todd and Burrell's step length (p. 10);
    - the automatically increased lower bound γ⁽ᵏ⁾ on the optimal value (Todd–Burrell) (pp. 14–16).
  - *Change 1: remove the projective transformation analytically* (§4, pp. 17–22).
    - The direction is the solution of the QP (4.12): minimize dᵀ∇V(x⁽ᵏ⁾) + ½dᵀX⁽ᵏ⁾⁻²d subject to Aᵀd = 0 (p. 19).
    - Equivalently, it is the Newton–Raphson direction of the problem in which n log cᵀx is replaced by its first-order Taylor expansion, (4.18)–(4.19). This is the barrier-method view of Gill et al. (1986) (p. 20).
    - Theorem 6 restates one iteration in these terms. He says this description "seems to be much clearer" than the one in §2 (p. 20).
  - *Why not a full Newton step on V*: because V is homogeneous, that QP has no unique solution. Its objective is linear along x⁽ᵏ⁾ (4.22). Linearizing log cᵀx therefore gives "stabilization" that is "certainly needed" (p. 21).
  - *Change 2: treat general inequalities directly* (§5).
    - The problem is to minimize hᵀy subject to Zᵀy ≥ 0, Bᵀy = 0 and b₀ᵀy = 1 (5.16).
    - The potential is W(y) = n log hᵀy − Σ_j log Z(·,j)ᵀy (5.17), and the step comes from the QP (5.19), whose Hessian is Σ_j Z(·,j)Z(·,j)ᵀ/[Z(·,j)ᵀy]² (p. 28).
    - At most two new variables are added (p. 2).
  - *Why this route*: substituting the partitioned definitions into the closed-form direction (4.7) is possible in theory, but "the algebra is awkward". Using the QP characterization instead makes the derivation "easy and highly instructive" (pp. 25–26). The linear term turns out to be eᵀ∇W(y), "a very nice result" (p. 26). The quadratic term is the Hessian of the barrier part of W, "the good news" (p. 27).
  - Homogeneity makes the x⁽ᵏ⁾ term of (4.7) redundant. A line search over α ∈ [0, ∞) is equivalent to one over [0, 1] along d⁽ᵏ⁾ − x⁽ᵏ⁾ (pp. 21–22). He uses this freedom in §6 (p. 35).
  - Transferable: when a method is defined through a change of variables, find a QP (variational) characterization of its step in the original coordinates. Then extend it (to new constraint forms, or with eliminations) by substituting into the QP, not into the closed-form formula (pp. 19, 25–27).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The feasible set S is nonempty and bounded. A strictly positive, non-optimal feasible starting point is given. A has linearly independent columns (p. 5).
  - The "restrictive assumption" (2.14) is that the optimal value is zero. He admits it is "embarassing" to need to know the optimal value in advance (p. 8). It is later replaced by a user-supplied lower bound on the optimal value (pp. 14–16, 22, 31–32).
  - In §5 the feasible region must again be bounded and the starting point feasible (p. 27). Theorem 7 uses boundedness to prove that the change of variables is one-to-one (p. 29).
  - §6 deliberately breaks the finite-constraint setting: the constraint angles are distributed uniformly over [0, 2π] (p. 32). Theorem 4's guaranteed reduction becomes "a positive constant multiplied by zero" and is "of no relevance" (p. 34).
  - The failure proof assumes exact arithmetic (p. 37).
  - Sparsity is ignored, although he admits it is essential for problems that the LP community regards as substantial (p. 41).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *Lemma 1* (pp. 5–6) bounds the elements of the homogeneous cone. It also shows that every solution has at least one and at most n−1 zero components; the proof builds a contradiction with boundedness.
  - *Lemma 2* (p. 9): V is not bounded below, and if V → −∞ then all limit points of the normalized iterates are solutions. The proof evaluates V on the segment from x* to x⁽¹⁾ and isolates the (n−|I*|) log θ term.
  - *Theorem 4* (pp. 11–14) says the reduction in V per iteration is bounded away from zero. The proof starts from three sufficient conditions for a good line search, stated in words:
    - (a) the initial directional derivative is bounded away from zero (3.8);
    - (b) the curvature is bounded above (3.3), because the Hessian of the barrier term is the unit matrix at e;
    - (c) the bounds do not restrict the step (3.1) (pp. 11–13).
    Lemma 3 (p. 12) supplies (a) from the restrictive assumption: at least one component of P⁽ᵏ⁾ĉ⁽ᵏ⁾ is nonpositive. A Taylor series with explicit remainder at a fixed trial step completes the proof (3.9) (pp. 13–14).
  - *Corollary*: the number of iterations is at most a constant multiple of n (p. 14).
  - *Lemma 5* (p. 15): if every component of P⁽ᵏ⁾ĉ⁽ᵏ⁾ is positive, the current bound γ⁽ᵏ⁾ is strictly below the optimal value. This licenses the automatic increase of γ⁽ᵏ⁾ (p. 16). A contradiction argument (3.17) shows γ⁽ᵏ⁾ tends to the optimal value (pp. 16–17).
  - *Theorem 7, an equivalence theorem* (pp. 28–30). For the direct y-space algorithm there is a standard-form LP with n variables on which Karmarkar's algorithm generates iterates x⁽ᵏ⁾ = Zᵀy⁽ᵏ⁾, with V(x) = W(y). The proof has three steps:
    - (i) the map from {y : Bᵀy = 0} to S is an isomorphism, proved by contradiction with boundedness (p. 29);
    - (ii) choose c = Z⁺h (p. 29);
    - (iii) induction: the step QPs (5.19) and (5.25) coincide through the identities (5.26)–(5.27), and exact line searches on equal potentials then give equal iterates (p. 30).
    The polynomial-time property transfers as a corollary (pp. 30–31).
  - *Exact-arithmetic failure proof* (pp. 37–39).
    - Each iterate is written as x⁽ᵏ⁾ = (sin ψ cos ω, −cos ψ) (6.17).
    - After a cancellation, the step length is the positive root of a quadratic (6.20)–(6.22).
    - This gives an exact reduction identity, F(x⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾) = F(x⁽ᵏ⁾) − log((1+sin ω)/(1−sin ω)) (6.24).
    - The angle contracts: sin²ω⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ ≤ [(1−cos ψ)/(1+cos ψ)] sin²ω⁽ᵏ⁾, so it decays geometrically (6.26)–(6.27).
    - Hence Σ sin ω⁽ᵏ⁾ < ∞, F stays bounded below, and the iterates cannot reach (0, −1) (p. 39).
    - He omits "most of the details" because they fill "several pages of algebra" (p. 37).
  - *Algebraic self-checks*:
    - The closed-form direction (4.7) is checked against two invariants: Aᵀd = 0, and identity (4.8) (p. 18).
    - The §6 direction (6.13) is checked by its orthogonality to the barrier gradient (pp. 34–35).
    - The step length is verified through (6.19) "instead of wrestling with the quadratic equation" (p. 38).
  - *Closing the infinite case*: the sum over infinitely many constraints becomes an integral, evaluated with formula 865.44 of Dwight (1961) (6.5) (p. 33).
  - Transferable: to prove that a descent method converges to a wrong point, find an exact identity F_{k+1} = F_k − g(ω_k), prove that ω_k decays geometrically, and conclude that Σ g(ω_k) < ∞, so F stays above its optimal value (pp. 38–39).
  - Transferable: to carry all theory of method A over to a reformulation B, prove an iterate-by-iterate equivalence. On a constructed instance of A, B's iterates are a linear image of A's iterates. Show this by proving that the two step-defining QPs coincide (pp. 28–30).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - There is a single test problem, a 2-variable semi-infinite one: minimize x₂ over the unit disk, written as infinitely many tangent constraints (6.1)–(6.3) (p. 32).
  - The usual termination condition may be unattainable, so it is replaced by (6.16) (p. 35).
  - *Table 1* (p. 36):
    - From (0.1, −0.5) the iterates settle within five iterations at (0.0415579, −0.9991361), not at the solution (0, −1).
    - From (0.599, 0.8), close to the boundary, 21 iterations converge towards (0.7323741, −0.6809025), a spurious point.
  - *Figure 2* starts from (0.8, 0.5) and plots the iterates, their limit and level lines of F; it is titled "An example of premature convergence" (pp. 36–37).
  - There is no comparison with the simplex method. Powell calls his interest in numerical comparisons "relatively small" (pp. 39–40). He points to Tomlin (1987) and Shanno (1988) for numerical comparisons with the simplex method and for projections that are easy to compute, topics he chose not to discuss (p. 4).
  - Role of the experiment: falsification. The numbers led him to prove the failure analytically (p. 37).
  - Transferable: if a run shows "quite rapid convergence" to a spurious point (p. 36), do not stop at the table. Derive the iteration in closed form on that example and prove the failure in exact arithmetic (pp. 37–39).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Credits, given precisely*:
    - Gonzaga (1988) was the most helpful source (p. 4), and gave the simplification of ignoring the inhomogeneous constraint until the end (p. 41).
    - Strang (1987) supplied the "room to move" picture (p. 4).
    - Todd and Burrell (1986) supplied the line search and the lower-bound technique (pp. 10, 15).
    - He agrees with Gill et al. (1986) that the barrier connection is "of fundamental importance" (p. 4), and credits them with the viewpoint that starts §5 (p. 41).
  - *Novelty claimed narrowly*: the direct general-constraint version is "not taken from the papers that I read" (p. 4). For the rest, he believes only that the main conclusion of §6 is new (p. 41).
  - *Anticipated objection*: that the §5 method is no longer Karmarkar's algorithm. Theorem 7 answers it (p. 40).
  - *Critique of the consensus*: the results "seem to contradict the current opinion" that the algorithm suits very large LP calculations (p. 36).
  - *Generous praise*:
    - the lower-bound device is "brilliant";
    - the ideas break new ground and are of fundamental importance, whether or not the speed claims hold (p. 39).
  - *Self-critique*: he admits he has viewed the field "through a window that is shamefully narrow" (p. 41).
  - *His own alternative*: TOLMIN (1989) treats linear constraints explicitly. Its search directions may not move towards constraints with "small" residuals, where "small" depends on an automatically adjusted tolerance. It suits problems with far more constraints than variables but ignores sparsity (pp. 40–41).
  - Transferable: when you write about someone else's field, say how much you read and exactly what you took from whom. Claim novelty only for the pieces you are sure of (pp. 4, 41).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - He writes in the first person and states his motives candidly (pp. 3–4, 39–41).
  - The abstract states the negative result plainly, with "unfortunately" (p. 2).
  - The outline paragraph gives each section a wry label: §3's theorem "delights experts in complexity theory"; §4 is the "big simplification" for a nonlinear programmer (p. 4).
  - Conditions are stated in words before the proof. The three sufficient conditions for a good step (p. 11) become (3.8), (3.3) and (3.1) (p. 13).
  - Hedging is moderate: results "seem to contradict" (p. 36); "It might be possible to devise an ad hoc remedy" (p. 40).
  - The conviction is stated openly. He had avoided the topic because he believes linear constraints should be treated explicitly, and the paper has strengthened that view (p. 40).
  - Transferable: before a convergence proof, list in words the conditions that make a step succeed, then map each condition to one numbered inequality (pp. 11–13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contributions*:
    - (1) an exposition of Karmarkar's algorithm in nonlinear-programming terms (Theorem 6, p. 20);
    - (2) a direct treatment of general inequalities, with the equivalence Theorem 7 and the polynomial-time property (pp. 27–31);
    - (3) a semi-infinite counterexample on which the iterates converge to a non-solution, shown numerically and proved analytically (pp. 32–39).
  - *Limits, stated concretely*:
    - §4 "is just a reformulation" (p. 22);
    - the theory does not cover §6 (p. 34);
    - sparsity is not treated, and with good sparse techniques the elimination of §5 may be unnecessary (p. 41).
  - *Remedies, only sketched*: force the line search away from the circle (he shows that the direction already does so in part of the disk); or change the potential so that it is unbounded at the boundary, which conflicts with homogeneity when the constraints are infinite (p. 40).
  - *Future*: study the loss of efficiency as more and more linear constraints approximate the disk (p. 41).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Heuristic 6 and Taste warning sign 3 (model constraints individually rather than lumping them into one penalty), evidence. He gives this as his reason for not studying Karmarkar's algorithm earlier, and says the results strengthened the view. TOLMIN is offered as the explicit-constraint alternative (pp. 40–41). This is a 1989 statement of the principle, three years before COBYLA.
- ⚠ Method 1 (relax one limitation), variant. He applies it to someone else's algorithm: he removes the standard-form requirement, keeps the potential, the line search and the lower-bound device, and proves with Theorem 7 that no guarantee is lost (pp. 23–31).
- ⚠ Method 5 (experiment-first validation with honest scope), variant. The experiment is a falsification test on one tiny, fully specified problem, followed by a proof in exact arithmetic. He explicitly deprioritizes benchmark comparisons (pp. 36–40). The honest-scope half holds: the negative result is in the abstract (p. 2).
- ✅ Method 4 (cost as part of the design), evidence. §5 is motivated by keeping the variable count at 5 instead of at least 200 (p. 23).
- New-pattern candidate: tiny counterexample against a popular default. The two-variable problem refutes the belief that the algorithm suits problems with very many constraints (pp. 4, 32–39). Also S165 p. 3, S148 p. 5 (c01).
- New-pattern candidate: numerics → conjecture → proof or counterexample. Table 1 suggested spurious convergence, and the analytic proof followed (pp. 36–39). Also X002, S045, S055 and other cards.
- New-pattern candidate: clarify–implement–prove someone else's idea. Karmarkar's method is re-derived as a QP/Newton–barrier step and extended (pp. 17–31). Also R007.
- New-pattern candidate: self-derivation over literature study. He read about one per cent of the literature and then derived the rest himself. He says he prefers to develop some ideas independently rather than read the literature comprehensively (pp. 4, 41). This is a variant of the X002/X001 pattern: here a crowded topic is entered deliberately by committing to a talk (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: equivalence theorem to carry guarantees across a reformulation (Theorem 7, pp. 28–30).
- New-pattern candidate: check derived formulas against invariants they must satisfy (pp. 18, 34–35, 38).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Characterize the step of a transformed method as a QP in the original variables, (4.9) → (4.12). Extend the method by substituting new structure into that QP rather than into the closed-form direction (pp. 19, 25–27).
- If a full Newton step on a homogeneous objective is not unique, linearize the term that breaks homogeneity, here n log cᵀx. This restores a unique, stabilized step (p. 21).
- Prove equivalence by induction. Given x⁽ᵏ⁾ = Zᵀy⁽ᵏ⁾, show that the two step-defining QPs coincide. Exact line searches on equal potentials then give x⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = Zᵀy⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ (p. 30).
- Failure proof: combine an exact per-iteration reduction identity with a geometric contraction of an angle variable, so that the total reduction is finite (pp. 38–39).
- Test a method in the limit of "very many constraints" by letting the constraints form a continuum and integrating the barrier sum analytically (pp. 32–33).
- After deriving a formula, test it against invariants the method must preserve, such as feasibility of the direction and orthogonality to the barrier gradient, before using it (pp. 18, 34–35).

**Quotes**:
- "Applying this version to a semi-infinite programming problem in only two variables, we find that the algorithm is not always efficient when there are very many constraints." (p. 4)
- On treating linear constraints explicitly rather than inside a penalty function: "I still hold this view, and it has been strengthened by the results of this paper." (p. 40)

### S030 · A trust region algorithm for equality constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming 1990, DOI 10.1007/bf01588787)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Y. Yuan (DAMTP, Cambridge; p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The text is the journal version, Mathematical Programming 49 (1991) 189–211. It was received on 20 February 1986 and revised on 14 March 1989 (p. 1). The original version is report DAMTP 1986/NA2 (Powell and Yuan 1986b) (pp. 20, 22). It extends Powell and Yuan's line-search recursive QP algorithm (Math. Program. 35, 1986a) to trust regions (p. 2). This is his derivative-based SQP period, before DFO. **Read**: full text, pp. 1–23, including the references. The OCR is noisy and most displayed formulas are garbled. The legible prose was enough to follow every lemma's statement and the logic of every proof, but not every constant.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry is his own earlier result, carried to a new globalization strategy: "extend the results in Powell and Yuan (1986a) from line searches to trust regions" (p. 2).
  - He gives three reasons.
    - A differentiable exact penalty avoids difficulties due to first-derivative discontinuities, the best known of which is the Maratos effect (p. 2).
    - Line searches can be severely inefficient far from the solution (Powell 1985) (pp. 2–3).
    - Trust regions remove the need for B_k to be positive definite on the null space of A_kᵀ, which line-search algorithms require (p. 3).
  - *Gap*: earlier trust-region methods for equality constraints (Byrd, Schnabel and Shultz 1985; Vardi 1985) use a nondifferentiable penalty (p. 2).
  - *Technical obstacle*: the trust-region bound and the linearized constraints may be inconsistent (p. 2).
  - The introduction says that the combination of a differentiable exact penalty with trust regions, without second derivatives, is "worthwhile to try" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: take an algorithm whose analysis you already own and change one component (here the globalization). Before starting, list the specific defects of the old component that the new one removes: the Maratos effect, inefficiency far from the solution, and the positive-definiteness requirement (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Subproblem* (2.1)–(2.3), p. 3: minimize g_kᵀd + ½dᵀB_kd subject to ‖c_k + A_kᵀd‖₂ ≤ ζ_k and ‖d‖₂ ≤ Δ_k.
    - ζ_k may be any number between the least linearized residual attainable in two smaller balls, radii b₁Δ_k and b₂Δ_k. The two constants are fixed in (0, 1); their order is partly illegible in the OCR.
    - The lower end keeps the constraints consistent. The upper end forces a strict reduction of the violation unless ‖c_k‖ = 0 (p. 2).
  - *Kept from Celis, Dennis and Tapia (1985)*: the linearized constraint is relaxed to a norm bound (p. 2).
  - *Changed*: ζ_k comes from the least residual in a smaller ball, not from a Cauchy step. With this choice ζ_k is zero on more iterations when ill-conditioning makes the Cauchy step short (p. 2).
  - *Merit function*: Fletcher's differentiable exact penalty φ_k(x) = f(x) − λ(x)ᵀc(x) + σ_k‖c(x)‖², where λ(x) are the least-squares multipliers (2.4)–(2.5) (p. 3).
  - *Predicted change D_k* (2.6) has three non-standard terms (pp. 3–4):
    - it uses λ(x_k + d_k);
    - its curvature term uses only d̂_k, the projection of d_k onto the null space of A_kᵀ;
    - it uses the midpoint constraint estimate c_k + ½A_kᵀd_k.
    The projection is there because the superlinear analysis needs only the weaker condition that the null-space part of (B_k − W*)d_k/‖d_k‖ tends to zero. The midpoint estimate is "important to the analysis of Section 4" (p. 4). Using λ(x_k + d_k) lets σ_k be adjusted without second derivatives (p. 5).
  - *Steps 0–3* (p. 4):
    - Stop when the Kuhn–Tucker residual is at most ε.
    - Increase σ_k by (2.9) only when D_k fails test (2.8).
    - Accept the step iff r_k > 0, that is, iff the penalty function decreases (p. 5).
    - Update Δ_k by rule (2.11). It uses the thresholds 0.9 and 0.1 as far as the OCR shows. After a rejected step, Δ_{k+1} is at most Δ_k/4 (pp. 4, 15).
  - Transferable: if a trust region can make the linearized constraints inconsistent, relax them to ‖c + Aᵀd‖ ≤ ζ, with ζ between two computable residual minima. The proofs then use only these two bounds, so an implementer may choose any ζ in the interval (pp. 2–3, 20, 22).
  - Transferable: tie the penalty-parameter update to the predicted-reduction test. Raise σ only when the predicted change fails a sufficient condition, and by at least a factor of 2 (p. 10). A bound on σ then implies only finitely many increases (Lemma 3.6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f and the constraint functions are continuously differentiable, with linearly independent constraint gradients. The theoretical analysis additionally assumes continuous third derivatives (p. 1).
  - *Assumptions 3.1* (p. 5):
    - (a) a bounded, convex, closed set Ω contains every x_k and x_k + d_k;
    - (b) A(x) has full column rank on Ω;
    - (c) the B_k are uniformly bounded.
    A condition like (a) is "inevitable" to exclude an objective that is unbounded below on the feasible region (p. 5).
  - B_k need not be positive definite (pp. 3, 21).
  - *Local assumptions* (p. 14):
    - x_k → x*;
    - second-order sufficiency on the null space of A(x*)ᵀ (Assumption 4.1);
    - a projected Dennis–Moré-type condition (4.4) on B_k (Assumption 4.2).
    4.1(a) is "not very restrictive", because the exact penalty has a local minimum at x* when σ is large enough (p. 14).
  - The global result in §3 needs ε > 0. The case ε = 0 is handled separately in §4 (p. 15).
  - The subproblem is analysed as if solved exactly. Tolerances of the form (5.7) are admissible, as shown in the original report (p. 20).
  - Full-rank constraint gradients are needed to define λ(x). A regularized λ, (5.1), is suggested as a remedy, but the authors say that their theory does not apply to it (p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *Lemma 3.2* (p. 6) is a Cauchy-type bound on gᵀd for the exact trust-region solution. It is proved with a generalized inverse and Theorem 5.2.1 of Fletcher (1987).
  - *Lemma 3.3* (p. 7) gives a guaranteed decrease of the linearized constraint violation in two cases, depending on whether the minimum-norm correction fits in the smaller ball.
  - *Lemma 3.4* (pp. 7–8) is an upper bound on the predicted change. The tangential component solves a reduced trust-region problem in the null space (3.22)–(3.23), and this remains true after the constraint is dropped, so Lemma 3.2 applies to the projected gradient P_kg_k (p. 8).
  - *Corollary 3.5 and Lemma 3.6* (pp. 9–10): σ_k is bounded. Because each increase at least doubles σ_k, it is increased only finitely often.
  - *Lemma 3.7* (p. 10): Δ_k → 0 if the algorithm does not terminate. The proof is by contradiction on a subsequence where Δ grows. The acknowledgment credits a referee with the method of proof (p. 22).
  - *Lemma 3.8* (pp. 11–12): ‖c_k‖ → 0, by an up-crossing argument. Each passage of ‖c_k‖ from level 0.9ξ down to 0.1ξ forces a fixed decrease in the monotone, convergent sequence φ(x_k), which is a contradiction.
  - *Theorem 3.9* (pp. 12–14): with ε > 0 the algorithm terminates, that is, liminf(‖c_k‖ + ‖P_kg_k‖) = 0. The proof first shows that the ratio ‖P_kd_k‖/Δ_k tends to zero on a subsequence (3.55)–(3.56). It then builds an explicit comparison vector (3.57) that is feasible for the subproblem and has a smaller model value for large i. The construction needs the constant in the left inequality of (2.3) to be less than 1 (p. 12).
  - *Local analysis* (pp. 14–18). The stated aim is to show that "our use of trust regions does not invalidate" superlinear convergence (p. 15).
    - Lemma 4.3: ‖d_k‖ = O(‖c_k‖ + ‖P_kg_k‖) (p. 15).
    - Lemma 4.4: σ_k stays bounded when ε = 0. "No details of the remainder of the proof are given" (p. 16).
    - Lemma 4.5: a lower bound on the predicted reduction (p. 17).
    - Lemma 4.6: r_k → 1. The proof differentiates the normal equation for λ(x) at x* (4.25)–(4.27), so that the λ(x_k + d_k) − λ_k term matches W* (p. 18).
    - Hence Δ_k stays bounded away from zero, the trust region becomes inactive, ζ_k = 0, and every step is an SQP step. The theory of Boggs, Tolle and Wang (1982) and Powell (1983) then gives superlinear convergence (Theorem 4.7, p. 18).
  - Transferable: to show that a safeguarded method keeps the fast local rate, prove that the ratio r_k → 1, so the safeguard (trust region, relaxed constraints) becomes inactive. Then cite the unsafeguarded method's rate theorem instead of re-proving it (pp. 15, 18).
  - Transferable: the up-crossing argument. To prove that a nonnegative quantity tends to zero under a monotone merit function, show that every excursion between two fixed levels costs a fixed amount of merit (pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - None. The algorithm "has not been used yet for any numerical computations", partly because the subproblem multipliers' adjustment was not yet decided (p. 21).
  - An earlier, tested procedure differed: it first minimized ‖c_k + A_kᵀd‖ in the trust region. Yuan reported its results in an unpublished talk at the 1985 Dundee conference, and they compared favourably with the 1986a line-search algorithm (p. 21).
  - The subproblem solver is left to Yuan (1988), for positive-definite B_k (pp. 5, 21).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - It continues Powell and Yuan (1986a) and Celis, Dennis and Tapia (1985) (p. 2).
  - It contrasts itself with Byrd, Schnabel and Shultz and with Vardi, who use a nondifferentiable penalty, and with Celis et al.'s Cauchy-step choice of ζ (p. 2).
  - It relies on outside results rather than re-proving them:
    - Boggs, Tolle and Wang (1982) and Powell (1983) for the local rate (pp. 15, 18);
    - Fletcher (1987) for the trust-region characterization and the ζ subproblem (pp. 6, 20);
    - Bertsekas (1982) for the known advantages of differentiable penalties (p. 19);
    - Sorensen (1981) for the difficulty of keeping sparse B_k positive definite (p. 21).
  - It rejects the common "sufficient decrease" acceptance rule and supports this with a one-variable example (p. 21).
  - The referee is credited for the proof of Lemma 3.7 (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The abstract has two sentences and hedges with "Under certain conditions" (p. 1).
  - The main limitation is stated right after the algorithm, before the proofs: "mainly of theoretical interest" (p. 5).
  - Straight after the algorithm, before any proof, a paragraph explains the two unusual terms of D_k by the analysis that needs them (p. 4).
  - Roadmap paragraphs open §1 and §4 (pp. 3, 15).
  - The Discussion argues each design choice by mechanism, not by numbers: trust region versus line search, differentiable penalty, and acceptance rule (pp. 19–21). One example: a line search scales down the predicted reduction in constraint violation, while a trust-region step keeps a constraint-correcting component of length at least a fixed fraction of Δ_k (p. 19).
  - It closes with hedged optimism: the theory seems to allow many suitable choices of the constants (p. 22).
  - Transferable: after stating an algorithm, add one paragraph that justifies each unusual term in the acceptance test by pointing to the proof that needs it (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: theory. Global convergence (Theorem 3.9) and local superlinear convergence (Theorem 4.7) are proved for trust regions combined with Fletcher's differentiable exact penalty, without second derivatives (pp. 12, 18). There are also two design contributions: the choice of ζ_k, and the form of D_k (pp. 2–4).
  - *Limits, stated concretely*:
    - no subproblem solver yet (pp. 5, 20–21);
    - no numerical results (p. 21);
    - the full-rank requirement for λ(x) (p. 19);
    - with ε = 0 the sequence might not converge. He declines to add a remedy, because the stopping test does not need one (p. 21);
    - the constants need practical experience (pp. 21–22).
  - *Future*:
    - a technique for minimizing (2.1) subject to both constraints of (2.2), which would also serve Celis et al.'s method (p. 20);
    - rules for the subproblem multipliers (p. 21);
    - B_k updates that need not be positive definite, useful when B_k is sparse (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Method 1 (relax one limitation per solver), evidence. The 1986a penalty function, the multiplier device λ(x_k + d_k) and the SQP subproblem are kept. Only the globalization changes, from line search to trust region (pp. 2–5).
- ⚠ Method 2 (two-radius discipline), variant. There is a single radius Δ_k driven by the ratio test (legible thresholds 0.1 and 0.9, not NEWUOA's 0.1 and 0.7) and no separate resolution parameter (p. 4). The rationale is related: a trust-region step keeps a constraint-correcting component of length at least a fixed fraction of Δ_k, while a line search shrinks it (p. 19).
- ✅ Heuristic 6 (model each constraint separately; use the merit function only to accept steps), evidence. The constraints are linearized individually in the subproblem, and the penalty function is used only in the ratio test (pp. 3–5). The Discussion says the main purpose of merit functions is to compare estimates of the solution (p. 21).
- ✗ Method 5 (experiment-first validation), contradiction. The algorithm is published with no numerical computation at all (p. 21). The honest-scope half of Method 5 holds: "mainly of theoretical interest" (p. 5).
- ⚠ Method 6 (ship code), variant. No code; the subproblem solver is delegated to the coauthor's report, Yuan (1988) (pp. 5, 21).
- New-pattern candidate: publish preliminary results with explicit status. This is a theory-only paper that says so (pp. 5, 21). Also S047, S072, S036.
- New-pattern candidate: make the theorem admit the practitioner's preferred choice, and prove it for a family. Any ζ_k in the interval, any admissible constants, and tolerances (5.7) are all covered (pp. 3, 20, 22). Also S129, S093.
- New-pattern candidate: safeguard designed so that the proof goes through. The null-space projection and midpoint terms in D_k exist for the §4 analysis (p. 4). Also R007, S085.
- New-pattern candidate: merit function only compares points. Accept any decrease, and reject "sufficient decrease" rules, supported by a one-variable example (p. 21).
- New-pattern candidate: safeguard-inactivity reduction to an existing local-rate theorem (pp. 15, 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Resolve trust-region/linearized-constraint inconsistency by bounding the residual ‖c + Aᵀd‖ by any ζ between the least residuals attainable in two smaller balls (p. 3).
- In the predicted reduction, use the null-space projection of the step for the curvature term, to obtain superlinear convergence under a projected, weaker Dennis–Moré condition (p. 4).
- Evaluate multipliers at the trial point, λ(x_k + d_k), inside the predicted change, so the penalty parameter can be adjusted without second derivatives (p. 5).
- Raise the penalty parameter by at least a factor of 2 whenever it is raised, so that a boundedness lemma immediately gives finitely many increases (p. 10).
- Prove termination by building an explicit feasible comparison step with a smaller model value, contradicting the optimality of the computed step (pp. 12–14).
- Reduce the local-rate proof to the unsafeguarded SQP theorem by showing r_k → 1 and hence an inactive trust region (pp. 15, 18).
