### R007 · Michael James David Powell. 29 July 1936 — 19 April 2015 (Biographical Memoirs of Fellows of the Royal Society 2017, no DOI in the batch list; DAMTP NA2017/04 preprint. SKILL.md's appendix gives the published version as Biogr. Mems 64:341–366, 2018, DOI 10.1098/rsbm.2017.0023)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M. D. Buhmann, R. Fletcher, A. Iserles, P. Toint (Powell is the subject). Fletcher drafted §2 and died in July 2016 soon after (p. 26). · **Position in trajectory**: A posthumous account of the whole career, 1936–2015. Harwell 1959–76 (p. 5); Plummer Chair at DAMTP from 1976 (p. 9); retired 2001 (p. 24); died 19 April 2015 (p. 3). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–34. That includes the honours and awards (pp. 25–26), the 83-item list of cited Powell papers (pp. 26–33) and the author biographies (pp. 33–34). The three figures (pp. 8, 16, 25) were available only as captions.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The memoir's own entry is a thesis sentence: optimization divides into algorithm designers and theoreticians, and Powell "refused to follow this dichotomy" (p. 3). §§1–5 then supply chronological evidence (pp. 3–24). The memoir shows five ways in which Powell entered problems:
  1. **Institutional application pull.** The Harwell numerical analysis group had a remit to do fundamental research *and* to write subroutines that other AERE staff could use (p. 5). Firms such as ICI and Rolls Royce needed to minimize functions whose derivatives were hard or impossible to obtain (p. 6).
  2. **Seeing promise in a hard-to-read report.** He grasped Davidon's variable-metric report, switched a Leeds seminar to it at the last minute, and pooled work with Fletcher, who had independently reached the same report (pp. 6–7).
  3. **An unexplained practical gap.** DFP could perform much worse than BFGS, a result that "had been observed in practice" before he analysed it (p. 9).
  4. **Testing a celebrated new result.** After Karmarkar's 1985 talk he produced an example showing that the complexity was "not always as good as expected" (p. 17).
  5. **Contact with practitioners.** Many practitioner contacts convinced him that expensive black-box DFO was valuable (p. 17).

  For the Toint project, Powell's own interest in problems with many variables became the student's topic (p. 15).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The recurring design move is to keep a working framework and change one lever.
  - *Augmented Lagrangian*: keep the penalty approach, but reach the solution through "a certain shift of origin in the constraint function" instead of an ever-growing penalty. The shift was then identified with Lagrange multipliers (p. 7).
  - *SQP*: keep the variable-metric mechanism and apply it to the first-order conditions of the constrained problem. Each iterate solves a QP that minimizes a quadratic model of the Lagrangian subject to linearized constraints, which also produces new multiplier estimates. It inherits the fast local rate of variable-metric methods (p. 14).
  - *Dog-leg trajectory* for nonlinear least squares (p. 6).
  - *Trust region*: change the radius using the ratio of actual to predicted reduction, and add a multiple of the identity to the Hessian, which biases the step toward steepest descent (p. 19).

  **Invariant first.** DFP's inverse-Hessian update is a low-rank change that always stays positive definite (p. 7). In SQP he insisted on positive-definite Lagrangian Hessian approximations. He designed "partial updating" to enforce this, although it means some new curvature information cannot be incorporated. He recognised this "small blemish" and kept the choice even after exact-second-derivative SQP appeared (p. 15).

  **DFO path.** The idea was to build a model of the objective, working on the model being cheaper than evaluating the true functions (p. 17). He tried radial basis function (RBF) models first, but "this did not bring, in the optimization context, the results he had hoped for". He then turned to multivariate polynomial interpolation, which produced the unconstrained → bound-constrained → linearly constrained series of papers and a "continuously improving view of how to construct second-order information" (p. 17). A 2011 lecture title in the Fig. 2 caption states the same aim: "A parsimonious way of constructing quadratic models from values of the objective function in derivative-free optimization" (p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Evidence base of the memoir.** Recollections of the four authors, two published interviews (Davis 2005; Vicente 2003), and one paper by another author (Davidon) (p. 26). Only these three non-Powell references are listed (p. 26). Priority claims are therefore asserted, not documented against others' work. Examples are the radius ratio test as "a method introduced by Mike" (p. 19) and the trust-region method "which he pioneered in [10]" (p. 15). The genre is laudatory ("It is difficult to overestimate", p. 14).
  - **Bracket citations are unreliable in §2 and at the start of §4.1.**
    - [3] on p. 6 (a derivative-free method) is a Molecular Physics paper on hard spheres (p. 27).
    - [16] on p. 8 (the coordinate-search counterexample) is "On the convergence of cyclic Jacobi methods" (p. 28).
    - [20] on p. 8 (a conjugate-gradient restart technique) is the Powell–Sabin paper (p. 28).
    - [19] and [20] on p. 14 (variable metric and conjugate gradients) are the bivariate-fitting and Powell–Sabin papers (p. 28).
    - [21], cited on p. 15 for nonlinear conjugate gradients and on p. 19 for ℓ1 approximation, is "Algorithms for nonlinear constraints that use Lagrangian functions" (p. 28).

    Do not identify papers from their bracket numbers in this preprint.
  - **Assumptions the memoir reports for Powell's methods.**
    - DFO: the functions are "typically user-supplied and possibly expensive", and work on the model is cheap relative to evaluating them (p. 17).
    - SQP: a convex quadratic model is kept even when the curvature observed along the step is not convex (p. 15).
    - Conjugate gradients were pursued because they store no Hessian and suit very large problems (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The memoir describes Powell's results but reproduces no proofs. The devices it names:
  - **Counterexamples as a main tool.**
    - The early coordinate-search algorithm can fail to converge (p. 8).
    - Cycling in a feasible-point algorithm ([28], p. 16).
    - The complexity of Karmarkar's method is not always as good as expected ([51, 59, 61], p. 17).
    - A paper on local convergence rates is famous for its counter-examples to widely held beliefs (p. 16). It is ambiguous which paper the memoir means: the penultimate of [36, 39, 71] would be [39], a test-performance paper (p. 30).
  - **Smallest-case analysis.** A very simple two-variable quadratic case with an inexact line search shows why DFP can do much worse than BFGS. The memoir calls this "perhaps his most influential theoretical contribution" and credits it with helping make BFGS the method of choice (p. 9).
  - **Negative-result theorem.** Conjugate-gradient algorithms cannot converge superlinearly without periodic restarts along steepest descent (p. 8).
  - **Safeguards that make a proof possible.**
    - The dog-leg trajectory "enabled the construction of a convergence proof" for Gauss–Newton (p. 6).
    - Ratio-based radius changes were introduced "with the goal of achieving global convergence" (p. 19).
    - He gave the first convergence proof for DFP with an exact line search (p. 9) and a convergence theory for SQP (p. 14).
    - Global-convergence papers for trust-region methods run from 1984 to his last paper in 2015 (p. 15). They include DFO ones: [73] 2003 and [81] 2012, titles on pp. 32–33, contents not read.
  - **Devices from approximation theory.**
    - *Counting conditions against degrees of freedom*: the six-triangle Powell–Sabin split has "nine conditions, the right number of conditions for the degrees of freedom" (p. 20).
    - *Removing a regularity assumption at no cost in rate*: he proved convergence of thin-plate-spline interpolation on any bounded domain, where earlier results needed Lipschitz boundaries. The rate is within a log h factor, as good as the best earlier result. He also found the best constants for 2-, 3- and 4-point configurations (p. 23).
    - *Other devices*: dynamic programming with a recursive reduction of the data (p. 19); nonsingularity of RBF interpolation matrices at distinct points (p. 21); an A-acceptability characterization with Iserles (p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments**: none in the memoir. The experimental practice it reports:
  - **Large-n demonstration as an argument.** When others said the curse of dimensionality ruled out even ten variables, he presented hundred-variable problems solved quickly with DFP (p. 7).
  - **Head-to-head practical experience.** Early derivative-free methods did worse than finite-difference versions of gradient methods (p. 8).
  - **Model class rejected by tests.** Numerical tests showed RBF approximations to be "not so useful" for the local models optimization needs (p. 21; also p. 17). His PhD student Hans Martin Gutmann later took up RBFs in optimization "with success" (p. 21).
  - **Observed gap, then explanation.** The DFP–BFGS gap was seen in practice first and explained afterwards (p. 9).
  - **A test-performance paper.** "The performance of two subroutines for constrained optimization on some difficult test problems" ([39], p. 30; title only).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Credit predecessors and state the added value.**
    - SQP: "Han and Biggs have had already made some inroads". Powell supplied "a clarification and implementation as well as a convergence theory" (p. 14).
    - DFP started from understanding Davidon's report and joining forces with Fletcher (pp. 6–7).
    - The sparse-Hessian work extends Curtis–Powell–Reid to symmetric matrices (p. 15).
  - **Critique rivals by constructed example, not rhetoric**: coordinate search (p. 8), Karmarkar (p. 17). His own first DFO method is recorded as "no longer used, being superseded" (p. 6).
  - **The memoir's own positioning.** It places Powell against the designer/theoretician split (p. 3). It also concedes that in the DFO years his interest in theoretical questions "slowly decreased over the years" in favour of defending the practical side (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **The memoir.** Chronological: §1 Early days, §2 Harwell, §3 Cambridge, §4 Research in Cambridge (Optimization, Approximation), §5 Epilogue (pp. 3–24). Anecdote alternates with technical summary, praise is high and hedging low, and Powell's interview voice is quoted (pp. 10–12).
  - **Powell's writing, teaching and editing, as reported.**
    - His textbook has "more explanations and less formulæ" (p. 18).
    - His lecture notes ran to "two sides of hand-written A4 per lecture" (p. 12), and his talks used colour-coded handwritten slides (p. 18).
    - His RBF survey [58], like his earlier reviews and his book, "offers simplifications, more clarity in the exposition and improvements of proofs and theorems" (p. 22). His book includes new results or streamlined proofs and carefully chosen problems (p. 18). He wrote a very large number of surveys (p. 17).
    - As founding co-editor of IMA J. Numer. Anal., he made "quality and relevance" the sole criteria for acceptance (p. 12).
    - Analogy as explanation: the gunnery analogy for the augmented-Lagrangian shift, which the memoir itself calls "not entirely correct" (p. 7).
    - Framing a method by analogy: he called SQP a variable metric method for constrained optimization (p. 14; not quoted because the text file breaks the word with a hyphenation mark).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution.** The only full-length career account in the evidence so far. It supplies dates (pp. 5, 9, 24) and names doctoral students: Toint (p. 15), Yuan (pp. 15–16) and Gutmann (p. 21), with Demetriou named as a "research student" and co-author (p. 19). It describes working habits (pp. 10–13) and lists 83 Powell papers (pp. 26–33). Powell never took a PhD. After a Second in the Tripos he did a Diploma in Computer Sciences (p. 4), and he received an ScD in 1979 for his published work (p. 12).
  - **Limits of the memoir.**
    - The DFO era, which is the skill's focus, gets one paragraph (p. 17) plus a few lines in the epilogue (p. 24).
    - COBYLA, BOBYQA and LINCOA are never named. Neither BOBYQA (2009) nor LINCOA appears in the reference list; only UOBYQA [72] and NEWUOA [76] are named (pp. 32–33).
    - The "new generation of software for large-scale optimization" of the retirement years (p. 24) is not identified.
    - Bracket citations are mismatched in places (D3).
    - There are no future directions, as the genre implies.
  - **Limits of Powell's work that the memoir admits**:
    - partial updating cannot always use the new curvature information (p. 15);
    - restarts do not work for very large problems (p. 9);
    - RBF models failed him in optimization (p. 17);
    - his first DFO method was superseded (p. 6).
  - **Working habits relevant to the skill.**
    - He called himself a loner who would "quite like to crack them myself" (p. 10), and he did not want to ask people how they were getting on "more often than maybe once a month" (p. 11).
    - He read and annotated everything a junior colleague wrote (p. 11).
    - After his thin-plate-spline papers he announced he would work "on optimization only" (p. 23).
    - He retired two years early to maximize time for research (p. 24).
    - In his last months he chose to "lend finishing touches to software" and complete papers (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence, strong. The practice is older than the skill says.
  - He made reports and software freely available "Decades before this has been endorsed by governments and funding agencies" (p. 13).
  - The Harwell group's remit included writing subroutines for others (p. 5).
  - The code-as-report practice predates COBYLA by two decades: Harwell "Fortran subroutine" reports in 1970 [9] and 1973 [12], and DAMTP reports VMCWD 1982 [33], ZQPCVX 1983 [35] and TOLMIN 1989 [47] (pp. 27–30).
  - He finished his software before his death (p. 24). This supports step 5 of Method 6 (arrange custodianship).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence.
  - He dropped RBF models for optimization after numerical tests (pp. 17, 21).
  - He used large-n demonstrations to argue for DFP (p. 7).
  - Gaps observed in practice came before the theory that explained them (p. 9).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence plus ⚠ variant.
  - The DFO papers progress from unconstrained to bound-constrained to linearly constrained problems (p. 17).
  - The same move occurs outside the solver series, at the scale of a research programme. SQP carries the variable-metric mechanism into constrained optimization (p. 14). The sparse-Hessian work extends the sparse-Jacobian estimate to symmetric matrices (p. 15). The thin-plate-spline theorem removes a domain-regularity assumption and keeps the rate (p. 23).
  - The variant: *remove one limitation* also applies to theorems and to problem classes, not just to codes.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. The memoir states the DFO premise: model work is cheap compared with evaluations (p. 17). The 2011 lecture title stresses parsimony (p. 16). The memoir traces the least-change idea to quasi-Newton low-rank updating (p. 7) and the PSB update (p. 9). Reference [82], "Beyond symmetric Broyden for updating quadratic models in minimization without derivatives" (p. 33; title only), shows that Powell himself framed the DFO model update in the symmetric-Broyden family. It is also a Powell title for the limitation on other choices of norm that Method 3 lists as unverified.
- Method 2 ✅ partial evidence. The memoir credits Powell with pioneering the trust-region method in [10] (1970) (p. 15) and with the ratio test for radius changes (p. 19). That is the DELTA half of Method 2. It says nothing about RHO.
- Method 4 ✅ weak evidence (from approximation theory). Truncated Laurent expansions reduce the number of logarithm evaluations needed for thin-plate splines (p. 23). The memoir lists efficient and accurate numerical computation and implementation among his interests in approximation theory (p. 18; not quoted because the text file breaks a word there with a hyphenation mark). He treated QP subproblem solvers as a necessary part of robust SQP codes (p. 15).
- Research Taste #5 ✅ verified. SKILL.md holds the dichotomy characterization only as a paraphrase from search snippets; it is an actual sentence on p. 3 (Quote 1).
- Inner Tensions ⚠ variant.
  - Tension 1 is confirmed by the memoir itself: his interest in theory "slowly decreased" in the DFO years (p. 17).
  - But the skill's remark that guarantees for Powell-style methods were apparently still being added in 2026 needs nuance. Powell himself published global-convergence papers for DFO trust-region methods ([73] 2003, [81] 2012; pp. 15, 32–33; contents not read). That is convergence, not complexity.
- Skill corrections for the merger (facts only; SKILL.md not edited):
  - *Academic Lineage*. The memoir names doctoral students: Philippe Toint (p. 15), Ya-xiang Yuan (pp. 15–16) and Hans Martin Gutmann (p. 21), plus research student Ioannis Demetriou (p. 19).
  - *Research Trajectory*: Harwell 1959–76 (p. 5); Plummer Chair from 1976 (p. 9); augmented Lagrangian in the late 1960s at Harwell (p. 7); SQP after 1976 (p. 14); retired 2001 (p. 24).
  - *Signature Work Anatomy (COBYLA)*. The abandoned path before the polynomial-interpolation codes was RBF models (p. 17). His interest in RBFs dates from a mid-1980s conversation with Carl de Boor (p. 21).
  - *Mentor Voice*. Recollections of his supervision exist: he read and commented on everything (p. 11), was patient with students (p. 11), and met visitors about once a month (pp. 10–11).
- New-pattern candidate: *minimal-instance counterexample / smallest-case analysis*. Beliefs are settled by constructing the smallest instance where a method fails or two methods differ. Evidence: coordinate search (p. 8), DFP vs BFGS with two variables (p. 9), cycling [28] (p. 16), Karmarkar (p. 17), and the memoir's statement of this "talent" (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *clarify–implement–prove someone else's idea*. Evidence: DFP from Davidon's report (pp. 6–7), and SQP after Han and Biggs (p. 14). Surveys that re-prove results are a related move (pp. 18, 22).
- New-pattern candidate: *a safeguard designed so that the proof goes through*. Evidence: the dog-leg for Gauss–Newton (p. 6), and radius updates by ratio aimed at global convergence (p. 19).
- New-pattern candidate: *preserve a structural invariant even at the cost of information*. Evidence: positive definiteness in DFP (p. 7), and partial updating in SQP (p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *survey as re-proof*. Evidence: reviews and the book give simplified or improved proofs (pp. 18, 22), and the RBF survey [58] was one of what Powell himself called his opera magna (p. 22).
- New-pattern candidate (working habit): *decisive scope pruning to protect solitary deep work*. Evidence: avoiding committees (p. 12), "optimization only" (p. 23), early retirement (p. 24), monthly check-ins (pp. 10–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] To explain why method A beats method B in practice, build the smallest problem where the gap appears (two variables, a quadratic, one specific inexact line search) and analyse it exactly. Publish that analysis as the explanation (p. 9).
- [D4/D6] To challenge a celebrated claim, whether about convergence or iteration count, construct an explicit instance that violates it rather than arguing in general terms (coordinate search p. 8; Karmarkar p. 17).
- [D4] If a global convergence proof will not go through, change the step so that it does. A dog-leg path or a ratio-controlled radius is designed with the proof in mind and then kept in the code (pp. 6, 19).
- [D2] If a method reaches the answer only as a parameter goes to infinity (a penalty), look for an additive shift of origin that reaches it at a finite parameter value. Then identify the shift with a known dual quantity, as with Lagrange multipliers (p. 7).
- [D2/D7] Introduce a new method as an existing successful mechanism applied to a new problem class, as with SQP presented as variable metric for constrained optimization (p. 14). Readers then inherit the intuition and the expected local rate.
- [D2] Decide which structural invariant the model must keep (positive definiteness, for example). When the data would break it, update only partially and say plainly what information is lost (p. 15).
- [D5] Before investing in a model class for an optimization method, test it inside the optimization loop. Drop it for that use if the tests disappoint, even if the class stays mathematically interesting (RBF, pp. 17, 21).
- [D4] When improving a known theorem, target one regularity assumption, here a Lipschitz boundary, and show that the rate survives without it. Then compute the best constants on the smallest configurations of 2, 3 and 4 points (p. 23).
- [D4] When designing a piecewise element, count the continuity conditions against the degrees of freedom before choosing the split (Powell–Sabin, p. 20).
- [D6] When your contribution builds on someone else's idea, name the originators and state your part as clarification, implementation and convergence theory (p. 14).
- [D7] Write a survey that re-proves the results it covers more simply or more sharply, instead of only summarizing them (pp. 18, 22).
