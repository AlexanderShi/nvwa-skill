### S090 · Univariate multiquadric approximation: reproduction of linear polynomials (Multivariate Approximation and Interpolation 1990, DOI 10.1007/978-3-0348-5685-0_17)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none. The work was begun during a visit by Nira Dyn to Cambridge and finished at the University of Canterbury, Christchurch, where Powell was an Erskine Fellow. Rick Beatson commented on a draft (p. 15). · **Position in trajectory**: The text is report "No. 54 December, 1989" from the UC Research Repository (p. 1). It belongs to the early Cambridge multiquadric theory line (Jackson 1987, Buhmann 1988, p. 2), before the RBF-solver papers of 1992–1999 (card s01). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–15, including the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a conjecture. On the integer lattice Z^d, multiquadric approximations reproduce all polynomials of degree d (Jackson 1987 for c = 0 and odd d; Buhmann 1988 for all c and d). Those proofs rest on Fourier transforms of the regular lattice (p. 2). Powell conjectures that the regular grid is not needed. He argues that this matters because orders of accuracy are tied to polynomial reproduction, and because RBFs are far more versatile than splines at irregular points (p. 2). He starts on the conjecture in the smallest setting: d = 1, and the only condition on the centres is that they are unbounded above and below (pp. 1, 3). For c = 0 the answer is already known, because hat functions reproduce linear polynomials (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Negative step first.* The obvious space S0 of absolutely convergent sums of translates φ(|x − x_j|) contains no nonzero polynomial, even when c = 0 (Lemma 1 and (2.5)–(2.9), pp. 4–5). This seems to contradict the lattice results. Powell resolves it by saying that S0 is "too small" (p. 6).
  - *Change of basis.* He takes the multiquadric analogue (1.5) of the hat function, a second divided difference of three neighbouring translates (p. 3). He rewrites it through the B-spline (Peano kernel) form of second divided differences as ψ_j(x) = ½∫B_j(θ)φ''(x−θ)dθ (3.4), with φ''(r) = c²/(r²+c²)^{3/2} > 0, so each ψ_j is positive (3.5)–(3.6) (p. 7). An approximant in the new space S2 is then a positive kernel applied to K, the piecewise linear interpolant of the coefficients (3.7)–(3.8) (p. 7). If the coefficients are values of a linear f, K is linear and the kernel integral returns f exactly (Theorems 3–4, pp. 8–9).
  - *Limits of S2.* S0 ⊂ S2 (p. 9). No element of S2 approximates a quadratic with bounded error on R (Lemma 5, pp. 9–10).
  - *Density on intervals.* For c ≠ 0, S0 is dense in C[a, b] (Theorem 6, p. 13). The proof expands φ(|x − x_j|) for very large x_j and combines ℓ+2 far centres, each at least twice the previous one, to extract a polynomial of degree ℓ−1 (4.3)–(4.10). Weierstrass then finishes the proof (pp. 11–12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: d = 1. The centres are strictly increasing and unbounded in both directions, with no spacing conditions (pp. 3–4). c may be zero in §2 (p. 4), but c ≠ 0 is needed for the positivity in §3 (p. 7) and in §4 (pp. 11, 13). "admissible" means the defining sum is absolutely convergent for every x (p. 3). In §4 only finitely many coefficients are nonzero (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices** (brief):
  - Characterization lemmas turn "absolutely convergent for every x" into one summability condition on the coefficients. They split off the finite index set I(x) near x and bound the tail by a constant, (2.3)–(2.4) (p. 4) and (3.11)–(3.12) (pp. 7–8).
  - Reproduction reduces to two moment identities of the kernel: it integrates to one (3.13), and odd moments vanish (3.14) (pp. 8–9).
  - Lemma 5 uses an ε–T split of the tail integral (3.22)–(3.24) (p. 10).
  - Theorem 6 uses an explicit Vandermonde-type solution (4.9) whose products w_k x_{j(k)}^ℓ stay bounded (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The only numerical evidence is secondhand. A least-squares fit by Philip Smith of IMSL, with free centres, drove the centres steadily away from the data, and Theorem 6 explains why (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Buhmann (1988) by removing the uniform-spacing requirement when d = 1 (p. 3). The B-spline identity is cited from his own textbook (Powell 1981) (p. 7). Nira Dyn's private results are credited in detail: uniqueness under spacing bounds, and a generalisation to other radial functions (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The introduction states why the conjecture is important before stating what will be proved (p. 2). The conclusion opens with the original aim and judges the result against it (p. 13). Self-criticism is explicit ("Unfortunately", p. 3; the construction is "inelegant", p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Theory. Linear polynomials are reproduced for any unbounded set of univariate centres and every c (c = 0 on p. 6, c ≠ 0 in Theorem 4, pp. 8–9; "valid for all c", p. 14), and for c ≠ 0 S0 is dense on compact intervals (Theorem 6, p. 13).
  - Limits, stated concretely: "most of our work is confined to the univariate case" (p. 3). Building spaces from intermediate combinations is "inelegant" (p. 13). Because of Theorem 6 it is hard even to define "reproduction of linear polynomials" (p. 13). S2 misses important approximants: on Z the cardinal functions reproduce quadratics with bounded error, which Lemma 5 rules out for S2 (pp. 13–14).
  - Future directions: a larger space (5.1) (p. 13); uniqueness of the representation, proved by Powell for S0 (p. 14); several variables. Lines of centres give linear polynomials through the projection (5.3), but these conditions are "stronger than necessary" (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (honest scope in a paper without experiments). The univariate restriction is announced in the introduction (p. 3). The conclusion says the main contribution is only to have "sustained the conjecture", and calls the space construction inelegant (pp. 13–14). No experiments are run.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (theory analogue). The paper removes one hypothesis of a known theorem, the regular lattice, while keeping the degree (linear) and the dimension (d = 1) fixed (pp. 2–3).
- New-pattern candidate: *minimal-instance counterexample / smallest-case analysis* ✅ (existing candidate). The multivariate conjecture is attacked in d = 1, in the hope that "studying the univariate case would provide some ideas" (p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *theory to explain a practitioner-reported failure*. The density theorem is used to explain the diverging centres in IMSL's least-squares fits (p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *change the basis, not the claim*. The natural space contains no polynomials (pp. 4–5). Rather than weakening the conjecture, Powell moves to a divided-difference basis, which makes the sums converge and the result true (pp. 6–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] For an expansion with infinitely many terms, first prove a lemma that reduces "absolutely convergent for every x" to one summability condition on the coefficients. Split off the finite set of terms near x and bound the tail uniformly (Lemmas 1–2, pp. 4, 7–8).
- [D2] If the span of growing basis functions contains none of the target functions, take divided-difference combinations with a positive Peano kernel. The approximant then becomes a kernel smoothing of the piecewise-linear interpolant of the coefficients, and exactness reduces to the kernel's zeroth and first moments (3.4)–(3.8), (3.13)–(3.14) (pp. 7–9).
- [D4] To show that a translate family is dense on an interval, send the centres to infinity at a geometric rate (each at least twice the previous one) and solve a small Vandermonde system. This isolates each Taylor coefficient of the far expansion as a polynomial of known degree (4.8)–(4.10) (p. 12).
- [D8] When a practitioner reports an odd run, check whether one of your theorems explains it, and say so in the paper (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "It seems, however, that our main contribution to this important problem is that we have sustained the conjecture that there is no need for the centres to form a regular grid." (p. 14)
- "Theorem 6 explains some unfortunate numerical experiments that were described to me by Philip Smith of IMSL." (p. 14)

### S089 · Tabulation of thin plate splines on a very fine two-dimensional grid (Numerical Methods in Approximation Theory, Vol. 9 1992, DOI 10.1007/978-3-0348-8619-2_13)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none. The problem was suggested by Ian Barrodale; Rick Beatson and Martin Buhmann commented on a draft (p. 21). · **Position in trajectory**: The text is report "No. 67 February, 1992" from the UC Research Repository (p. 1). The Fortran package was written in 1990 (p. 14). This is Powell's first fast-evaluation paper for RBFs, before the iterative TPS solvers of 1994–1999 (card s01). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–22, including the appendix (pp. 16–21), which was read in full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an application. Two pictures of a changing system are compared: a tumour in a patient, or silting in a shipping channel. Landmarks known to correspond between the pictures (bone structure; buoys, wrecks and rocks) define a thin plate spline (TPS) warp (1.1)–(1.3) (p. 2). The whole picture must then be mapped. That can mean up to 10^8 evaluations with n ≈ 200 (p. 3), or 10^8 grid points with n = 500 (p. 1), and direct evaluation costs O(h^{-2} n) (p. 4). Ian Barrodale posed the problem, for satellite pictures of the forests of British Columbia where road junctions and river confluences serve as landmarks (p. 3). The required accuracy is 6–9 decimal places (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Recursive halving.* Start from a coarse grid of about 20×20 to 40×40 points and halve h k times (p. 3). Values at (odd, odd) points come from a 16-point stencil (2.2) (p. 5). The other new points come from 10-point stencils (2.11)–(2.12) (p. 7). All stencils have O(h^6) error.
  - *Stencil design.* Weights are fixed by exactness on quadratics (2.3). The fourth-order Taylor term is then removed by using the fact that a TPS satisfies the biharmonic equation away from its centres (2.1), (2.6) (pp. 4–6). Two orders are gained without widening the stencil.
  - *Singularities.* For each halving, work with s_h, which deletes every term whose centre is within ∞-norm distance ρh (3.1)–(3.3). Correct explicitly the values near the neighbourhood boundaries, then add back the terms that leave the neighbourhoods as h halves. The corrections loop over the centres j, not over grid points, which avoids an O(h^{-2}) overhead (pp. 9–10).
  - *Parameter from tolerance.* ρ is the least integer with ρ ≥ (6/ε)^{1/6}, so ρ = 14 for ε = 10^{-6} and ρ = 43 for ε = 10^{-9} (p. 11). Total work is O(h^{-2} + nε^{-1/3}|log h|), where the exponent −1/3 comes from the order of the differences (pp. 1, 4, 11).
  - *Edges.* Each intermediate grid covers [−6h, M_h h]², so the recursion inherits three lines of values beyond the target square (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Interpolation points are distinct and not collinear, so the TPS is well defined; the method itself "does not depend on" the side conditions (1.3) (pp. 2–3). The target is relative accuracy ε in each term. The logarithm in the error estimate (3.12) is ignored, and this is justified by scale invariance: a change of units adds a quadratic polynomial to s_h, which the stencils reproduce exactly (p. 11). The stability of the stencils under recursion is not analysed (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices** (brief):
  - Taylor expansion with symmetry, plus the Laplacian of the biharmonic equation (2.8), gives the sixth-order error term (2.10) (pp. 5–6).
  - The bound |∂⁶φ/∂x⁶ + ∂⁶φ/∂y⁶| ≤ 72/(x²+y²)² (3.10) turns the error term into the choice of ρ (pp. 10–11).
  - The appendix obtains all derivatives of φ in closed complex form (Lemma 1, p. 17) and the radius of convergence of the Taylor series (Lemma 2, pp. 17–18). By rotational symmetry it computes the t-th order error term exactly (Lemmas 3–5, pp. 19–21). For ρ ≥ 10, terms of order ≥ 8 add at most 6.2% to the sixth-order bound (p. 20).
- **D5 Experiments**: Table 1 gives workstation times (Sparc; the OCR reads "Spare") for grids 100² to 800² and n = 25 to 400 with ε = 10^{-6}. Powell warns that the times vary by at least 1% with Unix scheduling (pp. 14–15). The table is used to split the cost into its two parts, about n/100 seconds of correction per halving (p. 15). Baseline: direct evaluation on the 800×800 grid takes 395.56 s for n = 25 and about 16 times longer for n = 400, against 6.59 s and 27.96 s (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: Adopts the TPS warping of Barrodale, Berkley and Skea (1992) and Duchon's (1977) variational justification (p. 2). The unanalysed stability question is related to subdivision schemes (Dyn 1992) (p. 14). The regular-grid method is contrasted with the fast multipole method for points in general position (Greengard–Rokhlin 1987; Beatson–Newsam 1992) (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Opens with two everyday applications in plain language before any formula (p. 2). The cost claim appears in the abstract (p. 1) and again as a formula in the introduction (p. 4). The acknowledgements say he "enjoyed" contributing to the successful use of the method at Barrodale Computing Services (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: An algorithm, a Fortran package delivered to Barrodale Computing Services and later made faster there, and an error analysis (pp. 3, 14). The general lesson drawn is that RBFs are expensive to evaluate compared with piecewise polynomials, so savings on regular grids matter (p. 16).
  - Limits, stated concretely: error accumulation over many halvings is not analysed; the coefficients only "seem to be harmless" (p. 14).
  - Better alternatives are named. A cheaper one-dimensional O(h^6) formula (4.1) could be used along grid lines, though it lacks the two-dimensional structure (p. 15). Working with s instead of s_h throughout is "probable" to be more efficient for typical ρ, judging from Table 1 (p. 16). Very fine grids can be split into pieces that fit in storage (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (outside DFO). The cost is written as a formula in h, n and ε before any timing is shown (pp. 1, 4, 11). The loop order is chosen to avoid an O(h^{-2}) overhead (pp. 9–10). The algorithm parameter ρ is derived from the accuracy tolerance through an error bound (p. 11).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. Measurement noise in the timings is disclosed (p. 14). The unanalysed stability question is stated (p. 14). He says that another design is probably faster than the one he implemented (p. 16).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant. Code and report travel together, but the package went to a company (Barrodale Computing Services), which later made it faster. No free-use statement appears in this paper (pp. 3, 14, 21).
- New-pattern candidate: *publish the rejected alternative* ✅ (existing candidate). Formula (4.1) and the f = s variant are described together with their trade-offs (pp. 15–16).
- New-pattern candidate: *verify the asymptotic design rule against all higher-order terms*. ρ is chosen from the leading O(h^6) term, and an appendix then bounds every higher-order term in closed form to confirm the rule at practical h (pp. 16–21).
- New-pattern candidate: *invariance argument to justify a simplification* (related to the existing *invariance as a veto on a more accurate variant*, card s01). Exactness on quadratics makes the error independent of the units, which licenses ignoring the log factor (p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] When the target function satisfies a known PDE away from its singularities, design finite-difference stencils by exactness on low-degree polynomials and then use the PDE to cancel the next Taylor term. This gains two orders of accuracy at no extra width (2.3)–(2.10) (pp. 5–6).
- [D2] Split a sum of singular kernels into a smooth part (terms whose centres are farther than ρh) and a near part that is evaluated exactly. Derive ρ from the leading error bound and the tolerance, ρ = ⌈(6/ε)^{1/6}⌉ (pp. 9–11).
- [D4] After fixing a parameter by the leading asymptotic error term, compute or bound all higher-order terms at the smallest allowed parameter value, and report the worst excess (6.2%) (p. 20).
- [D5] Lay out timings on a grid of size × n so that the two cost components can be separated. Use the smallest-n column as an upper bound on one component. Give the brute-force baseline time for the largest case (pp. 14–15).

**Quotes**:
- "Ian Barrodale suggested that I study this problem because he knew of my interest in radial basis function methods." (p. 3)
- "We have not investigated analytically whether a sequence of applications of the method of Section 2 can cause an unacceptably large accumulation of errors." (p. 14)

### S186 · DAMTP 1997/NA18, which is "Trust region calculations revisited" (DAMTP report 1997, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none. Jorge Moré commented on the draft of Sections 1–2 (p. 20). · **Position in trajectory**: This is a duplicate. The text is byte-identical to that of S165 (card c01): report DAMTP 1997/NA18, September 1997, presented at the 17th Dundee Biennial Conference (p. 1). The full D1–D8 analysis is in card c01 (S165). The entries below are short restatements with page references, plus the observations that c01 does not make. **Read**: full text, pp. 1–20.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a community belief. Truncated conjugate gradients (CG) (Steihaug 1983) is increasingly preferred to Moré–Sorensen (MS) because MS is thought too expensive (p. 3). A two-variable example with Δ = 1 shows CG obtaining a model reduction of only about 2×10^{-6} where more than 1.0 is available (p. 3). Details are in c01.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: MS with Powell's own λ updates (pp. 7–10), and the band-matrix change of variables that gives O(n^{7/3}), or O(n^{11/5}) in product form, per quasi-Newton iteration (pp. 12–20). Not in c01:
  - A cheap initial lower bound on λ* combines 0, the largest −B_ii and a triangle-inequality bound of the form ‖g‖/Δ − ‖B‖₁ (2.10). The last term is "particularly useful" when Δ is very small (p. 7).
  - When a Cholesky attempt fails at pivot k, the partial factor gives a vector u with uᵀ(B+λI)u ≤ 0, and the Rayleigh quotient raises λ_L (2.11) (p. 7).
  - An exactly singular B+λI gets no special code. The consistency test and shortest solution it would need are judged not worth it, and λ is adjusted instead (p. 8).
  - The rotation count can be roughly halved by zeroing the first p and last q components of r̂ at the same time (pp. 15–16).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Euclidean norm throughout (p. 2). B is symmetric and possibly indefinite (p. 2). §3 assumes B is dense and changed by rank-one or rank-two updates (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices** (brief): The characterization theorem is proved through the Lagrangian and a negative-curvature perturbation (pp. 4–5). The secular function 1/Δ − 1/‖d(λ)‖ is convex (p. 6). The MS termination test follows from an exact quadratic identity (pp. 10–11). Givens rotations are counted, O(n²/(s+1)) (pp. 13–17), and cost monomials are balanced (p. 20). New relative to c01: the general elimination is first shown on an explicit n = 10, s = 2 sparsity pattern (3.9), with the exact sequence of rotation types listed (pp. 14, 16), before the general bound (3.11) (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: One timing table, copied from Powell (1997): band versus full, n = 50–800, on a Sun 4 workstation. It gives c ≈ 0.12 and savings from n ≥ 40 (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Defends MS against Steihaug. It cites Gould, Rendl–Wolkowicz and Sorensen in one sentence (p. 3) and ends by reporting Yuan's (1997) conjecture, which favours CG for positive definite B (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The thesis is argued in the abstract, "should be employed more often" (p. 1). The Discussion critiques the standard theoretical questions (pp. 17–18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: An exposition plus a complexity reduction. The practical importance is called "debatable" (p. 17). The advantage shrinks for positive definite B used with line searches, where O(n²) per iteration is already standard (p. 19).

**Method links** (as in c01, confirmed on the same text):
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The per-iteration cost falls from O(n³) to O(n^{7/3}) or O(n^{11/5}) (pp. 12–20). The λ lower bounds reuse work already done, the failed factorization (p. 7).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (rationale). "one should try to avoid small values of the radius" while the variables are far from a minimum (p. 18).
- New-pattern candidates, all already in the pool: *tiny counterexample against a popular default* (p. 3); *critique the questions theory asks* (pp. 17–18); *advocacy rewrite of one's own report for a broader audience* (p. 17); *balance cost monomials by choosing parameters as powers of n* (p. 20); *timing sweep to calibrate hidden constants and the crossover n* (p. 17).

**Transferable techniques** (only those not in c01):
- [D2] In a λ-search that factorizes B+λI, treat a failed Cholesky factorization as information. Build u from the partial factor so that uᵀ(B+λI)u ≤ 0, and use the Rayleigh quotient to raise the lower bound on λ* (2.11) (p. 7).
- [D7] Before stating a general elimination or annihilation procedure and its operation count, run it on one small explicit sparsity pattern, listing each rotation (n = 10, s = 2) (pp. 14, 16).

**Quotes**:
- "This unwelcome situation occurs because B is ill-conditioned, and the truncated conjugate gradient method has become the method of steepest descents." (p. 3)
- "It is hoped that this conclusion will be valuable to the future development of software for nonlinear optimization calculations." (p. 20)
