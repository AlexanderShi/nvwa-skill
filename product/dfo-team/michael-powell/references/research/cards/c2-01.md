The four core papers cover four genres: the DFP quasi-Newton paper with Fletcher (S001); a Harwell software report whose algorithm combines Newton and steepest-descent steps under a step bound, with Broyden updates (S019); an ICM rate-of-convergence study driven by extreme-precision numerics (S139); and an SQP merit-function paper with Yuan (S050). ### S001 · A rapidly convergent descent method for minimization (The Computer Journal 1963, DOI 10.1093/comjnl/6.2.163)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: R. Fletcher, who then held a D.S.I.R. research studentship (acknowledgement, p. 6) · **Position in trajectory**: The start of the Harwell-era quasi-Newton line, and the paper that introduced the DFP method. It reworks Davidon's 1959 report and supersedes Powell's own 1962 method (p. 1). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–6, including the appendix (line search) and the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is to find an unconstrained local minimum of f of n variables when the gradient is available analytically. The derivative-free case is referred to Rosenbrock (1960) (p. 1).
  - The entry is a neglected good idea. Davidon's work "has been little publicized" but is judged "a considerable advance over current alternatives" (p. 1).
  - The selection criterion comes from asymptotics: near a minimum the second-order Taylor terms dominate, so only methods that minimize a general quadratic quickly can converge fast. This filter leaves four of the listed methods, and two of those do not apply to general functions (p. 1).
  - Newton–Raphson is ruled out because it needs second derivatives and "frequently fails to converge from a poor approximation to the minimum" (p. 1).
  - Transferable: turn an asymptotic argument into a necessary property (here quadratic termination), and use it to filter the whole list of competing methods before proposing your own (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Method*: keep a symmetric positive definite matrix H, initially the identity, so the first step is steepest descent. Search along s = −Hg with a line minimization, set σ = αs, and add two terms to H: A = σσᵀ/σᵀy and B = −Hyyᵀ H/yᵀHy (pp. 2–3; formulas paraphrased from garbled text).
  - *Kept from Davidon*: the variable metric idea and the cubic-interpolation line search (p. 2; appendix, p. 6).
  - *Changed*: (a) a simplification that preserves the orthogonality conditions on which the rate depends; (b) an improved convergence criterion (p. 1). Some of Davidon's other ideas can stop H tending to G⁻¹ even on a quadratic (p. 3); in the 2-D test the culprit was "one of the alternatives allowed by Davidon" (p. 4). His iterations may also search along more than one direction (p. 4). The new method needs one line search per iteration (p. 3).
  - *Termination*: stop when the predicted distance to the minimum, or every component of s, is small. Two additional safeguards had been found necessary in automatic programs: do at least n iterations, and apply the tests to σ as well as to s (p. 2).
  - *Why this form*: the update is derived, not guessed (§6, p. 3). A makes the sum of the A terms equal G⁻¹ on a quadratic, and B is "the simplest form" that satisfies the eigenvector condition (9), made symmetric.
  - *By-product*: H tends to G⁻¹, so the curvature at the minimum is available for convergence tests and variance estimates (p. 1). If a good positive definite approximation is known in advance, as when solving simultaneous equations, use it as H⁰ (p. 5).
  - Transferable: derive an update from the invariant you want it to have. Here a sum of rank-one terms reconstructs G⁻¹ on quadratics, and the remaining term is the simplest symmetric one satisfying the required condition (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f can be computed at every point, and the gradient is analytic (p. 1). H⁰ is symmetric positive definite (p. 2).
  - The theory assumes exact line minimization. The practical line search (cubic interpolation) is declared "not central to the theory" (p. 2).
  - The termination proof assumes f is the quadratic (1) with a well-defined minimum (p. 2).
  - No hypothesis for a general (non-quadratic) f is stated anywhere (pp. 1–5); see D8.
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *Stability* (§4, p. 2): s is downhill if and only if H^i is positive definite, which is proved by induction. Set p = (H^i)^{1/2}x and q = (H^i)^{1/2}y, apply Schwarz's inequality, and use σᵀy > 0, which follows from the exact line search (the new gradient is orthogonal to σ).
  - *Quadratic termination* (§5, pp. 2–3): an induction carries two invariants together, G-conjugacy of the steps (10) and H^kGσ^i = σ^i (11). After n steps the σ's are n independent eigenvectors of H^nG with eigenvalue 1, so H^n = G⁻¹. The final gradient is orthogonal to n independent vectors and is therefore zero.
  - *Derivation of the A term* (§6, p. 3): conjugacy gives SᵀGS = Λ diagonal, so G⁻¹ = SΛ⁻¹Sᵀ, which is the sum of the A terms (15).
  - Transferable: prove finite termination by an induction that carries a geometric invariant (conjugacy) and an operator invariant (eigenvectors of H·G) together. The matrix limit H^n = G⁻¹ then comes for free (pp. 2–3).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Rosenbrock* from (−1.2, 1.0): 18 iterations, one line search each. Table 1 compares steepest descents and Powell's 1962 method in "equivalent" iterations, charging Powell's method three line searches per iteration (pp. 3–4).
  - *Powell's four-variable function* from (3, −1, 0, 1): f fell from 215 to 2.5×10⁻⁸ in six iterations. In the equivalent of seven iterations, Powell's 1962 method reached 9×10⁻³ and steepest descents 6.36. The growth of H's elements revealed the singular G at the minimum (p. 4).
  - *2-D quadratic against Davidon's original*: the new method terminates in two iterations with H = G⁻¹ (Table 2). Davidon's H was still inaccurate, and "the computation had to be stopped manually" (p. 4).
  - *Helical valley* (3-D) from (−1, 0, 0): 18 iterations against Davidon's 10. Because Davidon's iterations search several directions, "the time taken by the two procedures was almost identical" (p. 4).
  - *Large n*: a sum of squares of trigonometric equations with random integer coefficients in [−100, 100] and a perturbed start, on the Stretch computer, with accuracy 0.0001 in every variable (p. 5).
    - There were 15 runs with n from 5 to 100. Evaluations ranged from 19 (n = 5) to 318 (n = 100) and were "approximately linear in the number of variables". Five runs found a different minimum, which is explained by the existence of up to 2ⁿ solutions (p. 5, Table 4).
    - All runs took fifteen minutes in total (p. 5).
    - The trace shows the first n iterations behaving like steepest descents, then rapid decrease once H has been built (p. 5).
  - *Comparison units* are equivalent line searches (Table 1), time (helical valley) and function evaluations (Table 4). Runs that reached another minimum stay in the table, marked "No" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: when methods do different amounts of work per iteration, normalize the comparison to one unit (line searches, time or evaluations) and put that unit in the table header (pp. 3–4).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Davidon is credited generously while two specific defects are named: options that break H → G⁻¹ (p. 3), and a termination rule that failed in practice (p. 4).
  - Powell demotes his own 1962 method as slower and more expensive per iteration (pp. 1, 3).
  - Newton is dismissed on cost (second derivatives) and robustness (poor starting points) (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Strong claims with little hedging. The tests "confirm that the method is probably the most powerful general procedure for finding a local minimum which is known at the present time" (p. 1), and Davidon-type methods are "considerably superior" to earlier ones (p. 5).
  - The paper borrows Dirac's bra-ket notation from physics for vectors and operators (p. 1).
  - Order of sections: method as a list of steps (p. 2), then stability, termination, derivation of the update (pp. 2–3), numbers (pp. 3–5) and conclusion (p. 5), with the line search in an appendix (p. 6). Theory comes before numbers, in six pages.
  - Transferable: state the algorithm as an explicit step list before any proof, and move the non-essential component (the line search) to an appendix with a note that it is "not central to the theory" (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution levels: an algorithm (the DFP update), theory (descent at every iteration and quadratic termination), and numerical evidence up to n = 100 (pp. 1–5).
  - Gap between claim and proof: the abstract says the theorems show that "it always converges" (p. 1). The proofs in the text cover descent at every iteration and n-step termination for quadratics only (pp. 2–3). No convergence theorem for general f appears.
  - Limits stated: the first n iterations are slow, remedied by a good H⁰ (p. 5); runs may converge to another local minimum (p. 5).
  - No future-work paragraph. The conclusion asserts practicability for large n "even if only poor initial approximations to a solution are known" (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Method 1 (relax one limitation), evidence: the paper keeps Davidon's variable-metric core and changes two named things, the orthogonality-preserving simplification and the convergence criterion (p. 1). The motive is Davidon's alternatives that break H → G⁻¹ (p. 3).
- ⚠ Method 3 (fewer evaluations than model parameters), variant or precursor: second-derivative information is accumulated from successive gradient differences instead of being evaluated, and it becomes exact only after n steps (pp. 2–3, 5). The later least-change norm idea is not in this paper.
- ✅ Method 4 (cost as part of the algorithm), evidence: "each iteration is quick and simple to carry out" is part of the claimed superiority (p. 1). Comparisons charge each method its line searches (p. 3) or time (p. 4).
- ⚠ Method 5 (experiments with honest scope), variant: known test functions, a sweep up to n = 100 and wrong-minimum runs kept in the table (pp. 3–5) are all consistent with the Method. The tone is not: superlatives ("probably the most powerful", p. 1) replace the tested-range statements of the later notes.
- ✗ Research Taste warning sign 2 (accuracy/convergence claims the method cannot guarantee), contradiction in an early co-authored paper: the abstract claims "it always converges" (p. 1), while only descent and quadratic termination are proved (pp. 2–3). For contrast, S139 (1983) states that variable metric theory holds only for convex objectives (S139, p. 1).
- ✅ Research Taste mark 2 (evaluations as currency), evidence: Table 4 reports evaluation counts against n and notes that they grow roughly linearly (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: *derive the update from the invariant the proof needs* (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *return the algorithm's by-products for user diagnostics*, here H ≈ G⁻¹ for variance estimates (p. 1); also S019, p. 12.
- New-pattern candidate: *cost-normalized comparison*, counting equivalent line searches or time (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Filter the literature with an asymptotic necessary condition: second-order terms dominate near the solution, so demand quadratic termination (p. 1).
- Derive a matrix update from what it must reconstruct on quadratics, then choose the simplest symmetric correction that satisfies the remaining condition (p. 3).
- Prove positive definiteness is inherited by working in the H^{1/2} coordinates and applying Cauchy–Schwarz, with the line-search orthogonality supplying σᵀy > 0 (p. 2).
- Put wrong-answer runs (here a different minimum) in the main table with a Yes/No column and one sentence on why they are not surprising (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "Because, near the minimum, the second-order terms in the Taylor series expansion dominate, the only methods which will converge quickly for a general function are those which will guarantee to find the minimum of a general quadratic speedily." (p. 1)
- "The conclusion to be drawn from this behaviour is that for many applications of the method a substantial number of the iterations required will be spent on setting up the inverse of the matrix of second derivatives." (p. 5)

### S019 · A FORTRAN subroutine for solving systems of nonlinear algebraic equations (AERE Harwell report R 5947 1968, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: Harwell, Theoretical Physics Division, November 1968 (p. 4). This is the software report for Harwell Library routine NS01A (p. 10). The justifying theory is deferred to a "companion paper", Powell (1969), "A hybrid method for non-linear equations", listed as in preparation (pp. 5, 44). **Read**: pp. 1–45 in full. Pages 46–58 (Fortran listing, driver, CALFUN and printed output) were skimmed through comments and messages only.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The problem is to solve n nonlinear equations f_k(x) = 0 when the f_k have continuous first derivatives that the user need not supply (p. 4).
  - The entry is failure analysis. The new algorithm came from "asking when certain useful algorithms would fail". The Newton family fails when the Jacobian becomes singular, and it diverges from poor guesses (p. 5).
  - Haselgrove's approach (Newton direction plus a search on F = Σf_k²) underlies most practical codes. Powell prefers Levenberg–Marquardt, because Haselgrove's method breaks down whenever J is singular, while Levenberg–Marquardt breaks down only at a stationary point of F (p. 7).
  - A two-equation example has det J equal to x₁ (the formula is garbled), so every successful path from a start with x₁ < 0 must cross a singular Jacobian. This shows that singular Jacobians are not uncommon (p. 8). A failing example is deferred to the companion paper (p. 8).
  - The report opens by justifying "yet another algorithm" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: enter a crowded field by listing when each existing method fails, and support each failure mode with a two-variable example (pp. 5–8).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Step* (§4, pp. 18–19): if the Newton correction v satisfies ‖v‖ ≤ Δ, take it. Otherwise, if the predicted steepest-descent minimizer μg is at least Δ long, take the steepest-descent step of length Δ. Otherwise take the point on the segment from μg to v at distance Δ (this is the dogleg). It is preferred to Levenberg–Marquardt because it costs O(n²) operations. Powell concedes that Marquardt's elegant theorem cannot be matched, but says the key property is kept: short steps are biased towards steepest descent (p. 19).
  - *Step bound Δ* (§5, pp. 20–23):
    - If F(x+δ) > F(x) − 0.1·[F(x) − predicted F], Δ becomes max(½Δ, DSTEP) (p. 21).
    - Increases use an extrapolated factor λ, which assumes that prediction errors scale like Δ². Δ is enlarged only after two λ values have been computed since the last reduction, by the smaller λ, by at most a factor of two, and never beyond DMAX. This rule was found by trying strategies that had "caused inefficient oscillatory behaviour" (p. 22).
    - After a full Newton step, Δ is set to max(‖v‖, DSTEP) (p. 23).
  - *Derivatives* (§6, pp. 23–25): Broyden's rank-one update of J, plus a companion update of H ≈ J⁻¹, so that each iteration costs O(n²) instead of O(n³) (pp. 9, 24). A factor α = 0.8 replaces 1 when the denominator is small, which keeps it at least 0.1‖δ‖²; the 0.1 was "chosen empirically" (p. 23).
  - *One evaluation per iteration*: there are no line searches, "unlike earlier methods by the author", following Broyden's advice (p. 9).
  - *Geometry device* (§7, pp. 25–30): because Broyden's update leaves J unchanged on directions orthogonal to δ, the code keeps an orthonormal matrix Ω and counts ω. These ensure that most sets of 2n+1 successive steps span the space, where "independent" means an angle of at least thirty degrees (p. 25). When the test fails, a special step DSTEP·d₁ is taken (p. 27). The bookkeeping costs O(n²) and is stable against rounding (pp. 29–30).
  - *Restart*: when a stationary point of F seems near (test (71)), the Jacobian is recomputed by differences before the routine gives up (p. 35).
  - *Kept*: Newton's fast final convergence, and the Levenberg–Marquardt bias to steepest descent. *Changed*: a path that is cheaper to compute, and Jacobians maintained without derivatives (pp. 6, 9, 19).
  - The conclusion generalizes this into a programme: a step-length parameter Δ, a predicted correction with ‖δ‖ ≤ Δ, automatic adjustment of Δ, and one function value per iteration (p. 43).
  - Transferable: when the preferred method has an expensive subproblem, keep its qualitative property (short steps bend towards steepest descent) and replace it with a piecewise-linear path that costs O(n²) (pp. 18–19).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The f_k have continuous first derivatives, and twice-differentiability is used for the Newton rationale (pp. 4–5). N must be greater than 1 (p. 10).
  - Convergence theorems (bounded iterates; Jacobian approximations converging to the true Jacobian) are claimed for exact arithmetic in the companion paper (pp. 16, 24). None are proved here.
  - Assumptions shifted to the user:
    - Variables must be scaled to similar magnitudes, because one DSTEP and the Euclidean metric are used (p. 10).
    - The functions must be scaled comparably, because of the ACC test (p. 11).
    - DSTEP must be small enough that f is nearly linear over it, yet large enough that the differences are not dominated by rounding (pp. 33, 43).
  - The routine is content to reach a stationary point of F that is not a solution, because some systems have no solution (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - The Newton direction is downhill for F, with directional derivative −2F (7) (p. 6).
  - *Broyden's error identity*: for linear f, the new Jacobian error equals the old error times (I − δδᵀ/‖δ‖²). The Frobenius norm of the error therefore never increases, and it strictly decreases unless the prediction was exact, in which case F should drop substantially (pp. 24–25). This gives a dichotomy: either the model improves or the step succeeds.
  - *Rounding accumulation*: identity (50) shows that the discrepancy J − H⁻¹ is multiplied by a projection at each update, so rounding errors do not accumulate. The maintained independence of the steps makes this "particularly favourable" (p. 25; Powell 1968b).
  - *Rosenbrock analysis*: with an exact Jacobian, the Newton step reduces F only if 0.9 < x₁ < 1.1. This explains why DSTEP must be small (pp. 33–34).
  - *Badly scaled example* (73): the Newton step reduces F only once F is already extremely small (the exponent is garbled), and this change is invariant under linear transformations of the variables. The slow convergence is therefore intrinsic, not a bug (p. 42).
  - The stationary-point test F > 2·DMAX·‖g‖² is justified by a mean-gradient argument along the line to a hypothetical solution within DMAX (p. 34).
  - The step-increase rule is admitted to have a "tenuous" justification (p. 21).
  - Transferable: when an update leaves a subspace unchanged, pair it with explicit bookkeeping (an orthonormal basis and span counts) that forces the steps to span the space within a fixed window (pp. 17, 25–30).
- **D5 Experiments**: All runs were on an IBM 360/65 in single precision (p. 35). The unit of comparison is calls of CALFUN.
  - *Rosenbrock equations* from (−1.2, 1.0), with DSTEP = 0.01, DMAX = 10 and ACC = 10⁻⁶: 28 calls (p. 35), given as 27 evaluations in the comparison on p. 37. Every call is listed, and asterisks mark calls made only to revise the Jacobian (pp. 35–36).
    - The published counts for other methods are Powell (1965) 70; Broyden (1965) 59 and 39; Fletcher (1968) 30 and 90 (p. 37).
  - *Trigonometric equations* of Fletcher–Powell (1963), n = 5, 10, 20, 30, two random systems each (pp. 37–38). This method needs 11–47 calls, against 24–106 for Powell (1965) and 24–135 for Rosen's hybrid figures (Table 2).
  - *Chebyquad* is used for Fletcher (1968), because Fletcher did not run (72) (pp. 37, 39). For n = 2, 4, 6, 8, 9 this method needs 7, 14, 34, 204 and 46 calls. Fletcher needs 15–340 calls for 4 decimals and 19–838 for 6 (p. 39).
    - For n = 8 no solution exists, and the routine exits with the nearby-stationary-point message. Powell grants that Fletcher's handling is "more sophisticated" there (p. 39).
  - *Freudenstein–Roth* from (15, −2) converges to a local minimum (F = 48.98). It is chosen for the appendix as "a severer test of the method than one which yields a solution" (pp. 39–40, 45).
  - *A deliberately badly scaled system* (73), which ignores the report's own advice, needs 223 calls (Table 4, pp. 40–41). After about 30 calls Δ oscillates with period four, and rescaling avoids this (p. 42).
  - Transferable: run the case that violates your own usage advice (bad scaling), report the cost, and explain it analytically, so readers can tell an intrinsic difficulty from a bug (pp. 40–42).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Lists Barnes, Broyden, Fletcher, Haselgrove, Levenberg, Marquardt and Powell (1965), then claims "certain advantages" (p. 6).
  - Takes sides against the majority, preferring Levenberg–Marquardt to the Haselgrove approach used by most practical codes (p. 7).
  - Adopts Broyden's advice against his own earlier habit of line searches (p. 9).
  - Concedes Marquardt's stronger theorem (p. 19) and Fletcher's better no-solution behaviour (p. 39).
  - Compares on the competitor's own test problem, using the competitor's published counts (pp. 37, 39).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Organized for users first*: Section 2 gives the parameters so that a reader "who just wishes to make use of the subroutine" can skip the method (p. 9).
  - Every design statement points to Fortran instruction numbers (e.g., instructions 120, 139 and 153, pp. 18–19; instruction 242, p. 21).
  - The error messages are quoted and explained with their likely causes: DSTEP too large, programming mistakes, or rounding errors (pp. 11, 13).
  - Arbitrary choices are labelled candidly: 2n+1 "chosen intuitively" (p. 17), 0.1 "chosen empirically" (p. 23), a "rather contentious decision" (p. 16), a "tenuous" justification (p. 21).
  - The conclusion isolates the few important points: "Because this report contains so much detail" (p. 43).
  - Transferable: label every magic constant with its origin (intuition, experiment or proof requirement) in the sentence that introduces it (pp. 16–17, 21, 23).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution levels: an algorithm (the dogleg hybrid, Broyden updates and the independence device) and software (NS01A, with listing, driver and output, pp. 9, 45), plus numerical comparisons (§9). The theory is deferred to the companion paper (p. 5).
  - Limits, stated specifically:
    - The user must choose DSTEP and the scaling carefully (p. 43).
    - The routine may stop at a stationary point of F that is not a solution. Powell admits being "unable to solve the familiar difficulty of recognising whether or not a point of convergence is a global minimum" and suggests other starting points (pp. 8–9).
    - Δ oscillates on badly scaled problems (p. 42).
  - Future: assign DSTEP and the metric automatically (p. 43). Apply the Δ-controlled strategy to "many calculations that involve searching in many variable space" (p. 43).

**Method links**:
- ⚠ Method 2 (RHO vs DELTA), variant or ancestor:
  - The step bound Δ is never allowed below DSTEP, a floor justified by rounding in the differences (p. 21), and it is capped by DMAX (p. 22).
  - The reduction test uses the same 0.1 fraction of the predicted reduction as the later codes (p. 21).
  - The floor is a fixed user parameter, however, not a decreasing resolution schedule, and increases use λ-extrapolation, not a 0.7 threshold (pp. 21–22).
  - Several constants' origins are documented (0.1, 2n+1, 30°, factor 2) (pp. 17, 22–23, 25). This partly answers the Method 2 limitation about undocumented constants: they are admitted to be empirical or intuitive.
- ✅ Method 4 (cost and rounding), strong evidence:
  - The design cuts O(n³) to O(n²) per iteration (pp. 9, 19).
  - The rounding floors are DSTEP on Δ (p. 21) and refusing to use δ shorter than DSTEP to update J (p. 33).
  - The update denominator is safeguarded (p. 23).
  - The projection identity blocks accumulation of rounding error (p. 25).
  - The O(n²), rounding-stable orthonormal bookkeeping (pp. 29–30) is a named repair device, triggered by a measurable test (angle < 30°) (pp. 25–27).
- ⚠ Method 3 (cheap model updating), precursor: one function evaluation per iteration updates an n×n Jacobian model. The error decreases in the Frobenius norm (Broyden's property), which anticipates the least-Frobenius-norm updating of NEWUOA (pp. 9, 24–25). The window of 2n+1 steps (p. 17) shares its number with NPT = 2n+1, but the text draws no link.
- ✅ Method 5 (experiments with honest scope), evidence: a no-solution case (p. 39), a local-minimum case chosen as the "severer test" (p. 45), a deliberately badly scaled case (pp. 40–42), and every CALFUN call listed (p. 36).
- ✅ Method 6 (ship code), strong evidence: listing, test programme, CALFUN example and output are in the report (pp. 9, 45). The parameters are explained in user terms, with advice such as "fewer than 10*N evaluations" typical and MAXFUN set above that (p. 11).
- ✅ Method 1 (interface continuity; relax one limitation), evidence extending back to 1968:
  - NS01A(N, X, F, AJINV, DSTEP, DMAX, ACC, MAXFUN, IPRINT, W) with a user routine CALFUN(N, X, F) (pp. 10, 12) already has the MAXFUN/IPRINT/W/CALFUN pattern of the 1992–2013 codes.
  - The algorithm keeps Newton's fast convergence and removes its singular-Jacobian failure (pp. 5–6).
- ✅ Heuristic 1 (rescale before choosing steps): pp. 10–11, 40–42. ✅ Heuristic 5 (local minima → other starts): pp. 8–9.
- ⚠ Heuristic 4 (check the model before shrinking), variant: before exiting at a suspected stationary point, the Jacobian is rebuilt by differences and the test repeated (p. 35).
- ✅ Workflow B (diagnose a stalled run), evidence: each error return names its likely causes (p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate: *failure-mode entry: ask when a useful algorithm fails* (p. 5). S001 p. 1 and S050 p. 1 are similar.
- New-pattern candidate: *safeguard designed so that the proof goes through* (existing name). After a special independence step, x is not moved even if F decreased, so that the bounded-iterates theorem still applies. Powell calls this a "rather contentious decision" (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *adversarial test-case construction for one's own fast method* (existing name): the badly scaled system and the no-solution Chebyquad (pp. 39–42).
- New-pattern candidate: *benchmark on the competitor's own test problem with their published numbers* (pp. 37, 39).
- New-pattern candidate: *return the algorithm's by-products for user diagnostics*. AJINV is returned so the user can estimate the error in x and the sensitivity to changes in f (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- The dogleg rule: take Newton if it fits in Δ; else steepest descent to Δ if the Cauchy point is outside Δ; else the point on the Cauchy–Newton segment at distance Δ. It costs O(n²) given J⁻¹ (pp. 18–19).
- Enlarge a trust radius only after two consecutive successful extrapolation estimates since the last reduction, by at most ×2. This removes grow-shrink oscillation (p. 22).
- Keep an orthonormal basis ordered by the recency of the step directions, plus counts of how many recent steps span j dimensions. Insert a special step along the least-recently-explored direction when a new step is less than 30° away from the recent span (pp. 25–27).
- Put a floor on the step bound at the finite-difference step, and never update a secant model from a displacement shorter than that floor (pp. 21, 33).
- Make each error message a diagnosis: name the condition and list its likely causes in the documentation (pp. 11, 13).

**Quotes**:
- "The number (2n+1) was chosen intuitively, and numerical experiments show that the extra programming that is needed to obtain "sufficient independence" is worthwhile." (p. 17)
- "Finally we wish to state the opinion that our main strategy could provide very good algorithms for many calculations that involve searching in many variable space." (p. 43)

### S139 · On the rate of convergence of variable metric algorithms for unconstrained optimization (Proceedings of the International Congress of Mathematicians, Warszawa, pp. 1525–1539 [batch list venue: Report DAMTP, 1525-1539] 1983, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author). §5 is "joint work" with A. Griewank, and J. Stoer and Yuan Ya-xiang commented on the draft (p. 14). · **Position in trajectory**: Cambridge DAMTP (p. 15), an ICM section talk in the convergence-theory period. It uses his 1968 orthogonal-vectors construction as a computational tool (p. 5) and his 1976 global convergence result (p. 2). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–15. The key bounds (1.9)–(1.13), (2.7) and several §4 and §6 formulas are garbled in the extraction and are described, not reproduced.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Variable metric methods are "highly successful", but their good properties are proved only for convex F, and "Even in this case little is known about rates of convergence" (p. 1).
  - Existing rate results (Burmeister's n-step quadratic rate; Schuller; Ritter) need extra assumptions, notably that the matrices of normalized consecutive search directions stay bounded away from singularity. Powell says these "should be questioned", because examples where some variables never change are easy to build (p. 3).
  - The entry is therefore two-sided: find the *usual* rate numerically, then see what can be proved without the questionable assumption (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable: attack a theorem through its extra hypothesis. Build a trivial example that violates it, then ask what survives without it (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The main contribution is a research instrument, a numerical procedure for observing rates of convergence over very many iterations (abstract, p. 1; §2, pp. 4–6).
  - Take F = quadratic + Z(x), where Z is O(‖x‖³), and handle the quadratic part analytically (p. 4).
  - Update the error matrix F_k = H_k − I rather than H_k (p. 4).
  - Before each iteration, change to orthonormal coordinates whose first j axes span the j most recent search directions. This uses n−1 Givens rotations (Powell 1968), and after it the update changes only the first row and column (p. 5).
  - The first new component comes from the perfect line-search condition (p. 5). Gradients of Z are evaluated in the original coordinates, since that error is O(‖x‖³) (p. 5).
  - To defeat underflow, mantissa and exponent are stored separately, so ‖x_k‖/‖x₁‖ reached values below 10^−1000000 (pp. 5–6).
  - Beyond the instrument, the paper proves a rate that holds without Ritter's extra assumption (Theorem 1, pp. 6–8). It gives two n = 2 examples (pp. 8–10), an infinite-dimensional example (pp. 10–12) and a unit-step analysis (pp. 12–14).
  - Transferable: to study asymptotics numerically, subtract off the known part analytically, carry error quantities (H − I) rather than the quantities themselves, and rotate coordinates so each update is low-rank and local (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - F is real-valued and three times differentiable (p. 1). The iterates converge to x*, where ∇²F(x*) is positive definite (p. 3). BFGS updating is used (p. 2).
  - Line searches are perfect, or almost perfect, in §§2–5, while §6 uses unit steps (pp. 4, 12).
  - Without loss of generality, ∇²F(x*) = I and x* = 0, by the affine invariance of variable metric methods (p. 3).
  - The uniform boundedness of H_k and of the step lengths is borrowed from Dennis–Moré (pp. 6–7).
  - The pathological example is strictly convex and twice continuously differentiable. That its defining conditions are consistent is asserted ("It can be shown"), not proved (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *Lemma 1* (pp. 6–7): two asymptotic bounds, (3.1) and (3.2), on how the step δ_k interacts with later iterates and with the error matrix F_{k+i}, for 1 ≤ i ≤ n+1. The base case uses perfect-line-search orthogonality and the rank-two form (1.7). The induction uses (3.4), a step-by-step expansion of the next iterate.
  - *Theorem 1* (pp. 7–8): a continuity/compactness argument over the n+1 nonzero directions d_{k+i}, i = 0…n, in Rⁿ gives a positive constant for which an inequality holds for some pair 0 ≤ j < i ≤ n (the inequality is garbled). Lemma 1 then gives (3.8)–(3.9), and the superlinear limit (1.8) finishes the proof.
  - An attempt to sharpen the bound for larger n "was unsuccessful" because of slack in the inequalities (p. 6).
  - *§4 examples* (pp. 8–10):
    - In the first, the rate is reduced to the sine of the angle between d_{k+1} and −x_{k+1}. It is achieved by keeping the direction angles in [π/6, π/3] through a small ‖x₁‖ and a symmetric start (pp. 8–9).
    - In the second, F is reverse-engineered from a prescribed iterate pattern. The function a(·) is an infinite product that solves the functional equation (4.14), which forces the alternating iterates (4.15). The resulting convergence has Q-order only 1.5, below its R-order (the R-order value is garbled) (pp. 9–10).
  - *§5*: for a quadratic with B₁ = I and perfect line searches, the iterates solve a polynomial best-approximation problem (5.1). For a weighted L² problem on [−1, 1] whose second-derivative operator has the continuous spectrum [μ−1, μ+1], this reduces to Legendre polynomials, and bounds (5.6)–(5.7) show that the R-rate is only linear (pp. 10–12).
  - *§6*: for n = 2, a quadratic and unit steps, F_k is singular. Recurrences for an angle ψ_k and the nonzero eigenvalue λ_k ((6.6)–(6.12)) show that λ_k → 0 summably and that the angles stay away from zero. A lower bound on ‖x_{k+1}‖ follows, and with it log‖x_{k+1}‖/log‖x_k‖ → 1 (pp. 12–14).
  - Transferable: to show that a rate bound is attained, or that Q- and R-orders differ, prescribe the iterate sequence you want and solve for the objective that produces it (a functional equation), then check convexity and smoothness (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Run in Basic, without a compiler, on a TRS-80 micro-computer ("TBS-80" in the OCR), so only 2 ≤ n ≤ 4 was possible (p. 6).
  - Accuracy check: every run was repeated in single (7 decimals) and double (16 decimals) precision. The largest discrepancy was below 5 units in the sixth decimal, excluding attempts to simulate the §4 pathology (p. 6).
  - Stopping rule: stop when three consecutive iterations leave the first six digits of log‖x_{k+1}‖/log‖x_k‖ unchanged (p. 6).
  - Result: the ratio always converged to the root in [1, 2] of the polynomial (2.7) implied by Ritter's bound (1.12). Any n consecutive search directions became mutually orthogonal "to full machine precision" (p. 6).
  - §6 experiment: with unit steps the rate stayed superlinear, but the log-ratio "only tended to one" in all cases (p. 12).
  - Transferable: validate numerical evidence by rerunning it in two precisions and reporting the largest discrepancy, and define the observed rate by a stopping rule on a stabilized log-ratio (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Reviews Burmeister, Ritter and Schuller (p. 3), and Winther and Stoer for the infinite-dimensional case (pp. 10–11).
  - The abstract endorses Ritter's rate ("The usual rate seems to be the one" given by Ritter, p. 1), while Ritter's and Schuller's assumptions are challenged (p. 3).
  - Fletcher's book is recommended for details (p. 2).
  - The infinite-dimensional example is motivated by finite-element practice, since neither published case "is typical of finite element calculations" (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The abstract leads with the instrument ("A procedure is described that is suitable for investigating rates of convergence of variable metric algorithms numerically."), then states what could not be proved: "we are unable to prove" Ritter's rate except for n = 2 (p. 1).
  - Failures are reported openly ("but it was unsuccessful", p. 6).
  - A surprise is named ("It is surprising", p. 14), and a hedged suggestion follows ("Perhaps it is sometimes very valuable to replace the BFGS formula by one that gives quadratic termination without perfect line searches", p. 14).
  - The closing hope is that the procedure itself will "help further study of rates of convergence" (p. 14).
  - The sections are small self-contained items: experiments, proof, two examples, n = ∞, unit steps, discussion (§§2–7, pp. 4–14).
  - Transferable: in the abstract, list which conjectured statements you could *not* prove, next to what you did prove (p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution levels: a methodological instrument (§2), one theorem (§3), constructive examples (§4), an infinite-dimensional linear-rate example (§5) and a negative result for unit steps (§6).
  - Limits: the experiments cover n ≤ 4 only (p. 6). The gap between the observed rate (1.12) and the provable rate (1.13) remains open for n ≥ 3 (p. 14).
  - Future directions:
    - Find the least Q-order when F is infinitely differentiable and uniformly convex (p. 14).
    - Perhaps replace BFGS with a formula that terminates on quadratics without perfect line searches (p. 14).
    - Use the §2 procedure for further study (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- ⚠ Method 5 (experiment-first validation), variant: experiments come first here too, but they serve theory. The numerics establish the "usual" rate (1.12), and the proof then covers what can be proved (pp. 4–8). Scope is stated honestly: n ≤ 4, and the pathological case is excluded from the accuracy claim (p. 6).
- ✅ Method 4 (rounding error as part of the design), evidence in an experimental instrument: analytic quadratic part, error-matrix variables, orthogonal re-coordinatization, mantissa/exponent split, and a single-versus-double precision check (pp. 4–6).
- ✅ Research Taste mark 5 (theory and practice feed each other), evidence: the theory exposes that unit steps, which are practice, lose the rate that perfect line searches, the theory's assumption, deliver. It then proposes an algorithmic change, a formula with quadratic termination without perfect line searches (p. 14).
- ⚠ Research Taste warning sign 1 (know a method's failure cases), variant for theory: the paper constructs the failure cases (Q-order < R-order, linear rate in infinite dimensions, unit-step deterioration) instead of collecting them from practice (pp. 9–14).
- New-pattern candidate: *numerics → conjecture → proof or counterexample* (existing name) (pp. 4–10).
- New-pattern candidate: *construct the objective from the iterates you want* (functional-equation counterexample) (pp. 9–10). Related to the existing *adversarial test-case construction*.
- New-pattern candidate: *precision cross-check of numerical evidence* (single versus double, extended exponent) (pp. 5–6). Related to the existing *exact-arithmetic-equivalence control run*.
- New-pattern candidate: *question the extra hypothesis of a published theorem with an easy construction* (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *WLOG normalization by invariance* (affine invariance gives ∇²F(x*) = I and x* = 0) (p. 3). The S165 card has a similar device (WLOG diagonal B).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Carry only the error matrix E = H − (the known limit) in an asymptotic experiment, and treat the known quadratic part analytically (p. 4).
- Rotate to coordinates aligned with the most recent search directions, using n−1 Givens rotations, so that the rank-two update touches one row and column (p. 5).
- Store mantissa and exponent separately to follow a superlinear sequence far past floating-point underflow (pp. 5–6).
- Use a compactness argument on n+1 directions in Rⁿ to extract a uniform constant for some pair, and combine it with step-wise asymptotic lemmas to get a rate (pp. 7–8).
- For a quadratic with B₁ = I and exact line searches, rewrite the iteration as polynomial best approximation and bound it with orthogonal polynomials (pp. 10–12).

### S050 · A recursive quadratic programming algorithm that uses differentiable exact penalty functions (Mathematical Programming 1986, DOI 10.1007/bf01580880)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Y. Yuan (Ya-xiang Yuan). Both authors give DAMTP Cambridge as their address (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: The SQP (recursive quadratic programming) period of the 1970s–80s, which follows Powell's own 1978 SQP papers [14, 15, 16], the watchdog technique [6] and his 1985 test-problem study [17] (pp. 1, 13–14). Received December 1984 (p. 1). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–14, including the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The problem is equality-constrained minimization, min f(x) subject to c(x) = 0, with f and c twice continuously differentiable (p. 1).
  - The entry is a known bottleneck. SQP converges superlinearly if unit steps are eventually accepted [15], and several papers study how to choose the line-search function so that this happens (Chamberlain et al., Gill et al., Schittkowski) (p. 1).
  - Fletcher's differentiable exact penalty function is a suitable choice [3, 4], but its gradient needs second derivatives of f and c (pp. 1–2).
  - Transferable: find the one computational obstacle, here second derivatives, that keeps a theoretically attractive tool (a differentiable exact penalty) out of practical algorithms, and remove only that (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Key idea*: along the trial step, replace the multiplier function λ(x_k + αβd_k) by linear interpolation between λ(x_k) and λ(x_k + βd_k). This gives a line-search function (2.3) whose initial derivative (2.4) needs only first derivatives. The cost is one λ evaluation for each trial step length (p. 2).
  - *Kept*: the QP direction from linearized constraints (2.5)–(2.6) (p. 3), Armijo-type acceptance (2.11) with μ in (0, ½) (p. 3), and Powell's damped BFGS technique [14], adopted "Notwithstanding its disadvantages" (p. 10).
  - *Penalty parameter*: σ is kept whenever the descent condition (2.7) holds. Otherwise it is at least doubled by (2.8), so it either stays the same or at least doubles (2.9)–(2.10) (p. 3). Lemma 2.1 shows that (2.8) never divides by zero (p. 4).
  - *Backtracking*: the next step length comes from a quadratic interpolation of Φ(0), Φ′(0) and Φ(β), clipped to [0.05β, 0.5β] (p. 10).
  - *The Hessian estimate's gradient difference y_k* drops the change in ∇λ, which involves second derivatives and is multiplied by constraint values that tend to zero (p. 10).
  - Transferable: when a merit function's derivative contains a term you cannot afford, approximate that term by a difference along the current trial step, and check that it is multiplied by something that vanishes at the solution (pp. 2, 12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Condition 3.1* (p. 4):
    - (i) x_k, d_k and B_k are bounded;
    - (ii) A(x) has full column rank everywhere;
    - (iii) B_k is uniformly positive definite on the null space of A_kᵀ.
  - Each QP is assumed to have a unique solution (p. 3).
  - Local theory adds Condition 4.1, x_k → x*, and assumes that the full steps x_k + d_k converge superlinearly. Conditions on B_k that give this are cited, not proved (Boggs–Tolle–Wang; Powell) (pp. 8–9).
  - Admitted: Condition 3.1(iii) "imposes a severe restriction on the matrices" (p. 13). Only equality constraints are treated (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *Lemma 2.1* (p. 4), by contradiction using QP optimality: if c_k = 0 and (2.7) fails, then either d = 0 or a large multiple of d_k would give a better QP value.
  - *Lemma 3.2* (pp. 4–5), sufficient descent Φ′(0) ≤ −η‖d_k‖². Split d_k = e_k + h_k into range-space and null-space parts, with ‖e_k‖ ≤ M₀‖c_k‖ and curvature ≥ δ on h_k. The two cases ‖e_k‖ ≥ ε‖h_k‖ and < ε‖h_k‖ use the two halves of (2.7). η is given explicitly in (3.6).
  - *Lemma 3.3* (pp. 5–6): backtracking is finite. Either σ → ∞, which is impossible because only one bounded term depends on i, or σ is eventually constant and a Taylor expansion applies.
  - *Lemma 3.4* (pp. 6–7): σ is eventually constant. The key bound (3.14) compares the QP multipliers λ̄_k with the least-squares multipliers λ(x_k), which gives O(‖d_k‖‖c_k‖).
  - *Lemma 3.5* (pp. 7–8): d_k → 0. Once σ is fixed, Φ(x_k, σ) is a monotone merit sequence, and step lengths are bounded below, so each step with ‖d_k‖ > ε decreases it by a fixed amount.
  - *Theorem 3.6* (p. 8): accumulation points are stationary.
  - *Lemma 4.2 and Theorem 4.3* (pp. 8–9), no Maratos effect: a trapezoid-type expansion f(x+d) = f(x) + ½dᵀ[g(x) + g(x+d)] + o(‖d‖²) shows Φ(1) − Φ(0) = ½Φ′(0) + o(‖d‖²), so μ < ½ accepts the unit step.
  - Transferable: to prove that an adaptive penalty parameter settles, show that the descent test holds automatically once σ exceeds a threshold computed from the problem's constants, and pair this with a *stay or at least double* update rule (pp. 3, 6–7).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Parameters*: β₁ = 0.05, β₂ = 0.5, μ = 0.1, initial σ = 10, B₁ = I, "because these values seem suitable, but there was no empirical tuning of parameters" (p. 9).
  - *Test set*: Powell's own random generator [17], with a nonlinearity parameter ρ controlling the constraint curvature. n = 4, x₁ = (1, 1, 1, 1). Five random cases × m = 1…4 × ρ = 0.01, 0.1, 1 gives 60 problems. x = 0 is a solution, but other solutions and local minima can occur (pp. 10–11).
  - *Setup*: IBM 3081 in double precision. Termination by a very tight test (5.9) on ‖d‖² + ‖c‖², or after ten unsuccessful function evaluations in one line search (p. 11).
  - *Reported quantity*: function and gradient evaluations (with iterations) in Table 1, from 4 to 92 per problem. Flags mark error returns and runs converging to a non-zero limit (p. 11; the second flag is rendered as a comma in the extraction).
  - σ stayed at 10 in 38 of 60 runs and exceeded 1000 in 3 runs, all with nearly dependent constraint gradients. Unit steps were accepted on most iterations (p. 11).
  - *Failure diagnosis*: two error returns are rounding (final test values 5×10⁻¹² and 36×10⁻¹²). Two converge to a point with singular A_k and large violations, where the final ‖d_k‖ is 5×10⁶ and 1×10⁶, so ten step reductions are too few. One inefficiency comes from near-dependent constraint gradients (pp. 11–12).
  - There is no head-to-head comparison with other codes. The counts are only said to be "similar" to Powell [17] (p. 11).
  - Transferable: for each failed run, give the terminal numbers (test value, ‖d‖) that assign it to a cause (rounding, singular constraint Jacobian, near-dependence) (pp. 11–12).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Places the work in the SQP lineage: Wilson, Biggs, Han, Powell, Schittkowski (p. 1).
  - Adopts Fletcher's function and credits Bertsekas for its suitability (pp. 1–2).
  - Defends the technique against the singular-Jacobian failures, which are "probably not due to our technique" because Newton with exact line searches can converge to non-solutions (p. 12).
  - Weighs the ℓ₁ alternative (minimize the QP objective plus weighted moduli of linearized violations) and suggests that one might keep that direction choice with this line search (p. 12).
  - Criticizes Di Pillo–Grippo's use of a differentiable function for directions, because it loses "the highly useful sharp restrictions" of the linearized constraints (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The Discussion opens with a one-sentence purpose statement (p. 12).
  - The lemma chain is sign-posted ("Our next lemma shows", "We use this lemma to show", pp. 6–7).
  - Status is disclosed: "no empirical tuning" (p. 9); decisions that "deserve careful investigation, but we only chose an estimate that seemed suitable" (p. 10); program details "provisional" (p. 12).
  - The Maratos-effect claim is stated flatly as proved (p. 12).
  - Transferable: disclose the tuning status of every parameter set used in experiments. Saying there was *no empirical tuning* makes favourable results more credible (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution levels: algorithmic (a differentiable exact-penalty line search without second derivatives) and theoretical (global convergence; unit steps accepted near a superlinearly convergent solution), plus modest numerics (pp. 12–13).
  - Limits: equality constraints only; line search only; Condition 3.1(iii) is severe; the program is provisional (pp. 12–13).
  - Future: inequality constraints; trust regions, which "may allow the removal of Condition 3.1(iii)"; and research on these questions, which "would be valuable" (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Heuristic 6 (model constraints individually; keep the merit function only for accepting steps), strong evidence: each constraint is linearized in the QP (2.6) (p. 3), the penalty function is used only as a line-search function (p. 2), and the Discussion argues for keeping the "sharp restrictions" of linearized constraints when choosing directions (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Approximate the directional change of the multiplier function by linear interpolation between x_k and the trial point. This gives a line-search function with an exact initial derivative that needs only first derivatives (p. 2).
- In a sufficient-descent lemma for SQP, split d into range-space and null-space parts, and handle the two cases (range part large or small) with the two halves of a combined descent test (pp. 4–5).
- Show that the unit step is accepted (no Maratos effect) with the trapezoid expansion Φ(1) − Φ(0) = ½Φ′(0) + o(‖d‖²) and an Armijo constant μ < ½ (pp. 8–9).
- Choose the next trial step by minimizing the quadratic that interpolates Φ(0), Φ′(0) and Φ(β), clipped to [β₁β, β₂β] (p. 10).
