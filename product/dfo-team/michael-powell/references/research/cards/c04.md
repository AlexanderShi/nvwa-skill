# Paper cards · batch c04 (core)

Batch intent: See Powell in 2007–2010, between the NEWUOA release (2006) and the BOBYQA release (2009), working in four registers on one family of methods. S029 is an invited-lecture essay that gives his own view of derivative-free optimization and states his research philosophy. S036 is a work-in-progress paper that publishes design experiments (five versions of the *alternative* step, three values of m) before a release decision is made. S007 is the BOBYQA design record, which adds the RESCUE device. S129 is a pure convergence theorem for gradient-based trust-region methods, written to cover a wide range of methods at once. The questions are: how experiments drive design decisions (and how undecided or chaotic results are reported), how he places theory against practice in the same years, and which research moves the six Methods of SKILL.md do not yet cover.
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all four papers are core). Extra weight on D2 (what is kept and what is changed along NEWUOA → BOBYQA), D4 (proof devices of S129; derivation devices of S007), D5 (experiment protocol: identical random numbers, permutation cases, ranges of #F) and D7 (candour, hedging, first-person voice).
Material roles: S029 core · S036 core · S007 core · S129 core.

### S029 · A view of algorithms for optimization without derivatives (Mathematics Today – Bulletin of the IMA 2007, no DOI/arXiv; text is report DAMTP 2007/NA03)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: the written version of a William Benter Distinguished Lecture given at City University of Hong Kong on 7 February 2007 and written up there (p. 1 footnote; p. 11). It comes after NEWUOA (2006) and before the BOBYQA report (2009), and is his own retrospective on the line COBYLA → UOBYQA → NEWUOA (pp. 3, 9–11). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–12, including the 11 references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry is an observation about practice: most unconstrained calculations in practice use no derivatives (p. 1). Attention is restricted to methods that suit noisy functions and that change the variables in non-random ways (p. 1).
  - The personal motive is told as a story. IMSL made his TOLMIN package use difference approximations because its customers preferred derivative-free methods. He was unhappy about it, and also about the popularity of simulated annealing and genetic algorithms, which make many decisions randomly instead of exploiting precise function values. This gave him strong motivation to build better algorithms (p. 2).
  - The first algorithm came from an application: a four-variable constrained problem from Westland Helicopters led to COBYLA (p. 3).
  - Transferable: open a methods essay with the concrete episode that made the research question urgent (a vendor forcing a worse method, a client's 4-variable problem), then restrict scope explicitly (noisy F, non-random methods) (pp. 1–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: This is a survey organized as a chain of failures, each shown on a small example and each answered by the next method.
  - *Original simplex method* (Spendley–Hext–Himsworth 1962): reflection formula (1). A contraction far from the solution wastes about 200 iterations on F = x₁² + 100x₂² with the explicit simplex (2) (pp. 3–4).
  - *Nelder–Mead*: the extra point (3) avoids that inefficiency (pp. 4–5), but McKinnon's example makes it converge to a non-stationary point (pp. 5–6).
  - *COBYLA*: linear interpolation on a simplex plus a trust region. The step (10) minimizes the linear model in the ball. The vertex to drop is chosen mainly to keep the simplex volume away from zero (p. 6).
  - *Quadratic models*: COBYLA's linear model fails on example (13), a well-conditioned simplex far from the solution whose model step points directly away from the solution. The remedy is curvature, via a quadratic model and the trust-region subproblem (15) (p. 9).
  - *UOBYQA*: m = (n+1)(n+2)/2 interpolation points, and the replaced point is chosen so that |ℓ_t(x̂)| is large (Lagrange function). It still changes only one point per iteration, the feature kept from COBYLA (pp. 9–10).
  - *NEWUOA*: m = 2n+1. The freedom is taken up by minimizing the Frobenius norm of the change to ∇²Q, which he calls analogous to the symmetric Broyden method. Two features justify it: an O(m²) update through form (17), and the projection identity (18) for quadratic F (p. 10). He had not expected NEWUOA to need fewer F values than UOBYQA (p. 10).
  - Transferable: present your method as the last link of a chain in which each predecessor fails on a small, explicit example (pp. 4, 5–6, 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Pattern-search theory: bounded level sets, continuously differentiable F, exact arithmetic and infinitely many iterations (p. 7).
  - Noise is handled by design, not by assumption. The lower bound ρ on Δ exists because ∇L becomes misleading when vertices are too close and F has errors (p. 6).
  - For COBYLA, no convergence assumptions are offered at all. He cannot give a favourable answer within the usual conventions (p. 8).
  - The philosophy is to design on contrived problems, often quadratic F, seeking good performance there in a way that allows general F (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *Lattice argument*: for n = 2, the vertices of the original simplex method before a contraction are integer combinations αy¹ + βy² of the first simplex's vertices. No vertex is visited twice, and bounded level sets force another contraction. He calls this argument fundamental to the later pattern-search methods (p. 4).
  - *McKinnon reconstruction*: the repeated inside contraction is written as a three-term linear recurrence (4). Its eigen-solution ((1 ± √33)/8)^k (5) shows that the simplex collapses onto the ξ-axis. Inequalities (8)–(9) then verify the ordering conditions (6) on the convex piecewise-quadratic function (7) (pp. 5–6).
  - *Pattern-search convergence sketch*: the set K of iterations that reduce Δ is infinite, because each grid admits only finitely many strict reductions. Either the x^(k), k ∈ K, have infinitely many distinct values, or one repeated x* has ∇F(x*) = 0. In both cases lim inf ‖∇F‖ = 0 (pp. 7–8).
  - *Finite-precision counting*: at most about 10²¹ distinct floating-point values of F exist, fewer than the lattice points of the unit cube for n = 20 and Δ = 0.1. He offers this as a second example of a theoretical bound on work that is not useful (p. 8).
  - *Projection identity* (18) for quadratic F: ‖∇²Q_new − ∇²F‖² = ‖∇²Q_old − ∇²F‖² − ‖∇²Q_new − ∇²Q_old‖² (p. 10).
  - Transferable: reconstruct a published counterexample as a linear recurrence with a closed-form solution, then check the needed inequalities with explicit constants (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Setup*: one table. NEWUOA on the trigonometric sum of squares (19): random integer S, C in [−100, 100], σ_j in [1, 10], and a planted minimizer x* with F(x*) = 0. The start has weighted offsets random in [−π/10, π/10]. ρ_beg = 0.1 and ρ_end = 10⁻⁶. There are five random cases for each n = 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320 (pp. 10–11).
  - *Metric*: #F. All final ‖x − x*‖∞ are below 6×10⁻⁶. Growth of #F is no faster than linear in n (p. 11).
  - There are no baselines and no failures reported.
  - *Observation*: S036 reports the same described experiment as its Table 1 (S036 p. 6) with different counts (for example n = 10, Case 1: 373 here, 370 there) and a different error bound (6×10⁻⁶ here, 1.5×10⁻⁵ there). Neither paper comments, and the cause is not stated.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Against popular practice*: Nelder–Mead was reported to him in the 1970s as the most used method, and simulated annealing now seems to be first. Users should therefore attend to McKinnon's examples (p. 5).
  - *Generous to pattern search*: Kolda, Lewis and Torczon (2003) is praised as a mine of information (p. 8). Pattern-search mesh reduction is compared with COBYLA's ρ reduction as the same idea, keeping evaluation points apart (p. 8).
  - He is open that pattern search has guaranteed Δ decreases where COBYLA may not decrease ρ (p. 8).
  - *References*: 11 in total, 5 of them his own work (including Fletcher–Powell) (p. 12). McKinnon is spelled MacKinnon in the text (pp. 3, 5) but McKinnon in the references (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - First person and autobiographical: Harwell 1962, HSL, the Apollo 11 anecdote about DFP, and a frank aside that the IMSL episode made him unhappy (p. 2).
  - The research philosophy is stated in the introduction (p. 2).
  - Every section turns on a two-variable worked example with explicit coordinates (pp. 4, 5, 9).
  - Strong words for his own result (his favourite example; a stunning success) sit beside a plain admission of ignorance about how pattern-search efficiency depends on n (pp. 8, 10–11).
  - The close offers free copies of four packages by e-mail (p. 11).
  - Transferable: in an invited essay, state your methodological creed in one sentence in the introduction and let each later section illustrate it (pp. 2, 9–11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: an expository and position essay with no new algorithm. The headline claim is that least values of functions of more than 100 variables can be computed (p. 1).
  - *Limits, stated concretely*: no convergence theory for COBYLA (p. 8); no practical experience of pattern search versus n (p. 8); UOBYQA's O(n⁴) work stops n above about 100 in practice (p. 10).
  - *Future*: no future directions are given beyond NEWUOA (not stated).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Method 1 (relax one limitation per solver): the lineage is explained limitation by limitation. The linear model ignores curvature, answered by quadratic models (p. 9). O(n⁴) work limits UOBYQA, answered by m = 2n+1 in NEWUOA, which superseded it (p. 10). One point changed per iteration is kept throughout (p. 9).
- ✅ Method 2 (two-radius discipline): COBYLA's lower bound ρ on Δ is never increased and is reduced only when it seems to block progress. Before ρ is reduced, a far vertex may be moved by an *alternative iteration* that maximizes simplex volume. The rationale is noise: ∇L misleads when vertices are too close (p. 6). This is the same logic as pattern-search mesh reduction (p. 8).
- ✅ Method 3 (fewer evaluations than parameters): m = 2n+1 with least Frobenius change; #F grows at most linearly although Q has O(n²) parameters (pp. 10–11).
- ⚠ Research Taste mark 2 / Heuristic 7 (UOBYQA limited to n ≤ 20), variant: here Powell puts UOBYQA's practical limit at about n = 100 (p. 10), not 20. SKILL.md's figure comes from the 2002 abstract. The two may measure different things (promise versus feasibility); not resolved here.
- ✅ Research Taste warning sign 1 (methods recommended without knowing failure cases): the McKinnon discussion is confirmed in the full text, including the reconstruction (pp. 5–6).
- ✅ Method 6 (free code): all four packages are free, and HSL distribution was unrestricted (pp. 2, 11).
- ⚠ Heuristic 9 (say so when published numbers differ), possible tension: the Table 1 counts differ from S036's Table 1 for the same described experiment, with no comment in either paper (p. 11; S036 p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: tiny counterexample for every mechanism (already in pool from c01). Here: simplex contraction far away (p. 4), McKinnon (pp. 5–6), COBYLA's linear step pointing away from the solution (p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate: design for the quadratic case in a way that admits general F. It is stated as how he built several useful algorithms (p. 2) and realized in the projection argument (18) (p. 10).
- New-pattern candidate: critique the questions theory asks (already in pool). Here the finite-precision count shows that a finiteness bound on work is useless (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before you introduce a method, show its predecessor failing on a 2-variable quadratic with explicit starting vertices, and count the iterations wasted (about 200 here) (pp. 4, 9).
- Turn a known counterexample for a direct-search method into a linear recurrence and give its closed-form solution, so readers can check it by hand (pp. 5–6).
- When a convergence argument rests on finiteness (grids, floating-point values), put a number on the finite bound to show whether it means anything in practice (p. 8).
- Use a random problem family with a planted minimizer (trigonometric sum of squares, 5 instances for each n, n doubling from 10 to 320) to test how #F scales with n (pp. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "It is very helpful, however, to try to solve some particular problems well, in order to receive guidance from numerical results, and in order not to be misled from efficiency in practice by a desire to prove convergence theorems." (p. 2)
- "Therefore, when asked about the convergence properties of COBYLA, I am unable to give a favourable answer within the usual conventions of convergence theory." (p. 8)

### S036 · Developments of NEWUOA for minimization without derivatives (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2008, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drm047; text is report DAMTP 2007/NA05, revised November 2007)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: written for the IMA J. Numer. Anal. Special Issue for his 70th birthday. It was deliberately published before the release decision on BOBYQA's *alternative* step had been made (p. 19). It records the 2006–2007 BOBYQA prototype (begun in 2006 and presented at the Beijing conference in September 2006, p. 7), and S007 (2009) later cites its tables (S007 pp. 3, 12, 33–34). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–19, including both references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry is a success. NEWUOA solves dense 320-variable problems with #F growing no faster than linearly (§1, p. 6), and this encouraged an extension to simple bounds (p. 6).
  - The second entry is a surprise inside that extension. A cheaper approximate solution of the bound-constrained *alternative* subproblem (Version 2) was much more efficient (pp. 10–11). That raised the question whether the same simplification helps without constraints (§3, p. 11).
  - A third question is the choice of m (§4, p. 16).
  - The abstract advertises a surprising case: at n = 160, reducing m from 2n+1 to n+6 reduces the number of iterations (p. 1).
  - Transferable: when a simplification wins unexpectedly in the constrained setting, go back and test it in the unconstrained parent method (pp. 10–11).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *NEWUOA summary* (pp. 2–5):
    - There are two iteration types. *Trust region* steps come from an extended truncated CG. A step shorter than ρ/2 is abandoned (pp. 2–3).
    - *Alternative* steps move the interpolation point furthest from x^(k), and only if it is more than 2Δ away. They maximize |Λ_t| in the ball (1.9)–(1.11) (pp. 3–4).
    - The model change is fixed by an (m+n+1) linear system (1.12)–(1.15) whose inverse is stored and updated in O((m+n)²) operations. A factorization of the leading m×m block limits the loss of accuracy (p. 5).
    - The Lagrange-function coefficients sit in the t-th column of that inverse (p. 5).
  - *BOBYQA prototype, kept*: most features are copied from NEWUOA: both iteration types, the Δ and ρ rules, the storage and updating, and the choice of t (p. 7).
  - *BOBYQA prototype, changed*: the subproblems gain bounds (2.1)–(2.2). The start is shifted so that all initial points are feasible, using b_i ≥ a_i + 2ρ_beg (pp. 6–7).
    - *Trust-region step*: components pushed against an active bound by the gradient sign are fixed at zero. A free component that hits a bound is fixed there and CG restarts with one fewer variable. Each change costs O(mn), and there are rarely more than ten changes (pp. 7–8).
    - *Alternative step*: Version 1 (d_full) starts from d_cd and improves it by CG and 2-D boundary searches. Version 2 uses d_cd itself (pp. 8–10). d_cd is the exact maximizer of |Λ_t| over n+1 lines: the coordinate directions plus the line through the point y_t to be dropped (2.3).
  - *Key move*: exact optimization over a cheap restricted set can beat approximate optimization over the full ball. d_cd is found cheaply and exactly in O(mn) + O(n) operations, because a quadratic on a line needs only the gradient at x^(k) plus one extra functional (p. 9). The reason it helps (coordinate steps stay on active bounds) was found only afterwards (p. 10).
  - *Five variants for the unconstrained case*: d_full, d_cd, d_ip, d_ip+ and d_ip++ (pp. 11–14).
    - d_ip is the exact maximizer on the m−1 lines through the other interpolation points. It is convenient for linear constraints, because segments between feasible points stay feasible (pp. 12–13).
    - d_ip+ adds a Cauchy-point safeguard. It falls back to the gradient step (3.3) unless |Λ_t(x + d_ip)| ≥ c_ip·‖∇Λ_t‖·Δ (3.2), with c_ip = 0.1. d_ip++ uses c_ip = 0.5 (pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable: when a subproblem is solved only roughly anyway, try solving a restricted version exactly (on n+1 or m−1 lines) and compare end-to-end #F, not subproblem quality (pp. 9–12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The problems are unconstrained, or have simple bounds with b_i ≥ a_i + 2ρ_beg (pp. 6–7).
  - The component-fixing rule is argued from first-order optimality of a differentiable F (p. 7).
  - The degeneracy worry about d_ip (points drifting into an affine subspace, which later points inherit) is called hypothetical (p. 13).
  - There are no convergence assumptions and no theorems. Conclusions about permutations must come from experiments, because the permutations are invisible in exact arithmetic (pp. 18–19).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *Variational reading of the update*: ∇²Q^(k+1) is a least-squares projection of ∇²Q^(k). For quadratic F the errors ‖∇²Q − ∇²F‖_F therefore decrease monotonically and the changes tend to zero, which he offers as a possible reason for NEWUOA's good convergence (p. 5).
  - *Reuse of stored data*: the Lagrange function needed by the geometry step is a column of the stored inverse, so no extra solve is needed (p. 5).
  - *Cost counting*: ∇Λ_t(x) and the diagonal of ∇²Λ_t in O(mn), then n+1 line maximizations in O(n) (p. 9).
  - *Cheap lower bound*: because Λ_t is quadratic and zero at x^(k), a multiple of its gradient reaches at least ‖∇Λ_t‖·Δ. This gives test (3.2) (p. 13).
  - *Invariance as an instrument*: permuting the variables changes nothing in exact arithmetic, so the spread of #F across permutations measures rounding effects (pp. 14, 18–19).
- **D5 Experiments** (the core of the paper):
  - *Problems*:
    - Trigonometric sum of squares (1.16), with the same data and random numbers throughout (pp. 6, 12).
    - Points in the unit square (2.5)–(2.8), with random starts subject to a separation condition, ρ_beg = 10⁻² and ρ_end = 10⁻⁶. Its many local minima are deliberately ignored and accuracy is measured by KKT residuals (pp. 9–10).
    - ARWHEAD (3.4), with 5 cases per n made by random permutations of the variables (p. 14).
    - CHROSEN (3.5) with permutations (p. 15), and with random log-uniform starts in [0.5, 2] (pp. 15–16).
  - *Sizes and metric*: n ∈ {10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320}. The metric is #F, and final ‖x − x*‖∞ or KKT residuals are always reported.
  - *Tables 1–11*: Table 2 gives Version 1 and Table 3 Version 2 on the points problem, with major gains except some n = 20 cases (pp. 10–11). Table 4 shows d_cd better for n ≤ 40 but worse in the n = 160 and 320 rows (p. 12). d_ip+ with c_ip = 0.1 beats Table 1 in 25 of 30 cases, and c_ip = 0.5 improves on it in only 7 of 30 (p. 14).
  - *Chaos reported openly*:
    - The ARWHEAD and CHROSEN rankings reverse between problems and between n (pp. 14–15). He calls the major differences unreasonable (p. 15).
    - d_ip's success in four CHROSEN cases is left as an unexplained mystery (p. 15).
    - On ARWHEAD with m = n+6 and n = 320, d_ip gives the lowest ranges of #F, and he calls it a hero, although it was highly inefficient in Tables 6 and 8 (p. 18).
    - Local-minimum convergence is marked by a superscript ℓ (p. 15).
    - d_ip is excluded from some ranges, marked ∗, to keep them fair, and this is stated (p. 17).
  - *m study*: m = n+6, 1.5n+1 and 2n+1. On the trigonometric problem, smaller m increases #F, by roughly 3× at n = 320 (Table 9, p. 17). On CHROSEN with random starts, m = n+6 is the fastest for each n (Table 10, p. 18). On ARWHEAD at n = 160, #F falls as m falls (Table 11, p. 18).
  - Transferable: compare design variants on identical data and identical random numbers, and report the min–max range of #F over five cases with the final accuracy (pp. 12, 17).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - There are only two references: Dennis & Schnabel (1983) §9.1, for least-Frobenius updating when derivatives are available, and his own NEWUOA paper (pp. 5, 19).
  - There is no comparison with other DFO software. Every comparison is against his own previous version (Table 1 as the d_full baseline, p. 11).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Section 1 is titled after a success, then problems follow.
  - The paper is explicit about being incomplete (p. 19) and calls the explanation for Version 2 wisdom after the event (p. 10).
  - There is humour: the name BOBYQA caused amusement at the Beijing 70th-birthday conference (p. 7).
  - Table captions follow one template, the d∗ version applied to problem (u.v) (pp. 11–12).
  - Conclusions are hedged (suggest, may, seem).
  - He lists his release criteria for the *alternative* step before choosing one: it should adapt to linear constraints, be as easy to compute as the exact line solutions, be insensitive to orthogonal rotations, and combine good accuracy with low #F (p. 19).
  - Transferable: when you publish undecided design work, end with the explicit criteria the final choice must satisfy (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: an experimental design record, stated to be preliminary. It may assist the development of new software (p. 1).
  - *Limits*: details are strongly influenced by rounding (p. 1). Permutation effects cannot be analysed theoretically (pp. 18–19). The work is incomplete (p. 19). The best m may depend strongly on F (p. 19).
  - *Future*: pick one *alternative* step before releasing BOBYQA; investigate m further; let users of BOBYQA choose m, so the software must avoid the d_ip-type inefficiencies (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Method 1: BOBYQA copies most of NEWUOA and changes only what bounds require (p. 7).
- ✅ Method 3 and Heuristic 2 (start with 2n+1, also try n+6): the evidence is mixed and problem-dependent. n+6 wins on CHROSEN and ARWHEAD (pp. 17–18) but loses by about 3× on the trigonometric problem (p. 17). Powell concludes that the best m may depend strongly on F (p. 19).
- ✅ Method 4 (rounding as part of correctness): origin shifts and a factored inverse block (pp. 4–5); permutation cases used as a rounding probe. The final accuracy is called a triumph for BOBYQA's recovery from rounding errors (pp. 14, 18–19).
- ✅ Method 5 (experiment-first, honest scope): chaotic and unexplained results are printed rather than averaged away (pp. 15, 18). ⚠ Variant: here experiments are used to *choose between design variants before release*, and the paper is published while the choice is still open (p. 19). Method 5 describes validation of a finished method.
- ✅ Heuristic 3 (bound interval ≥ 2·RHOBEG): the condition b_i ≥ a_i + 2ρ_beg appears already in the prototype (pp. 6–7).
- New-pattern candidate: publish preliminary results with explicit status (already in pool from c02). Evidence p. 19.
- New-pattern candidate: explain a surprise with a designed follow-up experiment (already in pool). Version 2's surprise is tested in the unconstrained case, running BOBYQA with bounds ±10¹⁰ (p. 11).
- New-pattern candidate: restrict the subproblem to a few cheap lines and solve it exactly (pp. 8–10, 12–13).
- New-pattern candidate: exact-arithmetic-invariant perturbations (variable permutations) as a rounding-sensitivity probe (pp. 14, 18–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Test a design change for the unconstrained parent method inside the constrained code by setting bounds to ±10¹⁰, so there is only one code base (p. 11).
- Build the cases of a deterministic test problem as random permutations of the variables. Any spread in #F is then rounding sensitivity, not problem difficulty (p. 14).
- Guard a cheap heuristic step with a one-line comparison against an analytic lower bound (here c·‖∇Λ_t‖·Δ), and fall back to the Cauchy-type step if it fails (p. 13).
- Mark local-minimum runs in tables with a symbol, and state openly when a variant's numbers are excluded from a range and why (pp. 15, 17).
- Before releasing, write down the selection criteria for the undecided component (constraint-friendliness, cost, rotation invariance, accuracy, #F) (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "The last remark shows wisdom after the event that was accumulated from several numerical experiments." (p. 10)
- "I hope the reader finds that the information in the given numerical results is sufficiently interesting to compensate for the lack of completeness in the work that is presented." (p. 19)

### S007 · The BOBYQA algorithm for bound constrained optimization without derivatives (Cambridge NA Report NA2009/06, University of Cambridge 2009, no DOI/arXiv; text is report DAMTP 2009/NA06, August 2009)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: the release report for BOBYQA, the bound-constrained successor of NEWUOA. It settles the open choices of S036: the *alternative* step becomes line maximization plus a Cauchy-step safeguard (p. 12), and m remains the user's choice (p. 4). It adds the RESCUE device (pp. 20–25). Its future-work items (p. 39) point to later model-update research (cf. S108, card c05). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–39, including §§2 and 4–5 (closed-form initial inverse, update formulae, RESCUE) and the 5 references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The problem is min F(x) subject to a ≤ x ≤ b, where F is a black box evaluated only at feasible x (pp. 1–2).
  - The entry is to carry NEWUOA's excellent efficiency for large n over to problems with simple bounds (p. 3).
  - Behind it is an unexplained empirical fact: with m = 2n+1, #F is often of order n or less (p. 3).
  - Transferable: define the new solver's purpose as porting a measured strength of its predecessor to a wider problem class (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Kept from NEWUOA*: fixed m; least-Frobenius-change updates via the (m+n+1) system; the two iteration types; the ρ lower bound on Δ; origin shifts; the ρ-reduction formula (6.6) and the ρ-termination test (pp. 3, 27–30, 32).
  - *Changed or added* (in the order of the report):
    1. *Preliminary calculations*: x₀ is moved onto a bound or at least Δ₁ inside it. Points on a bound use +Δ₁ and +2Δ₁ along that coordinate (2.2), and there is an error return if b_i < a_i + 2Δ₁ (2.1). The initial inverse H and its factor Z are given in closed form (2.11)–(2.15), and their verification is left as an exercise for the reader (pp. 4–8).
    2. *TRSBOX*: an active-set truncated CG from d = 0. Indices are only ever added to the active set. It stops if the remaining gain bound ‖P_I g‖Δ ≤ 0.01 × the reduction so far (3.4). It then rotates d around the trust-region boundary in the plane of P_I(d) and P_I(∇Q), with θ ≤ π/4 (3.5)–(3.6). It rarely makes more than ten changes to d, within the O(n²) target (pp. 9–11).
    3. *ALTMOV*: t is the point furthest from x_k (3.7). d is chosen on the m−1 lines through x_k and the other interpolation points. It maximizes (3.11), which uses the analytic bound (3.10) on β instead of computing β (p. 12). A constrained Cauchy step c_k replaces d_k if it gives a larger denominator (3.12), to resist the drift of the points into a subspace when bounds stay active (pp. 12–13). The LP (3.13) is solved by growing a subset S in at most n−1 iterations of O(n) each (p. 13).
    4. *RESCUE*: it is called when σ ≤ ½τ² (5.2), for example after rounding makes σ too small or negative. It discards H and Z, shifts the origin to x_k, and builds exact matrices for a fresh coordinate stencil γ^j (5.4)–(5.5). It then swaps old points back one by one where safe, choosing t to maximize σ and requiring σ̂ > 0.01 × the best alternative (5.7). F is evaluated only at genuinely new points (pp. 20–25). NEWUOA instead tolerated a signed factorization Z S Zᵀ (p. 4).
    5. *Other features*: the Δ update (6.4) uses ratio thresholds 0.1 and 0.7 and snaps Δ to ρ when it falls to 1.5ρ or below (p. 28). The ρ-reduction test uses ε_max, the largest |F − Q| of the last three evaluations, compared with the curvature along the CG directions (6.10) and with a bound-direction test (6.11) (pp. 30–31). The model is replaced by the minimum-Frobenius-norm model Q_alt if (6.12) holds on three consecutive trust-region iterations (pp. 31–32). The origin is shifted when ‖d_k‖² ≤ 10⁻³‖x_k − x₀‖² (6.13) (p. 32).
  - *Why the model update exists at all*: its original reason was cost, O(n²) instead of O(n⁴) per iteration. The large saving in #F was not expected (p. 3).
  - Transferable: give each numerical safeguard its own named routine and trigger condition (TRSBOX, ALTMOV, RESCUE; test (5.2)), so a reader can see which symptom invokes which repair (pp. 9, 13, 21, 25–26).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Only simple bounds are handled, with b_i ≥ a_i + 2Δ₁ (p. 5). F is evaluated only at feasible points (p. 2).
  - The descriptions assume exact arithmetic with the origin at 0 (p. 9), but §5 and the shift rule (6.13) treat rounding explicitly (pp. 20, 32).
  - There is no convergence theory, and the report says convergence theory had nothing to do with NEWUOA's success (p. 3).
  - For quadratic F, the Hessian error decreases monotonically (1.7). Yet at termination ‖∇²F − ∇²Q_k‖_F still exceeds ½‖∇²F‖_F while x is accurate to 10⁻⁶ relative (p. 3, citing Powell 2009, to be published).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *KKT structure of the least-change problem*: ∇²Q_{k+1} − ∇²Q_k = Σ λ_ℓ ŷ_ℓ ŷ_ℓᵀ with Σλ = 0 and Σλŷ = 0 (4.1)–(4.2). This gives the (m+n+1) system (pp. 14–15).
  - *Implicit Hessian*: ∇²Q = M + Σ μ_ℓ (y_ℓ − x₀)(y_ℓ − x₀)ᵀ (4.6) keeps the update O(m²) and a product ∇²Q·v at O(mn) (p. 15).
  - *Inverse update*: (4.9)–(4.11) has the single denominator σ = αβ + τ². The identity Hv = e_s (4.13) lets the code drop the (m+1)-th row and column of H (pp. 16–17). Z is updated by first rotating it so that only its first column changes (4.14) (p. 17).
  - *Proof of the β bound (3.10)*: the parameters do not depend on x₀, so put the origin at x_k. Eliminate Υ and Ξ using WH = I (4.22)–(4.23). This gives β = α²(1−α)²{½‖y_j − x_k‖⁴ − e_jᵀAΩAe_j} ≤ ½α²(1−α)²‖y_j − x_k‖⁴, since Ω = ZZᵀ is positive semidefinite (pp. 19–20).
  - *Positivity of the RESCUE threshold*: Lagrange functions reproduce linear polynomials (5.8)–(5.9), so some e_jᵀHw is nonzero (pp. 23–24).
  - *Rounding argument*: if x₀ is far from x_k, the terms of β cancel at the scale ‖x_k − x₀‖⁸·H_ij. This eighth power justifies the shift rule (6.13) (p. 32).
  - *Why a ρ-exit test is needed*: by the triangle inequality, very successful models can make every trust-region step shorter than ½ρ, so the method would stall without an explicit test for giving up the current ρ (p. 30).
  - Transferable: when a quantity in a selection rule is expensive (β for every candidate j), prove a cheap upper bound along the candidate family and select with the bound (pp. 12, 19–20).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Problem 1*: the trigonometric sum of squares (7.1), with bounds of ±10⁶⁰ so they are irrelevant, and the same random numbers as S036 (p. 33).
    - The runs use n = 10–320, 5 cases each, and m ∈ {2n+1, n+6, (n+1)(n+2)/2} (Tables 1–3, pp. 34–35).
    - Each row reports the range of #F, the worst ‖x_f − x*‖∞, the average number of origin shifts, the average CPU seconds (DTIME) and the workspace (p. 34).
    - The #F ranges agree closely with NEWUOA's ranges in S036 (p. 34), and the reason for remaining differences is stated: the new *alternative* step (p. 33).
    - The full quadratic model m = (n+1)(n+2)/2 stops at n = 80, because the workspace is O(n⁴) and n = 160 would take about 3 days (p. 34).
  - *Problem 2*: points in square (7.3)–(7.4). Its many local minima are argued analytically (pp. 35–36).
    - The runs use n = 20–160, three random starts, and two starts perturbed by about 10⁻⁶ (cases e1, b1) to probe sensitivity, with ρ_end ∈ {10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁸} (p. 36).
    - Accuracy is measured by a relative projected gradient ǧ (7.7)–(7.8) (p. 37).
    - To tell distinct local minima from rounding noise, he compares the runs with ρ_end = 10⁻⁶ and 10⁻⁸. The changes in F are at most 0.000004, far below the gaps between runs, so the four close values 6863.9, 6864.0, 6870.0 and 6870.1 belong to four different local minima (p. 38).
  - *Outcome*: RESCUE was never called in Tables 1–7 (p. 38). He suggests that rounding errors may contribute strongly to the wide #F ranges in Tables 4–5, by analogy with NEWUOA before the ZZᵀ factorization was introduced (pp. 38–39). m = 2n+1 beating n+6 is usual but not general (p. 34). Powell calls the average #F in Tables 6–7 a triumph for m = n+6 on the points problem, perhaps because second-derivative information helps less when there are many local minima (p. 37). BOBYQA shows no clear winner against NEWUOA on unconstrained problems (p. 39).
  - Transferable: to decide whether different final F values are different local minima or rounding noise, rerun with a 100× smaller final tolerance. If the change in each run is far smaller than the differences between runs, they are distinct minima (p. 38).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - He claims novelty narrowly: NEWUOA and BOBYQA are the only methods known to him that combine quadratic models with least-Frobenius-change updates (p. 3).
  - He challenges a stated view directly. NEWUOA and BOBYQA are presented as a counterexample to Gould and Toint (2004) on the importance of theoretical insight (p. 3).
  - Conn, Scheinberg and Vicente (2009) is called interesting, but it does not address high accuracy with #F much below O(n²) (p. 3).
  - *References*: 5, of which 3 are his own (p. 39).
  - *Self-correction across papers*: here he writes that ∇²Q_k cannot be expected to approximate ∇²F(x*) well, because #F is far too small (p. 3). S029 (pp. 10–11) and S036 (p. 5) had suggested improving Hessian approximations as the reason for NEWUOA's success.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - It is a design record keyed to the Fortran names (TRSBOX, ALTMOV, RESCUE, BMAT, ZMAT) (pp. 9, 13, 17, 21).
  - Constants are justified as empirical. The 0.01 in (5.7) seems suitable in numerical experiments (p. 23), and the 0.1 in (6.12) provides some reluctance (p. 32).
  - He corrects a simplification in the text: ½Δ_k should read ½ρ_k (p. 28).
  - The extracted text of (6.6) reads 0.1ρ_end in its third case (p. 29). This conflicts with the usual decrease ρ_{k+1} = 0.1ρ_k stated on p. 27, so it is probably a typo in the report (SKILL.md's code reading gives RHO/10).
  - The release decision is told candidly (p. 39).
  - Transferable: when a constant is empirical, say so in the sentence that introduces it (pp. 23, 32).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: algorithm and software (free by e-mail, p. 39), with the new RESCUE device. It finds local minima of problems with hundreds of bounded variables (p. 39).
  - *Limits*: no clear gain over NEWUOA without bounds (p. 39). RESCUE costs O(m³) (p. 25). There is a very rare error return (p. 26). Rounding may widen the #F ranges (p. 38). The best m is problem-dependent (p. 34).
  - *Future*: automatic adjustment of m, and an update that minimizes the Frobenius change plus a term with first-derivative changes (p. 39). Research on these is not needed urgently (p. 39).

### S129 · On the convergence of a wide range of trust region methods for unconstrained optimization (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 30(1):289–301, 2010, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drp021; text is report DAMTP 2008/NA06, May 2008)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author); dedicated to Ron Mitchell (p. 1 footnote) · **Position in trajectory**: written in May 2008, between S036 and S007. It is gradient-based theory, not derivative-free. It covers the symmetric Broyden (least-Frobenius-change) update, the with-derivatives analogue of the NEWUOA/BOBYQA model update (pp. 5–6; S029 p. 10). The family-of-methods framing reappears in his later derivative-free convergence work (S093, card c05). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–16, including the 6 references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry is fragmentation. Nearly every trust-region method has its own convergence proof, and he wants a single theorem for a wide range of them (p. 2).
  - The range is chosen to admit his own practice. x_{k+1} = x_k + s_k is allowed whenever F decreases at all, so F(x_k) is always the least value computed so far. He calls this a strong preference of his, and notes that most published methods require a sufficiently large ratio first (p. 2).
  - A second gap: the analyses of safeguarded SR1 assume linearly independent steps (Conn–Gould–Toint 1991) or bounded B_k (Byrd–Khalfan–Schnabel 1996). He calls both assumptions questionable (p. 6).
  - Transferable: pick the theorem's scope so that it covers the choices practitioners actually make (any-decrease acceptance, unbounded B_k), and say so in the introduction (pp. 2, 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The method class is defined by inequalities, not by an algorithm.
  - s_k is any step with ‖s_k‖ ≤ Δ_k and Q_k(s_k) ≤ Q_k(ŝ_k), where ŝ_k is the Cauchy step (2.1). A weaker θ-version (2.2) is noted and set aside (p. 3).
  - Δ_{k+1} lies in [α‖ŝ_k‖, βΔ_k] if ρ_k < ρ*, and in [‖ŝ_k‖, max(Δ*, γΔ_k)] otherwise (2.8) (p. 4).
  - x_{k+1} is any point with F(x_{k+1}) ≤ F(x_k) if ρ_k < ρ*, and F(x_{k+1}) ≤ F(x_k + s_k) otherwise (2.9). This drops the usual constraint ‖x_{k+1} − x_k‖ ≤ Δ_k (p. 5).
  - B_k is allowed to grow linearly: ‖B_k‖ ≤ λ + μk (2.10) (p. 5).
  - *Result*: ‖∇F(x_k)‖ ≤ ε is reached after finitely many iterations for every ε > 0, which is lim inf = 0 (pp. 2–3).
  - The growth condition admits symmetric Broyden, via ‖B_{k+1}‖ ≤ ‖B_k‖ + 3Λ (2.13), and admits safeguarded SR1 under weaker conditions than before (pp. 5–6).
  - Transferable: define the admissible algorithm class by one inequality per decision (step, radius, next iterate, matrix), each justified by what existing codes do (pp. 3–5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - F is bounded below, and ∇F is Lipschitz *only on the level set* L = {x : F(x) ≤ F(x₁)} (1.6) (p. 3). For symmetric Broyden, L is enlarged to contain the segments [x_k, x_k + s_k] (p. 5).
  - Arithmetic is exact and ε > 0 (pp. 3, 11).
  - Simplifications he admits: θ = 1 (p. 3); Euclidean norms, with other norms sketched in §4 (pp. 3, 14–15).
  - He also admits that the upper bound on Δ_{k+1} is important to the analysis (p. 13).
  - The level-set Lipschitz condition is presented as a gain. It allows barrier functions of non-convex feasible regions, unlike conditions on the convex hull of L (Conn–Gould–Toint 2000, AF3) (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *Cauchy decrease* (2.6): F − Q ≥ ½‖g‖·min(Δ, ‖g‖/‖B‖), rederived from the scalar quadratic φ(σ) (p. 4).
  - *Lemma 1*: Δ_k ≤ (1 − ρ*)‖g‖/(Λ + ‖B_k‖) implies ρ_k ≥ ρ*. Because the Lipschitz condition holds only on L, he first takes τ̂ as the largest step fraction that keeps F below F(x_k), then proves τ̂ = 1 (pp. 7–8).
  - *Lemma 2*: by induction, Δ_k ≥ δ/(M + μk), and each successful iteration reduces F by at least ½ρ*δε/(M + μk) (3.11)–(3.12) (pp. 8–9).
  - *Theorem, by contradiction through counting*:
    - Sandwich Δ_{ℓ+1} between the lower bound and β^{ℓ−q(ℓ)}(η(M + μℓ))^{q(ℓ)}Δ₁ (3.22).
    - Take logarithms to get q(ℓ) ≥ 0.9·log(1/β)·ℓ/log ℓ successful iterations (3.26).
    - Group the successes into dyadic blocks Q_j (3.27), and bound the total decrease below by a harmonic series Σ 1/((M + 2μ)j), which diverges (3.33).
    - This contradicts F being bounded below (pp. 9–11).
  - *Sharpness example*: a 2-variable function u(x) + v(y), with v in (4.2) chosen so that y cycles through 0, 3, −5. With models (4.4), radii 4, 10, 6 and α = ½, β = ⅔, γ = 5/2, every step decreases F, yet two subsequences of gradient norms tend to 1. So lim inf cannot be replaced by lim under any-decrease acceptance (pp. 11–13).
  - *Non-Euclidean regions*: N_k is sandwiched between balls of radius ωΔ and ΩΔ (4.9), which costs Ω² factors in Lemma 1 (4.10) (pp. 14–15).
  - *Worst-case count*: the iterations in [2^j, 2^{j+1}) guarantee a total reduction of only order ε²/j, so up to about 2^22000 iterations may be needed (p. 15).
  - Transferable: to prove lim inf with growing B_k, turn the bounds on Δ into a count of successful iterations (at least order ℓ/log ℓ), then sum decreases over dyadic blocks to get a harmonic lower bound (pp. 9–11).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (theory paper).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Against Conn–Gould–Toint (2000): their basic algorithm has no upper bound on Δ after a success, but needs uniformly bounded B_k. They allow a relative radius change as small as desired, while (2.8) does not, which agrees with most implementations (pp. 5, 13–14).
  - Against Powell (1984): the same growth condition (2.10), but with much stronger conditions on s_k, x_{k+1} and Δ_{k+1} (p. 5).
  - SR1 analyses are called questionable (p. 6).
  - He recommends Nocedal & Wright, chapter 4, as an introduction (p. 3).
  - Yuan read a draft and suggested using ‖s_k‖ in (2.8). Powell turns this into a stated open problem, conditions (4.8), which may provide some challenging analysis (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - He calls it our ubiquitous convergence theorem (p. 3).
  - Section 2 is devoted to the reasons for the generality, with one practical justification per condition. For example, every implementation known to him bounds Δ_{k+1} (p. 4).
  - After the proof, he builds an example that shows the limit of the conclusion (pp. 11–13).
  - He is candid about the weakness of the bound, calling the iteration count monstrous (p. 15). He argues the theorem's use as removing the need to search for counterexamples, and as a first step for analysing any single efficient method (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: theory. One termination theorem covers any-decrease acceptance, linearly growing ‖B_k‖, a Lipschitz condition on the level set only, and arbitrary x_{k+1} subject to decrease conditions. Safeguarded SR1 is covered under weaker conditions (pp. 2, 5–6).
  - *Limits*: only lim inf, which the example shows is sharp (pp. 11–13); a monstrous worst-case count (p. 15); non-Euclidean norms only sketched, which he says seems to be sufficient (p. 15); the θ-generality omitted (p. 3).
  - *Future*: prove the theorem under Yuan's weaker radius conditions (4.8) (p. 14), and use the theorem as a starting point for analysing a single efficient trust-region method (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace a bounded-B_k assumption by ‖B_k‖ ≤ λ + μk, and show that your favourite update meets it with a one-line triangle-inequality bound (2.13) (pp. 5–6).
- If the Lipschitz condition holds only on the level set, work with the largest step fraction τ̂ that stays in the level set, then prove τ̂ = 1 (p. 7).
- To prove lim inf: bound Δ_k below by δ/(M + μk); sandwich Δ to count successes (at least order ℓ/log ℓ); sum decreases over dyadic blocks into a divergent harmonic series (pp. 9–11).
- After the main theorem, build a 2-variable example in which every hypothesis holds but the stronger conclusion (lim) fails (pp. 11–13).
- Compute the worst-case iteration count your proof implies and state it plainly, even if it is 2^22000 (p. 15).
