# Paper cards · batch c01 (core)

Batch intent: See how Powell, in 1997–2001, did two things. (a) He defended a reliable but "expensive" trust-region subproblem solver (Moré–Sorensen) by re-engineering its linear algebra (S165, S148). (b) In a survey (S014) and a toolkit paper (S068), he wrote down the ideas that later became UOBYQA/NEWUOA: two trust-region radii, geometry control by Lagrange functions, and an error bound that decides when ρ may be reduced. The question is where the habits behind SKILL.md Methods 1–5 were first argued in print, and which of his research moves the six Methods do not yet cover.
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all four papers are core). Extra weight on D2 (design moves: what was kept and what was changed), D4 (proof and counting devices) and D6 (how he positions himself against popular alternatives).
Material roles: S165 core · S014 core · S148 core · S068 core.

Page references use the `[[page N]]` markers of the extracted texts. All four texts are DAMTP report preprints, so the markers do not give journal pagination. The extractions drop Greek letters and the fi/ff/fl ligatures. Formulas below are therefore paraphrased and never quoted, and each quote was chosen from a passage without dropped characters.

### S165 · Trust region calculations revisited (Numerical Analysis 1997 [proceedings vol. 380] 1997, no DOI/arXiv; text is report DAMTP 1997/NA18)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: Part of a 1997 side-track on dense quasi-Newton trust-region linear algebra, just before the derivative-free quadratic-model period. It is the Dundee conference (advocacy) version of S148: its Table 1 is "copied from Powell (1997)" (p. 17), and that table is S148's Table 3. **Read**: full text, pp. 1–20, including the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a community belief, not a new problem. The truncated CG method (Steihaug 1983) is "usually preferred", and the preference is "becoming stronger" because many researchers think the Moré–Sorensen (MS) method costs too much (p. 3). Powell attacks both halves of that belief.
  - *Quality*: in a 2-variable example with Δ = 1, truncated CG gains only 2×10⁻⁶, while the optimal step gains more than 1.0. This happens because B is ill-conditioned and CG "has become the method of steepest descents" (p. 3).
  - *Cost*: he reviews the MS details to show that few cycles are needed, and brings in his own band-matrix result (pp. 1, 3).
  - Transferable: name the stated reason a community prefers method A to method B (for example, that B costs too much). Then refute that reason with a two-line example plus a cost analysis.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Kept*: the MS guarantee (1.3) that the model reduction is within a factor (1−ε) of optimal (p. 2), and what he calls the "brilliant feature" of MS. MS stops adjusting λ once d(λ) *allows the construction* of an acceptable d, not when d(λ) itself is acceptable (p. 10).
  - *Changed*: the λ iteration. The first trial aims at a slight over-estimate, λ_L + ‖g‖/Δ. Newton–Raphson supplies a lower bound on λ* and false position an upper bound. The next λ is a biased 0.9/0.1 combination of these (2.20) (pp. 8–10). Powell says explicitly that this choice departs from the one stated by Moré and Sorensen (p. 7).
  - *Added for quasi-Newton B*: an orthogonal change of variables Ω that makes ΩᵀBΩ banded with bandwidth 2s+1. Each Cholesky factorization then costs O(ns²), and s ≈ n^{2/3} gives O(n^{7/3}) per iteration, including updating Ω and M by Givens rotations after a rank-one or rank-two change (pp. 12–16). Storing the rotations in product form lowers this further to O(n^{11/5}) (pp. 19–20).
  - Transferable: if a subproblem solver is rejected as too costly, look for a change of variables that preserves the solver's invariant and makes its inner linear algebra cheap. Here orthogonal Ω preserves Euclidean lengths and hence the trust region (p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The Euclidean norm is used throughout. Powell admits it is "a highly useful choice that loses generality" (p. 2).
  - B is symmetric and may be indefinite (p. 2).
  - §3 requires a B with no useful sparsity that is revised by a rank-one or rank-two quasi-Newton formula (pp. 12–13).
  - The cost argument assumes that only a few trial values of λ occur per iteration. MS report an average of 1.6 (p. 12).
  - If B+λI is found to be exactly singular, there is no special code: Powell judges it "highly unlikely in practice" that this happens, and simply adjusts λ again (p. 8). The hard case in general is handled through the choice of z (pp. 11–12).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - The characterization theorem is given with an outline proof, via the Lagrangian Q(d) + ½λ(‖d‖² − Δ²) and a negative-curvature perturbation d* + αs (pp. 4–5).
  - B is taken to be diagonal, which loses nothing because an orthogonal similarity preserves Euclidean lengths and hence the trust-region bound (p. 5).
  - The secular function φ(λ) = 1/Δ − 1/‖d(λ)‖ is shown to be convex and decreasing, with a derivative bounded away from zero and infinity, via Cauchy–Schwarz (2.7)–(2.9) (p. 6).
  - φ′ comes from the same Cholesky factor, φ′ = ‖ŷ‖²/‖d‖³ (2.17) (p. 9).
  - The MS termination test (2.21) follows from an exact identity for Q(d) + ½λ‖d‖² about its minimizer (2.22)–(2.25) (pp. 10–11).
  - For the hard case, z is taken from the near-null space of LLᵀ, and a Rayleigh quotient raises λ_L (pp. 11–12).
  - Givens rotations are counted, O(n²/(s+1)) with bound (3.11) (pp. 14–16).
  - Balancing the terms of O(n² + n²t/s + ns² + n³/t) gives s ≈ n^{3/5} and t ≈ n^{4/5} (p. 20).
  - Transferable: to make a Newton root-finder robust, change what you solve for (1/‖d‖ rather than ‖d‖) until the function is convex with a bounded derivative. Then bracket the root with Newton on one side and false position on the other (pp. 6, 9).
  - Transferable: if the cost depends on free integers (s, t), write the average cost as a sum of monomials and choose each integer as a power of n that equalizes the dominant terms (p. 20).
- **D5 Experiments**: There are no optimization runs. The one timing table is copied from S148: "band" versus "full" seconds on a Sun 4 workstation for n = 50 … 800. The ratio is 1.43 at n = 50 and 10.85 at n = 800, and the table is used to estimate the constant c ≈ 0.12 in the c·n^{2/3} saving (p. 17). The MS average of 1.6 trial λ values is cited, not re-measured (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - He continues and defends Moré–Sorensen (1983), and contradicts the preference for Steihaug (1983) directly with the counterexample (p. 3).
  - Gould, Rendl–Wolkowicz and Sorensen (all 1997) get one sentence (p. 3).
  - At the end he is fair to the other side. He reports Yuan's (1997) conjecture that truncated CG achieves (1.3) with ε = ½ when B is positive definite, and concedes that line-search quasi-Newton methods already need only O(n²) work (p. 19).
  - He thanks Moré for comments on the draft of Sections 1–2 (p. 20).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The thesis is stated as an argument in the abstract: the MS estimate "should be employed more often" (p. 1).
  - The counterexample comes in the introduction (p. 3).
  - The Discussion turns to what convergence theory asks. It lists three standard questions (limit points, rate of convergence, efficiency on quadratics) and gives an objection to each (pp. 17–18).
  - Claims are low-key: "debatable" (p. 17), "It is hoped" (p. 20).
  - Transferable: end a technical paper with a section that says which question your result answers and which standard theoretical questions it does not address, and why those questions are not enough (pp. 17–18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: an exposition of MS with Powell's own λ-update refinements (pp. 7–10), plus the band-matrix complexity reduction (pp. 12–16).
  - *Limits, stated concretely*: the practical importance is "debatable" (p. 17). The case for MS is weaker for positive definite quasi-Newton matrices used with line searches (p. 19).
  - *Future*: he hopes the result will help "the future development of software" (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Method 4 (engineer cost as part of the algorithm), evidence: the paper cuts per-iteration flops from O(n³) to O(n^{7/3}) or O(n^{11/5}), so that the better-quality subproblem solution becomes affordable (pp. 1, 12–17, 19–20).
- ✅ Research Taste mark 2 (per-iteration work must still scale), evidence: the saving factor grows with n (p. 17).
- ✅ Method 2 (two-radius discipline), evidence for its rationale. He writes that in trust-region methods "one should try to avoid small values of the radius" Δ while the variables are far from a minimum. On example (1.4), a poor step would force Δ below about 0.001 (p. 18).
- ⚠ Heuristic 7 (redesign above O(n³)), variant: here the redesign is a change of coordinates for the linear algebra, not a change of model (pp. 12–13).
- New-pattern candidate: tiny counterexample against a popular default. p. 3 (also S148 p. 5; S014 pp. 4, 17–18).
- New-pattern candidate: critique the questions theory asks. pp. 17–18 (also S014 p. 42).
- New-pattern candidate: advocacy rewrite of one's own report for a broader audience. This paper repackages S148 for a conference audience (p. 17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a 2-variable, ill-conditioned counterexample in which a popular heuristic gets a model reduction 10⁶ times smaller than the exact solution does (p. 3).
- Reparametrize a secular equation (1/‖d(λ)‖) so that Newton's method has a convex target with a bounded derivative, and take the derivative from the same factorization (pp. 6, 9).
- Safeguard Newton with a false-position bound and a biased 0.9/0.1 combination, so that both ends of the bracket are replaced in turn (pp. 9–10).
- Report the hidden constant (c ≈ 0.12) measured from timings, not just the exponent (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "The importance of the given work to practical applications is debatable, partly because current methods of theoretical analysis have many inadequacies." (p. 17)
- "Therefore we take the view that one should try to develop algorithms that provide favourable answers to the questions, but that the questions do not go far enough." (p. 18)

### S014 · Direct search algorithms for optimization calculations (Acta Numerica 1998, DOI 10.1017/s0962492900002841; text is the DAMTP 1998/NA04 preprint)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none · **Position in trajectory**: The survey that sits between COBYLA (1994) and UOBYQA (2002). It is the first written record of the two-radius idea and of geometry control by Lagrange functions, and Powell says that recording them is one of its purposes (pp. 34–35). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–49, including §8 (simulated annealing) and the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - *Practice*: IMSL wrapped his gradient-based codes with finite differences because customers would not supply derivatives. User demand was "met in an imperfect way that was usually adequate" (p. 2).
  - *Objections to differences*: loss of accuracy from cancellation, a tight cluster of n+1 values per gradient, and amplified noise. Spread-out points are better (p. 2).
  - *Organizing principle*: there is no key idea except the problem itself, so the paper is "a collection of essays on particular strategies and algorithms" (abstract, p. 1). Its stated aim is to show that "there are still many opportunities for improvements" (p. 2).
  - *A flagged gap*: penalty and barrier methods lose the precision that explicit constraints give, so derivative-free methods for nonlinear constraints "could be important" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a field has no organizing theorem, organize the survey by mechanism (line search, grids, simplices, conjugate directions, linear and quadratic models, random search). For each mechanism, study advantages, limitations and theory rather than listing references (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: There is no single algorithm, but several design moves, some of them new.
  - *(a) Two radii for the linear-model method.* This is the COBYLA lineage, rebuilt with two radii:
    - ρ_k is a lower bound on Δ_k, is never increased, and sets the acceptable edge length of the simplex.
    - Δ_k ≥ ρ_k is the step bound. It may grow after successful minimization steps, and after a failure the suggestion is Δ_{k+1} = max[ρ_k, ½‖step‖] (pp. 26–27).
    - The reason for two radii: when ρ_k is reduced, the optimum can lie at a distance of order M·ρ_k, where M is the condition number of ∇²F, so steps much longer than ρ_k are sometimes needed (p. 27).
    - Nine priority rules decide between a "minimization step" and a "simplex step", and when ρ must be reduced (p. 28).
    - The idea is credited to his student Evan Jones (pp. 34–35).
  - *(b) Two causes of failure.* When a model step fails, he separates two causes: the step was too long (so shrink Δ), or the interpolation set is poor (so improve the simplex) (p. 25).
  - *(c) Constraints.* Each constraint gets its own linear model. If the linearized constraints are inconsistent, the step minimizes the greatest linearized violation (p. 24). The merit function Φ = F + μ[max_p c_p]₊ is used only to rank points, and μ is doubled when needed (pp. 29–30).
  - *(d) Quadratic version.* Here a "simplex step" maximizes |ℓ_ℓ(x)| within the trust region. The reason: replacing v_ℓ by x multiplies the determinant of the interpolation system by ℓ_ℓ(x) (7.6), and this does not depend on the basis. The step costs two trust-region subproblems, for +ℓ_ℓ and −ℓ_ℓ (p. 32). The point to drop is scored by |ℓ_i| weighted by its distance, capped at 1 (6.13), (7.8) (pp. 29, 34).
  - *(e) Suggestions for future work*:
    - line-search parameters that adjust themselves like trust-region radii (9.1) (p. 43);
    - Nelder–Mead generalized to value-dependent weights on a grid (9.3) (p. 44);
    - quadratic models with the Hessian sparsity of a partially separable F, following Toint's suggestion (9.5)–(9.6) (p. 46).
  - Transferable: when a model step fails, diagnose which of two causes it was (a step that is too long, or a degenerate sample set). Give each cause its own remedy and its own type of step (p. 25).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The assumptions change by section.
  - Convergence results assume F continuously differentiable with bounded level sets, in exact arithmetic (pp. 4–5, 10).
  - The discrete-grid theory needs a rational mesh ratio γ (pp. 10–11).
  - The simplex analysis takes every simplex to be regular, without loss of generality (p. 13).
  - Simulated annealing assumes a finite state set and a single eigenvalue of modulus one (p. 38).
  - The termination argument of §6 assumes that only finitely many iterations change x_k, which he justifies by the finite precision of computer arithmetic (p. 45).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *(i) Coordinate search.* A counterexample (Powell 1973) with exact line searches along coordinate directions: the iterates cycle round six edges of a cube (p. 4). The remedy is to force ‖x_{k+1} − x_k‖ → 0 with a sufficient-decrease condition (2.9). The proof is by contradiction at a limit point, using uniform continuity (pp. 4–7).
  - *(ii) Grid finiteness* (Torczon 1997). Every iterate differs from x₁ by integer multiples of one positive constant, so a bounded level set allows only finitely many decreases (pp. 10–11). He then computes that constant for realistic parameters, 2.93×10⁻²⁰, which is below machine precision (p. 11).
  - *(iii) Affine invariance.* The simplex method commutes with an affine change of variables, so every simplex may be taken to be regular (pp. 12–13).
  - *(iv) A number-theory counterexample.* For n ≥ 3, reflections about a fixed edge never repeat, because cos θ = 1/3 and the leading coefficient of cos(kθ), as a polynomial in cos θ, is 2^{k−1}, which is not divisible by 3 (p. 14).
  - *(v) Simplex termination.* An o(Δ) averaging argument shows that termination of the simplex method implies a small gradient (pp. 15–16).
  - *(vi) Conjugacy.* The "natural measure" of linear independence is greatest if and only if the directions are mutually conjugate. The proof rotates two non-conjugate directions by π/4 (p. 22).
  - *(vii) Lagrange functions.* A Lagrange function is the determinant ratio for replacing an interpolation point (p. 32).
  - *(viii) Simulated annealing.* A Markov-chain stationary-vector analysis (p. 38). The cooling rate T_k ~ 1/log k follows from the divergence of Σ exp(−δ/T_k) (pp. 41–42). A 4-state counterexample shows annealing preferring a non-global state when the sparsity of W is asymmetric (pp. 39–40).
  - *(ix) Termination of the two-radius linear method.* The proof is by contradiction: otherwise the simplex volume would become unbounded. The proof is also used to justify each rule (pp. 45–46).
  - Transferable: after proving convergence by a finite-grid argument, compute the grid constant for realistic parameters and compare it with machine precision (p. 11).
  - Transferable: prove that your set of rules terminates, and use the proof to justify each proviso. He writes that without the last proviso of rule (4), "lack of termination would be prevalent" (p. 46).
- **D5 Experiments**: The paper reports no new experiments.
  - Jones "investigated numerically" whether a second radius reduces the number of iterations, and "usually some improvements occur" (p. 34). Powell showed "some preliminary numerical results" at the 1996 SIAM conference (p. 35).
  - He cites Elster–Neumaier's comparisons with Nelder–Mead and with a finite-difference quasi-Newton method (p. 36).
  - On COBYLA's origin: it was written for Westland Helicopters, for problems with about 4 variables and 10 constraints. The program is "very slow, as expected, when there are no constraints" (p. 45).
- **D6 Positioning**: This is an opinionated survey.
  - *Nelder–Mead*: criticized through its own failure examples and McKinnon (1997) (pp. 17–18), and again in the first person (p. 44).
  - *Simulated annealing*: gets a full analytical section (pp. 36–42) but is criticized for its random decisions (p. 47).
  - *Grid theory*: both praised and criticized. He credits Torczon's (1997) advantage that any strict decrease is acceptable (p. 11), then gives example (9.2), where restricting to grids impairs efficiency even for a quadratic (p. 44).
  - *Conn–Scheinberg–Toint*: he notes their "generous acknowledgement" of his talks, and compares their single radius, which is allowed to increase, with Jones' two radii (p. 35).
  - *His own work*: self-critical about his 1964 conjugate-direction method (pp. 44–45).
  - Transferable: in a survey, give each rival method its strongest theorem and its cleanest failure example, then state your own opinion separately and in the first person (pp. 17–18, 44, 47).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Each section follows the same form: define the method, give a convergence argument, then give a counterexample or limitation. The Discussion (§9) goes back over every section from the angle of theory versus practice (pp. 42–47).
  - The tone is candid and in the first person: "I have never liked the simplex methods of Section 4" and "I was surprised" (p. 44); "My opinion of simulated annealing" (p. 47).
  - Open questions are admitted: "The author does not know the answer" (p. 14).
  - He states a purpose that is not in the title: recording unpublished work (p. 35).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contributions*:
    - a survey organized by mechanism;
    - the first written record of the two-radius and Lagrange-function techniques (pp. 26–35);
    - small new results: termination of the linear method (pp. 45–46), and a restatement of line-search convergence in "a result that is not well-known" (p. 43).
  - *Future directions*, listed concretely:
    - derivative-free methods for nonlinear constraints (p. 3);
    - line-search parameters that adjust themselves (p. 43);
    - simplices on grids with value-weighted reflections (p. 44);
    - convergence of conjugate-direction methods (p. 45);
    - sparse quadratic models, and linear constraints "for a while" before general ones (p. 46);
    - global methods that do not decide at random (p. 47).
  - *Open limits*: whether the Spendley–Hext–Himsworth method always shrinks for n ≥ 3 in exact arithmetic is unknown (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Method 2 (RHO vs DELTA), strong evidence. The ρ/Δ split is written out here (pp. 26–28):
  - ρ_k is a lower bound on Δ_k and is never increased;
  - ρ_k is reduced only when rules (5) or (6) show that it blocks progress;
  - Δ may grow after successful steps.

  The idea is credited to Evan Jones (pp. 34–35). Some constants are given with their roles: β = 2.1 marks a vertex as "far", γ = 1/4 tests near-degeneracy, and Δ_{k+1} = max[ρ_k, ½‖step‖] follows a failed step (pp. 26–27). This partly answers the Method 2 limitation that the constants' rationale is undocumented.
- ⚠ Method 2, variant of the limitation: the SKILL says that a monotone RHO is ill-suited to noise. Here a large ρ₁ is recommended because it "can alleviate the damage from any random noise in the function values" (p. 27).
- ✅ Method 1 (relax one limitation), evidence: §7 is derived from §6 by replacing the linear polynomial with a quadratic one. It keeps ρ, Δ and the step rules (pp. 31–32), and is generalized to any finite-dimensional linear space of models (p. 46).
- ✅ Method 4 (cost and rounding), evidence: geometry repair is a separate type of step, triggered by measurable tests (β, γ) (pp. 25–26, 33). Rounding is part of the analysis: the grid constant is compared with machine precision (p. 11), and a shrink is guaranteed "in practice" because computer arithmetic is finite (p. 14).
- ✅ Method 5 (experiment-first, honest scope), evidence: algorithms should come from ideas that "may provide improvements to actual calculations", and "it is important to study what can go wrong" (p. 42). An open question is admitted (p. 14).
- ✅ Method 6 (ship free code), evidence: "An easy-to-use Fortran program was written that implements the algorithm of Powell (1994), and it is available from the author." (p. 45)
- ⚠ Method 3 (fewer evaluations than parameters), variant: he proposes cutting model parameters through known Hessian sparsity rather than least-Frobenius updating (p. 46). He also notes that Elster–Neumaier fit only about 2n+2 values to a model whose Hessian shape is fixed in advance (pp. 35–36).
- ✅ Heuristic 6 (model each constraint separately), evidence: pp. 3, 24, 29–30.
- ✅ Warning sign 1 (Nelder–Mead failure cases), evidence: pp. 17–18, 44.
- New-pattern candidate: tiny counterexample for every mechanism. pp. 4, 9–10, 14, 17–18, 39–40, 44.
- New-pattern candidate: survey or talk as the vehicle for recording unpublished (student) work. pp. 34–35.
- New-pattern candidate: sanity-check proof constants against machine precision. p. 11.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Structure a survey as one essay per mechanism. Each essay gives the algorithm, the best convergence argument and the cleanest failure example. End with a discussion that revisits every section (pp. 1, 42–47).
- Separate *step too long* from *sample set degenerate*, and give the two different remedies (p. 25).
- Pick geometry-improving points by maximizing the Lagrange function of the point to be replaced. Its value is a basis-independent determinant ratio (p. 32).
- When choosing a point to drop, multiply each candidate's determinant ratio by its distance relative to Δ, so that far points leave first (pp. 29, 34).
- Compute the actual constant that a convergence proof relies on, and compare it with machine precision (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore the author has always supported the view that new algorithms should be developed from ideas for techniques that may provide improvements to actual calculations." (p. 42)
- "Therefore one of the reasons for the present contribution to Acta Numerica is to catch up on the recording of this work." (p. 35)

### S148 · The use of band matrices for second derivative approximations in trust region algorithms (Advances in Nonlinear Programming 1998, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4613-3335-7_1; text is report DAMTP 1997/NA12)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none · **Position in trajectory**: The July 1997 technical report behind S165. It is the last of Powell's dense quasi-Newton linear-algebra work before the derivative-free interpolation codes. **Read**: full text, pp. 1–24, including the lower-bound argument of §2 and the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an idea he had carried for years: "the author has had the following idea in mind for several years" (p. 3). The idea is to solve (B+λI)d = −g in a rotated basis where ΩᵀBΩ is tridiagonal, so that each trial λ costs O(n) (pp. 3–4). The paper tests the idea and reports two failures before it works:
  - Forming Ω costs O(n³). This pays off only if there are at least 10 trial values of λ, but practice needs far fewer (Table 1, p. 6), typically two (p. 3).
  - Updating a tridiagonal form after a rank-one change needs about n²/4 rotations, so the update is still O(n³) (pp. 7–13).

  He therefore relaxes tridiagonal to bandwidth 2s+1 (§3).
  - Transferable: test your favourite idea in its simplest form first. Report the measured failure (Table 1) and the lower-bound argument, then relax exactly one parameter (here the bandwidth s) until the costs balance (pp. 6–14).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Kept*: the Moré–Sorensen λ iteration through Theorem 1 (p. 3), and the quasi-Newton update.
  - *Changed*: only the basis. B = Ω M Ωᵀ is maintained with M banded.
  - *Absorbing a rank-one update ρ r rᵀ*: Givens rotations of adjacent type are applied in two phases (pp. 14–20).
    - First, zeros are chased into r̂. The part of the matrix below the band is then left with a rank-one structure, which is what makes the recursion work.
    - Second, the bandwidth is reduced from 2s+3 to 2s+1 by bulge chasing.
  - *Cost*: at most ¾(n−1)²/(s+1) rotations, so O(n³/s) work for the update. Each λ solve costs O(ns²). Choosing s ~ n^{2/3} gives O(n^{7/3}) per iteration (pp. 20–21).
  - *Refinements*:
    - a two-ended variant roughly halves the number of rotations (pp. 17–19);
    - storing Ω as Ω₀ times a list of rotations, and refactorizing every t iterations, gives O(n^{11/5}) (pp. 22–24).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - B is a general symmetric matrix "when there is no helpful sparsity" (p. 1).
  - B_{k+1} − B_k has rank at most two and is treated as two rank-one changes (p. 4).
  - The number of values of λ per iteration is bounded independently of n (p. 4).
  - Genericity is assumed: no zeros in the tridiagonal part (p. 8), and "no unexpected zero matrix elements" (p. 20).
  - The norm is Euclidean (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *§2 lower bound on rotations* (pp. 8–13):
    - He defines *stage q reached*.
    - Any rotation sequence is normalized using two rules: G(p) commutes with G(ℓ) when |p−ℓ| ≥ 2, and G(ℓ)G(ℓ+1)G(ℓ) can be rewritten as G(ℓ+1)G(ℓ)G(ℓ+1).
    - An induction, ℓ(q) ≥ min[2q+2, n], then gives at least about n²/4 rotations.
  - *Admitted gaps*:
    - The impossibility of an O(n)-rotation update is a conjecture that "he has not proved" (p. 7).
    - He adds that "some of the remarks about nonzero elements do not yet have a rigorous proof" (p. 12).
    - Stewart (1997) proves that the number is at least of magnitude n² (p. 14).
  - *§3 exact counts*: floor sums (3.3)–(3.7) give upper and lower bounds whose gap is only (s+1)/8 (pp. 18–20).
  - *Cost balancing*: O(s²n + n³/s) gives s ~ n^{2/3} (pp. 20–21). O(n² + n²t/s + n³/t + ns²) gives s ~ n^{3/5} and t ~ n^{4/5} (pp. 23–24).
  - Transferable: when you believe a cost cannot be beaten, prove a lower bound on the number of elementary operations (here Givens rotations) under explicit structural assumptions, and say which steps are not yet rigorous (pp. 7–14).
- **D5 Experiments**: Fortran programs (Full-solve, Transform, Band-solve, Update, Reduce) were timed on a Sun 4 workstation.
  - Table 1: the tridiagonal route, n = 50 … 800 (p. 6).
  - Table 2: n = 400 with s = 10 … 200, where Total = Update + Reduce + 2 × Band-solve. The minimum Total is at s = 60 (pp. 21–22).
  - Table 3: the best s for each n, with Full/Total ratios from 1.43 at n = 50 to 10.85 at n = 800. The scaling is checked against 2^{7/3} ≈ 5.04 per doubling of n (p. 22).
  - The stated purpose is to investigate "the unknown constant factors" (p. 22).

### S068 · On the Lagrange functions of quadratic models that are defined by interpolation (Optimization Methods and Software 2001, DOI 10.1080/10556780108805839; text is report DAMTP 2000/NA10)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none · **Position in trajectory**: The theory paper for the UOBYQA algorithm, which was then "being developed by the author" (p. 2). It is the first written account of three things: updating the Lagrange functions, the Lagrange-function error bound, and using that bound to decide when ρ may be reduced. It is dedicated to L.C.W. Dixon's 65th birthday (p. 1). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–17, including the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - *Main entry*: his own algorithm under development. "The main purpose of this paper is to draw attention to three more properties of the Lagrange functions that are important to an algorithm for unconstrained minimization without derivatives that is being developed by the author." (p. 2)
  - *History*: numerical testing of COBYLA "gave favourable results when most of the freedom in the variables is taken up by active constraints". It also exposed "some severe inadequacies when second derivative terms are important", which led to quadratic models (p. 4).
  - *A second, cost entry*: in S014's scheme, the tests (4.2) forced about ½n² new values of F at every reduction of ρ (p. 11).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Kept*: the full quadratic model, with m = (n+1)(n+2)/2 interpolation conditions (p. 2), and the two-radius trust region (p. 10).
  - *(1) Choice of the point to replace*. All Lagrange-function coefficients are stored. Replacing x_j by x̂ multiplies the determinant of the interpolation matrix by ℓ_j(x̂), so j is chosen with |ℓ_j(x̂)| relatively large (pp. 5–6).
  - *(2) Cheap update*. All ℓ_i are updated in O(m²) by (2.5)–(2.6), which is O(n⁴) per iteration. Powell calls this optimal when all the coefficients are stored, because a typical iteration alters every coefficient (pp. 3, 6).
  - *(3) Error bound*. |Q(x) − F(x)| ≤ (M/6) Σ_j |ℓ_j(x)| ‖x − x_j‖³ (Theorem 2, p. 9), with M estimated during the run (p. 4).
  - *(4) When ρ may be reduced*. The model is accepted, and ρ may be reduced, if every j satisfies one of two conditions: its error-bound term is at most ε, or ‖x_j − x_k‖ ≤ βρ (4.5). Previously all points had to lie within βρ (pp. 11–12).
  - *(5) Initial points*. The points x₀ ± σ_j e_j and x₀ + σ_j e_j + σ_k e_k are chosen so that the Lagrange functions are explicit and mostly zero, which gives an O(n²) setup. Known Hessian sparsity removes points (pp. 14–16).
  - *Rounding*: the model is centred at x₀ to reduce the effects of rounding (p. 2).
  - Transferable: choose the initial sample so that the basis you must maintain is available in closed form and is mostly zero (pp. 14–15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Theorem 2 needs bounded, continuous third derivatives. M bounds the third derivative along any line (pp. 8–9).
  - The interpolation points must be poised, i.e. define a unique interpolant (p. 9).
  - F is unconstrained.
  - The practical rule applies (1.6) "even if F does not have any third derivatives" (p. 3).
  - §4 explicitly targets noisy or non-smooth F: adaptive switching inside the computation of F, random errors in preliminary measurements, and auxiliary values restricted to discrete sets (p. 10).
  - The tolerance ε (4.3) combines ρ² with the least eigenvalue of G_Q, and is deliberately cautious when G_Q is nearly singular (p. 11). The numerical constant in (4.3) is garbled in the extraction and cannot be verified.
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - *(i) Determinant ratio*: as a function of x̂, the ratio det B(x̂, x₂, …, x_m)/det B(x₁, …, x_m) is a quadratic that vanishes at x₂ … x_m and equals 1 at x₁. It is therefore ℓ₁, whatever basis is used (p. 5).
  - *(ii) Self-correction* (Theorem 1, pp. 6–8). Let H be the coefficient matrix of the (possibly wrong) ℓ_i and B the matrix of basis values.
    - The update changes Bᵀ − H⁻¹ only by zeroing column j. The proof uses Ĥ = (I − v e_jᵀ)H and a rank-one inverse formula.
    - Corollary: ‖Bᵀ − H⁻¹‖_F decreases monotonically, so any error disappears once all initial points have been replaced.
  - *(iii) Error-bound proof*: subtract the Taylor quadratic T at y without loss of generality. Interpolation reproduces quadratics, so the error does not change. Then bound each F(x_j) by the one-dimensional Taylor remainder along the line from y to x_j (pp. 9–10).
  - *(iv) Estimating M*: M is the largest lower bound that (1.6) implies at the points where F has been calculated. The sum in (1.6) is positive because Σ_j ℓ_j ≡ 1 (p. 4).
  - Transferable: to show that an update is numerically robust, prove that it becomes exact from any nonsingular starting state after enough steps, because the error matrix loses a column per step (pp. 7–8).
  - Transferable: without loss of generality, subtract the part of the target that your approximation reproduces exactly (here the Taylor quadratic), so that the bound involves only the remainder (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Algorithm*: a provisional version, whose results were presented at the 17th Mathematical Programming Symposium in Atlanta (p. 12).
  - *Test functions*: the Fletcher–Powell (1963) trigonometric sums. S and C are random integers from [−100, 100], the optimum x* is random, and the start is x* plus random contributions from [−0.1, 0.1] (p. 12).
  - *Design*: five functions each for n = 3, 5, 10 and 20. ρ goes from 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁸, dropping by a factor of ten each time (pp. 12–13).
  - *Reporting*: Table 1 gives #F and F_min at the end of each ρ, for the n = 5 and n = 20 runs that needed the most function values. For n = 20, F_min reaches 3.3×10⁻¹⁸ after 1489 values. The last four reductions of ρ cost 13–17 values each, compared with ½n² = 200 (p. 13).
  - *Failure reported*: for still smaller ρ, the errors in computing F exceed the true model errors. M becomes huge and the gains vanish (p. 13).
  - *Metric*: function evaluations. n is kept small because each iteration costs O(n⁴) (pp. 12–13).
  - Transferable: report the cumulative evaluation count at each resolution level, so that readers see the marginal cost of each decade of accuracy (p. 13).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Winfield (1973) used quadratic interpolation without Lagrange functions (p. 4).
  - Conn–Scheinberg–Toint work with functions that are an alternative to Lagrange functions. Powell states his preference for Lagrange functions, and notes that some ideas from his 1994 talk influenced their algorithm (p. 4).
  - He discovered Theorem 2 himself. With the help of de Boor and Waldron he then found and credited Davis (1975), "Kowalewski's exact remainder", and Waldron (1998) (p. 10).
  - Conn–Toint (1996) is cited for initialization (p. 14).
  - He positions the paper against his own survey: the extra goodness tests recommended in Powell (1998), including one on the conditioning of the system, "have been abandoned" (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The paper is organized around "properties" of one mathematical object, and its purpose is stated in §1 (p. 2).
  - Results are called provisional, and details are promised "when the development of the new algorithm is complete" (p. 12).
  - The convergence claim is hedged: the results "suggest that a superlinear rate of convergence may be achievable" (p. 4).
  - There is candid lab-notebook detail: wrong initial ℓ_i happened "by mistake", and "the recovery process that has just been described worked very nicely in practice" (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contributions*: theory written for a code (update, stability, error bound), a cheaper criterion for reducing ρ, and an initialization (pp. 5–16).
  - *Limits*:
    - O(n⁴) work per iteration restricts n (pp. 3, 12–13);
    - very small ρ destroys the gains (p. 13);
    - the per-term test is "an optimistic error bound that seems to be successful in practice" (p. 12).
  - *Future*: sparsity and linear constraints could reduce m (p. 16).
