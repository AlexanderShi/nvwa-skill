### S025 · UOBYQA: unconstrained optimization by quadratic approximation (Mathematical Programming 2002, DOI 10.1007/s101070100290)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: The first full-quadratic interpolation code in the DFO series. It develops COBYLA's linear-model method (p. 4) and is the predecessor whose O(n⁴) cost drives S047, S045 and NEWUOA. It was written Dec 2000 and revised June 2001 (p. 1). **Read**: full text, including all tables. The reference list was checked for citation counts only.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a code: the paper "describes the techniques that are used by the Fortran 77 software" (p. 2). Derivatives are unavailable. The user supplies F, a start x_b, and ρ_beg ≥ ρ_end (p. 2). Two motivations are stated:
  - Noise tolerance. Keeping interpolation points about ρ apart gives some control over the noise in the derivative estimates. Noise, by contrast, "is very harmful to algorithms that calculate derivative estimates" from differences of F values (p. 2).
  - Curvature. A quadratic model needs (n+1)(n+2)/2 values of F, and the interpolation points must be poised, i.e. no nonzero quadratic vanishes on all of them (abstract, p. 1).

  The limit is stated in the abstract itself: results are "very promising" for n ≤ 20, and larger n is "problematical" because the work per iteration is O(n⁴) (p. 1).
  - Transferable: Put the scaling limit of your method in the abstract, as a number of variables tied to a cost formula (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Keep every Lagrange function of the interpolation problem explicitly, with its gradient and Hessian, and update all of them when one point moves (p. 3, §4). The model is Q = Σ F(x_j) ℓ_j (1.5) (p. 3). The Lagrange functions serve three purposes:
  1. They update the model: Q̃ = Q + [F(x̃_t) − Q(x̃_t)] ℓ̃_t (4.13) (p. 17).
  2. They choose the geometry move: maximize |ℓ_j(x_k+d)| within ‖d‖ ≤ ρ (1.8) (p. 4).
  3. They estimate the model error through the bound |Q(x) − F(x)| ≤ (M/6) Σ |ℓ_i(x)| ‖x − x_i‖³ (3.1) (p. 10).

  Kept from COBYLA: interpolation with Lagrange functions, a ρ that is never increased, and model steps. The UOBYQA paper itself says only that it develops the 1994 linear-model method (p. 4); the shared machinery is spelled out in S047, which describes both codes in one framework (S047 p. 5–9). Changed:
  - the model is a full quadratic (p. 2);
  - a second radius Δ ≥ ρ is added, so that steps may be longer than ρ (p. 3);
  - the Δ update follows the usual ratio rule (3.5), with r ≥ 0.7, 0.1 < r < 0.7 and r ≤ 0.1 read as "too conservative, adequate or overambitious" (p. 11). Δ is reset to ρ when it would fall to 1.5ρ or below (p. 11);
  - the model-step tests are relaxed by the error bound (see new-pattern candidates) (p. 13).

  Once the model is judged adequate, ρ is reduced by formula (3.13): ρ_end if ρ_old ≤ 16ρ_end, √(ρ_old ρ_end) if ρ_old ≤ 250ρ_end, and 0.1ρ_old otherwise. The formula is "designed to provide reductions by about a factor of ten" (p. 14). Other design points:
  - F is computed only if ‖d‖ ≥ ½ρ (p. 11).
  - The last Newton–Raphson step is evaluated at termination, a "device" that "often provides a substantial improvement" (p. 14).
  - The initial points are coordinate steps ±ρ_beg, biased toward low F: the second point on axis j goes to +2ρ_beg if the first one decreased F (4.1). Diagonal pairs give the off-diagonal Hessian terms (4.3). The initial model and Lagrange functions then cost only O(n²) (p. 14–16).
  - The point to replace is t = argmax |ℓ_i(x_k+d)| · max[1, ‖x_i − x̂_k‖³/ρ³] (4.16). Powell says the details of this rule were shaped by numerical results (p. 18).
  - Transferable: Give the resolution parameter a stated reduction schedule and write the design purpose next to the constants (reductions "by about a factor of ten" that end exactly at ρ_end, p. 14). Readers of the code can then tell design intent from tuning.
  - Transferable: When a step is too short to be worth an evaluation (‖d‖ < ½ρ), do not spend an F value on it. Treat it as the signal to check the model or lower ρ (p. 11–13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: F comes from a subroutine; no derivatives, no constraints, no sparsity exploited (p. 2, p. 4). The model is fully determined, m = (n+1)(n+2)/2 (p. 2). The error bound (3.1) needs bounded third derivatives. Powell concedes the view that it "is of academic interest only": the assumption is too restrictive, rounding errors make functions non-differentiable, and M is unknown (p. 10). He uses it anyway through an estimated constant (p. 10). The superlinear-convergence discussion assumes the following (p. 25):
  - bounded third derivatives;
  - convergence to a local minimum with a positive definite Hessian;
  - the Lagrange-function bound (6.3), max |ℓ_j| ≤ c.

  The finite-termination argument rests only on the finite precision of computer arithmetic (p. 25).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There are no formal theorems. The devices are:
  - **Self-correcting Lagrange updates** (4.10)–(4.11). After the update ℓ̃_t(x̃_t) = 1 and ℓ̃_i(x̃_t) = 0 hold for *any* old functions. Errors at the moved point are therefore wiped out, and later errors there come only from new rounding (p. 16–17).
  - **Determinant factor**: replacing x_t by x̃_t multiplies the interpolation determinant by ℓ_t(x̃_t) in any basis. This justifies preferring |ℓ_t| > 1 (p. 18).
  - **Cheap subproblem with a worst-case ratio**. Maximizing |ℓ_j| crudely, with the better of the gradient direction and an approximate top eigenvector, gives at least half the optimum (2.11) (p. 7). The eigenvector is taken from span{w, G_j w}, where w is the column of largest norm. A 4×4 matrix (2.13) shows that w alone can fail, and the span construction guarantees |d̃ᵀG_j d̃| ≥ ½ n^{−1/2} spectral radius · ρ² (2.15) (p. 7–8). Restricting the angle to multiples of π/4 loses at most a factor (12+2√2)/17 ≈ 0.872 (p. 9).
  - **Trust-region solver** (Moré–Sorensen). Orthogonal reduction to tridiagonal form costs O(n³). Stopping uses their Lemma 3.4 with ratio ≤ 0.01 (99% of the optimal reduction), plus a special vector for the hard case (p. 5–6).
  - **Scalar-product trick**. Q(x_k+d) − Q(x_k) = v_Qᵀw, with w built in O(n²) (5.4)–(5.5). Constant terms need not be stored (p. 20–21).
  - **Cheap sufficient test** (5.8)–(5.9). A bound using ‖G_j‖_F skips most solves of (1.8). The count drops from 16434 to 8466 (n = 20) and from 785 to 194 (n = 5) (p. 22).
  - **Heuristic superlinear argument**. Geometry moves become rarer as ρ → 0, so all trust-region steps eventually become Newton steps. This is offered as a conjecture (p. 24–25).
  - **Finite termination** by counting: the machine has only finitely many F values, and each model step increases the number of points within 2ρ (p. 25).
  - Transferable: If you approximate a subproblem, prove a worst-case ratio and also log the empirical ratio over real runs. Powell reports the ratio over 8466 occurrences, never below 0.5 (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Main family**. The random trigonometric sums of squares (6.1) of Fletcher–Powell 1963. S and C are integers in [−100, 100], x* ∈ [−π, π], and the start is perturbed by up to 0.1. Settings: ρ_beg = 0.1, ρ_end = 10⁻⁸, n = 3, 5, 10, 20, and 5 random instances per n (p. 22). These functions are chosen because ∇²F is often ill-conditioned at the solution and F has many saddles and maxima (p. 23). Table 1 reports NFTOT per instance. Two runs that reach a different minimizer are marked (p. 23).
  - **Per-ρ trace**. Table 2 gives NF and FBEST each time ρ is lowered (p. 24). Success criterion: fewer than ½n² new F values per ρ (p. 24).
  - **Rounding**. Adding the constant 10⁴ to F (Table 3) shows that the work at ρ ≤ 10⁻⁷ is wasted, and that the estimate of M/6 then becomes huge. These remarks "may be helpful to the choice of" ρ_end (p. 26).
  - **Kinks**. A sum of moduli with n terms *fails*: only two n = 3 cases reach ρ_end accuracy (p. 26–27). With 2n terms the runs cost many more F values but are accurate (Table 4, p. 26–27).
  - **Scaling**. A smooth, well-conditioned least-squares family gives NFTOT ≈ 0.8n² + 8.2n + 2 (Table 5, (6.6), p. 27–28).
  - **Timings**. Table 6 splits the time by task using DTIME on a Sparc 10. For n ≥ 10, more than half the time is in the O(n⁴) parts (p. 28).
  - **Classic problems**. Rosenbrock and the singular function (Table 7). The singular function needs over 30 F values per ρ and terminates early because of rounding, at F = 4.5·10⁻³⁴ (p. 29).
  - **Baseline**. Chebyquad, n = 2–8, with three values of ρ_beg, against Powell's 1964 conjugate-direction counts taken from Fletcher 1965 (Table 8, p. 29–30).
  - Transferable: Run a rounding-error stress test by adding a large constant to F (here 10⁴) and rerun the same instances. The ρ at which progress stops tells users how to set ρ_end (p. 26).
  - Transferable: Report per-task timing shares, measured by making the code repeat one task and differencing (p. 28). The result locates the bottleneck that the next design must remove.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The quadratic-interpolation idea is credited to Winfield 1973 (p. 4). The work is placed as a development of Powell 1994 (COBYLA) and his 1998 survey (p. 4).
  - Conn–Scheinberg–Toint 1997 share "Some of our techniques", but their "algorithm does not employ Lagrange functions" (p. 4).
  - The only numerical baseline is Powell's own 1964 method, using published counts (p. 23, p. 29–30).
  - The future direction is justified through Broyden–Dennis–Moré 1973: superlinear convergence does not need accurate second derivatives (p. 30).
  - The reference list has 12 items, 5 with Powell as an author (p. 31).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The paper is organised as a software description: roadmap (p. 4), subproblems (§2), variable changes (§3), initialization and updating (§4), an 11-step summary (p. 18–20), then numerics (§6).
  - Design choices are justified by numbers from development runs, not by theorems (p. 9, p. 22).
  - Hedging is explicit ("it may be possible to show", p. 24), and the gap is admitted: "It would be better, however, to establish this property without some of the assumptions" (p. 25).
  - The main thesis (Lagrange functions plus an error bound give fast convergence) is in the abstract (p. 1) and in §1 (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and in software: a free Fortran 77 code, "available free of charge from the author" by e-mail (p. 30). The limits are concrete:
  - O(n⁴) routine work, "prohibitively expensive for more than 50 variables" (p. 30);
  - kinks are handled poorly by quadratic models (p. 27);
  - rounding sets a floor on useful ρ (p. 26);
  - superlinear convergence is only conjectured (p. 25).

  Future directions: a sparse-Hessian version ("software for this straightforward extension to UOBYQA has not been written yet"), and techniques that avoid "the need for all the parameters of all the Lagrange functions" (p. 30). This is the seed of S047/S045. Powell also notes: "There are many applications of unconstrained optimization with no more than 20 variables" (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The published description of the two radii. ρ is never increased, because each increase would force more decreases later (p. 3). Δ ≥ ρ is "mandatory" (p. 11). The ratio thresholds 0.1/0.7 and the 16/250 formula are those later found in NEWUOA (p. 11, p. 14). ρ is not lowered until the model has passed the adequacy tests (p. 12–14).
- Method 2 ✗ contradiction (partial), aimed at its Limitations line, which says the rationale of the constants is undocumented. The paper gives reasons for several of the constants:
  - the three r bands are read as conservative, adequate and overambitious (p. 11);
  - the 1.5ρ reset "is helpful occasionally" (p. 11);
  - the 16/250 formula gives "reductions by about a factor of ten" that end exactly at ρ_end (p. 14).

  The values themselves remain empirical.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. UOBYQA is presented as a development of COBYLA's linear-model method (p. 4). The closing paragraph names its binding limitation (n > 50) and the next step (p. 30).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence:
  - x_b is moved to the best point whenever ρ is reduced, "to prevent damage from computer rounding errors" (p. 2, p. 14);
  - the run exits early when rounding makes the predicted reduction v_Qᵀw ≤ 0 (p. 21);
  - there is a dedicated rounding-error experiment (p. 26);
  - the O(n⁴) cost is accounted for task by task (p. 20, p. 28).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence, with a ⚠ variant. Failures are published: the sum of moduli with n terms (p. 26–27), two runs ending at a different minimizer (p. 23), the rounding floor (p. 26). The variant: the test set is a *random family* with 5 instances per n, not only hand-picked problems (p. 22).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: "The Fortran 77 code was developed for general use, and is available free of charge" (p. 30).
- Research Heuristic 4 ✅ evidence. Short steps (‖d‖ < ½ρ) trigger the adequacy tests on point distances (3.7)–(3.12) before ρ is lowered (p. 11–13).
- Research Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence. The O(n⁴) diagnosis leads to redesign (p. 28, p. 30).
- New-pattern candidate: *theory bound as a runtime test with an online-estimated constant*. The unknown M/6 in bound (3.1) is set to the largest observed ratio |Q − F| / Σ|ℓ_i|‖x − x_i‖³ (3.2) (p. 10). It is used only after ten updates, and it decides which far points need not be moved (3.11)–(3.12) (p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate: *tolerate model error of the size of the reduction you forgo*. Skipping a step of predicted gain ε means errors of size ε in Q "should be tolerable" (p. 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Turn an error bound whose constant is unknowable into a practical test. Replace the constant by the running maximum of observed error ratios, and switch the test on only after enough observations (p. 10, p. 13).
- [D2] Make the tolerance of an accuracy test equal to the gain you are giving up by not stepping. It is a self-consistent threshold with no new constant (p. 13).
- [D4] For a crude maximization of |quadratic| on a ball, compare the gradient direction with a top-eigenvector surrogate from span{w, Gw} (w = the column of largest norm). This costs O(n²), guarantees a factor ½, and has a counterexample that shows why w alone fails (p. 7–8).
- [D5] Add a large constant to the objective and rerun: a cheap way to find the precision floor of a DFO method (p. 26).
- [D5] Report failures in the same tables as successes, and mark instances that converged to a different minimizer (p. 23).

### S047 · On trust region methods for unconstrained minimization without derivatives (Mathematical Programming 2003, DOI 10.1007/s10107-003-0430-6)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: The bridge paper between UOBYQA and NEWUOA. It was presented at NTOC 2001, Kyoto (p. 1), and written Feb 2002, revised Aug 2002 (p. 1). It compares four model spaces inside one framework. §5 is the first published appearance of least-Frobenius-norm updating (p. 17–21), begun in January 2002 (p. 19). **Read**: full text, including all tables.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a cost trade-off between model spaces M of dimension m. Each iteration keeps m values and updates the model in O(m²) (p. 2). Full quadratics give O(n⁴) work, "intolerable for more than about 50 variables", while linear models "have no curvature" (p. 2–3). The question: does a model in between (diagonal Hessian, m = 2n+1) work? Expectations are stated before the tests: the diagonal model should beat linear models but need more iterations than full ones (p. 3). A contrast opening sets trust-region methods against simulated annealing and line searches (p. 2).
  - Transferable: Frame a design question as a choice of model space, with dimension m, and write down beforehand the result you expect for each choice (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: One generic trust-region framework, §2, is valid for any linear space M (p. 4–9):
  - a trust-region iteration versus a model iteration (p. 4–5);
  - F is evaluated only if ‖x − x_1‖ ≥ ½ρ, because a model fitted to two very close points is sensitive to errors in F, "especially if the errors cause substantial discontinuities" (p. 5);
  - the ratio test uses 0.1 (2.5) (p. 5);
  - the centre moves on *any* decrease, unlike methods that require sufficient decrease. This keeps x_1 the best point and makes the strict inequalities (2.6) hold (p. 6);
  - Lagrange functions are used for nonsingularity and updating (p. 7–9).

  The framework is instantiated as four codes: COBYLA (linear), UOBYQA (full quadratic), UOBSQA (Hessian sparsity of F), and UOBDQA (diagonal Hessian even when F is not sparse) (p. 3, p. 9–11). UOBDQA was computed by modifying the UOBYQA code (p. 3). §5 changes the update: keep m̂ = 2n+1 interpolation conditions but let Q be a full quadratic. Take up the freedom by minimizing ‖∇²Q_new − ∇²Q_old‖_F, by analogy with the symmetric Broyden formula (p. 17–18). The new Q solves an (m̂+n+1)-dimensional saddle system (5.11) (p. 19).
  - Transferable: Build one framework and swap only the model space. Differences in results can then be attributed to the model alone (p. 3, §2–3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: No derivatives; F comes from a subroutine (p. 2). No convergence theorem is claimed. UOBYQA's error estimate needs zero error when F is quadratic, which fails for UOBDQA. Powell keeps it "as if it were true" in order to test UOBDQA empirically (p. 11). For §5 he assumes rank(X) = n+1 and rank(B) = m̂ (p. 18). The projection property holds only for quadratic F (p. 18–19). The §5 prototype keeps diagonal Lagrange functions for geometry control. This is "not invariant under orthogonal rotations", so it is "intended only for some preliminary investigations" (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The devices are elementary:
  - **Induction on nonsingularity**. If the system first becomes singular when x_t is replaced by x*, the vanishing function must be a multiple of ℓ_t. So singularity ⇔ ℓ_t(x*) = 0 (p. 7).
  - **Optimal update cost**. Updating all Lagrange functions costs O(m²), which "may be optimal" because the matrix has m² elements (p. 8–9).
  - **Toy counterexamples, one per model class**:
    - A 2-D quadratic with one very large curvature parameter (3.1) and three symmetric points (3.2) makes COBYLA's linear model step fail. The result is termination or a ρ reduction while x_1 is still far from the solution. With Δ = ρ, as in COBYLA, the iteration count for the next ρ is of a magnitude the paper calls unsatisfactory when the constant is very large (p. 9–10). The symbols for the constant and the distances are lost in the extracted text, so only this qualitative conclusion is recorded.
    - A rotated ill-conditioned quadratic (3.3) with five points (3.4) gives UOBDQA an isotropic model (3.6) whose step is too short. Again the run terminates or lowers ρ although the optimum is still far away, at a distance of √2 times a quantity whose symbol is lost in the extracted text (p. 11–12).
  - **Ill-conditioning explained**. ∇²F(x*) = 2ZᵀZ (4.5). The least eigenvalue is small when some v is nearly orthogonal to all rows of Z, which is likely when the number of terms equals n (4.6) (p. 14–15).
  - **Projection (Pythagoras) property** of the least-Frobenius update for quadratic F. Minimizing ‖(∇²Q_new − ∇²Q_old) + θ(∇²F − ∇²Q_new)‖_F over θ gives orthogonality (5.6)–(5.8) (p. 18–19).
  - **KKT form**. ∇²Q_new = ∇²Q_old + Σ λ_j x_j x_jᵀ with Xλ = 0 (5.9), leading to system (5.11) with A_ij = ½(x_iᵀx_j)² (p. 19). A has no negative eigenvalues, a fact Parlett confirmed for him (p. 19, p. 21).
  - Transferable: For each cheap model class, build the smallest problem (two variables, one huge parameter) on which its step is provably useless. This makes the class's failure mode concrete before any benchmark (p. 9–12).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Sparse problems**. ARWHEAD and BDQRTIC (from the appendix of Conn et al. 1994) and CHROSEN (Toint 1978), n = 10, 15, 20, 25, ρ from 0.5 to 10⁻⁶. The metric is the number of F values (Table 1, p. 12–13). For CHROSEN the final distance to the optimum is also reported (p. 14).
  - **Random trigonometric family** (4.4), with the number of terms equal to n or 2n and 5 random instances per case. Only the least and greatest counts are shown, and the same random numbers are used for every method (p. 14, Table 2 p. 15).
  - **Scaled variant**. After a surprise, a designed follow-up: F_new(x) = F_old(D⁻¹x) with D log-uniform on [1, 10] (Table 3, p. 15–16).
  - **Timings** in seconds, averaged over 5 instances, n up to 40, on a Sun Ultra 10 (Table 4, p. 16–17).
  - **§5 prototype** (Table 5, p. 20). The n = 40 count falls from 6831 to 2179, n = 80 from 15147 to 4623, and n = 160 from 34854 to 9688. Only two instances were run at n = 160, because each took about 25 hours (p. 20–21).
  - Unexpected results are reported as such: COBYLA "better than the author expected" (p. 13); UOBDQA's success "staggering and unexplained" (p. 13); COBYLA beating UOBDQA was "unexpected" (p. 15). They are then tested: mild diagonal scaling "destroyed" COBYLA's efficiency (p. 16).
  - Transferable: When a baseline does surprisingly well, write down the mechanism you suspect (here, a Hessian near a multiple of I) and design a transformation that removes it (random diagonal scaling). Rerun, and report whether the surprise disappears (p. 15–16).
  - Transferable: Report evaluation counts and wall-clock time side by side. Powell finds UOBDQA faster than UOBYQA at n = 20 despite about 3× more F values (p. 16–17). The reader's cost model then decides.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Winfield 1973 proposed quadratic interpolation "but he does not provide a robust way of maintaining nonsingularity" (p. 10).
  - Conn–Scheinberg–Toint 1997 is noted as starting iterations before m values are available (p. 2).
  - The symmetric Broyden formula (Fletcher 1987) is the explicit template for §5 (p. 17).
  - All baselines are the author's own codes (p. 3).
  - The gradient-method result that superlinear convergence does not need ∇²Q → ∇²F is invoked from "about 30 years ago" without a citation in this paper (p. 21).
  - The reference list has 10 items, 4 with Powell as an author (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The main results sit in the abstract: diagonal models are often more efficient than full quadratic models, and the Frobenius method gives "some very promising numerical results" (p. 1).
  - Expectations come before results (p. 3).
  - Surprises are labelled in the first person (p. 13, p. 15, p. 16).
  - Incomplete work is flagged candidly: "The reason for jumping the gun in the publication of results is that it is easy to include the least Frobenius norm updating method" in a version of UOBDQA (p. 19–20).
  - There is a history note: Lagrange functions "were the reason for beginning the development of UOBYQA about ten years ago" (p. 10).
  - There is a small arithmetic slip. A quadratic in n = 160 variables is said to have 13122 parameters (p. 21). (n+1)(n+2)/2 = 13041, the figure S045 gives (S045 p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an empirical comparison of model spaces plus a preliminary new update. The limits are concrete:
  - UOBDQA can be very inefficient when ∇²F is ill-conditioned or not diagonal (p. 12, p. 15–16);
  - the §5 prototype is not rotation-invariant (p. 20);
  - the direct O((m̂+n)³) solve is to be replaced by an updating technique the author "expects to develop" (p. 19).

  The closing claim, paraphrased because the text file has lost ligatures in this passage: the new method solves the problem in fewer evaluations than UOBYQA needs to build its first model, so trying to make every parameter of the model accurate may be inefficient (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (strong). One framework, four solvers that differ in exactly one component, the model space (§2–3, p. 3–12). Each is judged by its limitation: the linear model has no curvature (p. 3), the full model costs O(n⁴) (p. 3), the diagonal model is not rotation-invariant (p. 12). §5 removes the limitation that remains (p. 17–21).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (origin). The first published statement: m̂ ≈ 2n+1 conditions, freedom taken by the least Frobenius norm of the Hessian change, the symmetric-Broyden analogy (p. 17–19), and n = 160 solved with fewer evaluations than a quadratic has parameters (p. 21).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence:
  - ρ is never increased, with typical reductions by a factor of ten (p. 5);
  - COBYLA uses Δ = ρ, while UOBYQA adjusts Δ ≥ ρ (p. 5);
  - F is skipped for steps shorter than ½ρ (p. 5).

  The paper also explains why UOBYQA needs relaxed geometry tests. After a ρ reduction, about m model iterations would be needed to bring all points within the new radius: "tolerable for COBYLA but not for UOBYQA" (p. 6).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence:
  - the same random instances are used across methods (p. 14);
  - ranges are shown, not means (p. 14);
  - the hardest instances are named, and deleting them is shown to change the ranges (p. 15);
  - unexpected outcomes are published (p. 13, p. 15).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. Time per iteration is weighed against evaluation count (Table 4, p. 16–17). The direct O((m̂+n)³) solve is flagged as temporary (p. 19).
- Research Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence. O(n⁴) work makes full quadratics intolerable beyond about 50 variables, and this drives the redesign (p. 2–3, p. 17).
- New-pattern candidate: *toy counterexample per model class*. Two-variable quadratics with one huge parameter expose the failure modes of linear and diagonal models (p. 9–12).

### X002 · An Interview with M. J. D. Powell (Bulletin of the International Center for Mathematics 14 2003, no DOI)
**Role**: core (interview, not a research paper; D1–D8 are adapted to *how he says he works*) · **Coauthors**: interviewer L. N. Vicente (p. 7) · **Position in trajectory**: June 2003, between the least-Frobenius papers (S047, S045) and the NEWUOA release. It is his most explicit first-person account of his research process. **Read**: full text (7 pages).

- **D1 Problem & entry** (how he chooses problems): Problems come from computing needs, not from the literature. "I seek fields that may benefit from a new algorithm that I have in mind" (p. 3). "It is unusual for me to make progress in research by studying papers that other people have written" (p. 3). For his students he chooses "topics that are not receiving much attention from other researchers, in order that they can become leading experts" (p. 5). He criticizes interior-point methods for "taking far more than its share of research activity" (p. 6). His origins: at school he solved problems rather than reading texts (p. 1). At Harwell his "principal duty" was to produce Fortran programs for general calculations (p. 3).
  - Transferable: Choose problems where a new algorithm would extend the range of calculations that can be solved, and avoid crowded areas (p. 3, p. 5–6).
- **D2 Core idea** (the research loop he describes): Test an algorithm numerically → conjectures about convergence and accuracy arise → settle each conjecture by a proof or a counterexample (p. 2). "I also try to explain and to take advantage of the information that is provided by both good and bad features of numerical results" (p. 3). On hand calculations: "I still find occasionally that they are very useful" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: Treat every observed convergence behaviour as a conjecture, and look for either a proof or a counterexample before the paper is written (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions & model** (working assumptions): "whenever I try to invent a new method, I assume initially that the computer arithmetic is exact" (p. 4). His justification: for general smooth functions, techniques that guard against nonlinear terms in exact arithmetic "can usually cope with the effects of computer rounding errors". He therefore builds stability properties into the algorithm and deals with the matrix details after the principal features are chosen (p. 4). He prefers to derive the matrix calculations himself (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Examples and counterexamples come from "my strong interest in problem solving" and from software development (p. 2). A current frustration is RBF convergence orders at edges, which are "shown clearly by numerical experiments" but not yet proved (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments** (stance): Numerical experience is his "main source of ideas" (p. 3). He is intolerant of partial reliability: when a speaker claims about 90% success on test problems, "I could not tolerate a failure rate of 10%" (p. 4). Simulated annealing and genetic algorithms are "very extravagant in their use of function evaluations" (p. 4). Slow turnaround in the 1960s (one or two runs per 24 hours) taught "careful attention to detail" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: State the failure rate you will accept before benchmarking. Powell's bar is essentially zero on the test set (p. 4).
- **D6 Positioning** (relation to others' work): "I never study the details of software that is written by other people" (p. 3). As a referee: "I often consider submissions in isolation, although I should relate them to published work" (p. 5), a self-acknowledged gap in positioning. Credit assessment: the DFP paper is "mainly a description of work by Davidon" (p. 6). Most of his papers are single-authored by choice; he is usually not a co-author of his students' papers (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric** (code and papers): Software discipline rests on subroutines with clearly specified functions. "the reliability and accuracy of individual subroutines is of prime importance" (p. 4). No formal structure is needed if that holds. Documentation should state what the programs can do, but he does "not favour the inclusion of lots of internal comments" (p. 4). His refereeing standard asks three things (p. 5):
  - Is it a substantial contribution?
  - Is it correct?
  - Is the amount of detail about right?

  Published accuracy depends on "someone, different from the author(s)", checking "every line" (p. 5). His PhD standard: correct mathematics, relevance to numerical computation, and careful investigation of new ideas "instead of a review of a subject with some superficial originality" (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution & limits** (self-assessment): His most gratifying paper is "A method for nonlinear constraints in minimization problems", as a source of the augmented Lagrangian method (p. 6–7). None of his results was "unfairly neglected" (p. 6). Open problem: RBF orders at edges (p. 6). A planned nonlinear optimization book, to be dedicated to his son, had been postponed (p. 6). TOLMIN (1989) is limited to "a few hundred" variables because it uses full second-derivative matrices (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence, stated side: numerical experience is the "main source of ideas" (p. 3); he explains "both good and bad features of numerical results" (p. 3); zero tolerance for failure (p. 4).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence, stated side. He produced Fortran for general calculations and started the Harwell Subroutine Library "to reduce duplication in Fortran software" (p. 3). Documentation should say what the programs can do (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:

### S045 · Least Frobenius norm updating of quadratic models that satisfy interpolation conditions (Mathematical Programming 2004, DOI 10.1007/s10107-003-0490-7)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author; dedicated to Roger Fletcher on his 65th birthday, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The linear-algebra foundation of NEWUOA. It develops §5 of S047 into an O((m+n)²) stable update. Written Oct 2002, revised May 2003 (p. 1). It ends by saying Fortran software "has begun" (p. 35). **Read**: full text, including all proofs (Lemma 3's proof, which the author says "may be skipped", was read, p. 26–28) and all tables.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is the measured limit of the predecessor. UOBYQA's work becomes "prohibitive at about n = 50". The total time grows from 20 to 1087 seconds when n goes from 20 to 40 (p. 3). The S047 prototype suggested that fewer conditions suffice: n = 160 was solved with 9688 evaluations, while a quadratic has 13041 parameters (p. 5). Hence "high accuracy in the solution of an optimization problem may not require high accuracy in any of the quadratic models" (p. 5). There are two stated reasons for the study (p. 5–6):
  1. per-iteration work is at least m²;
  2. m = (n+1)(n+2)/2 is likely inefficient in evaluations for large n.

  The problem is then posed as: implement the update in O(m²) "without serious loss of accuracy", even when m = O(n) (p. 6). The open questions are listed: the value of m, which point to remove, and the trust-region solver (p. 6).
  - Transferable: Open with the predecessor's measured scaling (seconds at two values of n) and one striking preliminary number. Then state the precise sub-problem this paper solves and list the questions it does *not* solve (p. 3–6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Choose Q⁺ to satisfy m interpolation conditions and to minimize ‖∇²Q⁺ − ∇²Q‖_F (1.7) (p. 4). The norm is chosen by an invariance requirement: the change from Q to Q⁺ must not depend on the fixed vector x₀. Second derivatives are shift-invariant, and strict convexity gives uniqueness (p. 4). The design is analogous to symmetric Broyden (p. 4–5). What is kept from UOBYQA: interpolation, Lagrange functions, one-point replacement, and choosing t with ℓ_t(x⁺) ≠ 0 (p. 3, p. 11–12). What is changed:
  - the model now comes from the saddle system W (2.8) of size m+n+1, whose inverse H holds the Lagrange-function coefficients as columns (p. 8, p. 10);
  - H is updated, not factorized, by a rank-two formula (4.12) in O((m+n)²) (p. 12, p. 16);
  - the Hessian is stored implicitly as Γ + Σ γ_k (x_k − x₀)(x_k − x₀)ᵀ (3.14), so that the model update stays O(m²). A matrix-vector product costs O(mn), which suggests a conjugate-gradient trust-region solver (p. 13);
  - x₀ is shifted toward the best point when ‖x₀ − x_b‖/Δ grows (§5, p. 19–21);
  - the constant terms of the Lagrange functions are suppressed (the matrix Ω), and ℓ_j(x⁺) is computed from the step d and the midpoint x_mid (6.4)–(6.6) for accuracy (p. 23–24).
  - Transferable: Choose the least-change norm by an invariance you need (here, shift of origin), not by convenience, and say so in one sentence (p. 4).
  - Transferable: When the explicit update of a matrix would cost more than your budget (O(mn²) for G⁺), store it as a base matrix plus stored multipliers on known rank-one terms (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - (A1): the interpolation constraints are linearly independent (dimension m̂ − m);
  - (A2): no nonzero linear polynomial vanishes at all the points (p. 7);
  - m ≥ n+2, so that the Hessian can change (p. 4);
  - exactly one point is replaced per update (p. 6);
  - fitting to more than m values is excluded (p. 2).

  The projection property (1.8) holds for quadratic F only (p. 5, p. 9–10). The stability analysis assumes the errors of the current iteration are negligible and tracks only inherited errors (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Saddle system from the KKT conditions**. Dropping the symmetry constraint gives symmetry automatically, G = Σ λ_k(x_k − x₀)(x_k − x₀)ᵀ (2.5). This yields W (2.8) (p. 7–8).
  - **A is positive semidefinite** by a sum-of-squares rewrite (2.10) (p. 8–9). (A2) ⇔ the last n+1 rows of W are independent (p. 9).
  - **Projection / Pythagoras property** (1.8). Minimize the Frobenius norm along (∇²Q⁺ − ∇²Q) + θ(∇²F − ∇²Q⁺); the optimum is at θ = 0 (2.13)–(2.14). So ‖∇²Q − ∇²F‖_F decreases monotonically for quadratic F (p. 9–10).
  - **Lagrange functions as the columns of H = W⁻¹** (3.3)–(3.4). All ℓ_j(x⁺) are available in O(m²) (3.6)–(3.7) (p. 10–11).
  - **Inheritance of (A1)/(A2)** whenever ℓ_t(x⁺) ≠ 0 (p. 11).
  - **Rank-two inverse update** (4.2)–(4.3), proved by checking H⁺ times each column of W⁺ (Theorem, p. 15–16). It is re-expressed with a t-independent vector w (4.12). The first m components of Hw are the ℓ_j(x⁺), and the parameters are α_t = H_tt, β_t, τ_t = ℓ_t(x⁺), σ_t = α_tβ_t + τ_t² (4.19) (p. 16–17).
  - **Error-propagation invariant**. Let Ξ = W − H⁻¹. After an update, the t-th row and column of Ξ are zero and the other entries are unchanged (4.1). So errors do not grow, and the errors in the replaced point's row and column are erased (p. 14).
  - **Lemma 1**: the leading m×m block of H is independent of x₀ (p. 18–19).
  - **Lemma 2**: α_t, β_t ≥ 0, proved from the sign structure of the blocks of the inverse of [A Bᵀ; B 0] (p. 21–22). The degenerate case α_t = 0 is handled by "a tiny change to the positions of the interpolation points" plus a continuity argument (p. 22).
  - **Cancellation analysis**. With ‖x₀ − x_b‖ = MΔ, the formula for ℓ_j(x⁺) carries errors of order M⁴ times machine precision. Hence the recommendation to shift x₀ (p. 19).
  - **Lemma 3**: self-correction for the Ω update. The set of points at which the Lagrange conditions hold grows monotonically, and becomes all points once every point has moved (p. 26–28). "The lemma was suggested by numerical calculations" (p. 26).
  - **Magnitude bookkeeping**. The block orders O(Δ⁴), O(1), O(Δ⁻¹), … of W and H (7.12)–(7.13) are checked against the Test 6 values (p. 34–35).
  - Transferable: To show that an update is numerically stable, define the error matrix Ξ = W − H⁻¹. Show that one update zeros the replaced row and column and leaves the rest unchanged. Then *no growth* and *self-correction* follow without floating-point analysis (p. 14).
  - Transferable: When a nonnegativity you need depends on the inverse of a saddle matrix, use the block-inverse identities to transfer semidefiniteness from A to V and −Ω (5.13)–(5.15) (p. 21–22).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Setup**. No optimization runs. These are stress tests of the update alone: n = 50, m = 101, initial points 0 and ±e_j, and new points uniform in a ball of radius Δ, with up to 10⁵ iterations in double precision on a Sun Ultra 10 (p. 29–30).
  - **Seven tests**:
    - Tests 1 and 2 compare rules for choosing t: the max |ℓ_t| rule (7.1), and the oldest point among those with |ℓ_t| ≥ ½ max (7.2) (p. 29).
    - Tests 3 and 4 use x₀ = n^{−1/2}e and x₀ = e (p. 29).
    - Test 5 perturbs H by random errors in [−10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁴] (p. 30).
    - Tests 6 and 7 cut Δ by 10 every 500 iterations, with x₀ = 0 and x₀ = 10⁴e (p. 32).
  - **Metrics**. log₁₀ of the largest error in the Lagrange conditions and in the constraints (7.4), their Ω analogues (7.8)–(7.9), and a normalized version (7.11) when Δ is small (p. 30–33).
  - **Results, including failures**:
    - Test 4 loses about 7 digits (Table 1, p. 31);
    - Test 5's injected errors are corrected "to full machine precision" (p. 30–31);
    - Ω is more accurate than H (Table 2, p. 32);
    - Test 7 shows "losses of accuracy that are unacceptable" (p. 33).
  - **Safeguard**. A code-level precaution replaces σ_t by max[0, α_t]·max[0, β_t] + τ_t² (7.5), and the paper says openly that this breaks property (4.1) (p. 31).
  - Transferable: Run *fault-injection* tests. Perturb the maintained matrix at the start and show that the update restores the invariants to machine precision (Test 5, p. 30–32).
  - Transferable: Include a deliberately bad configuration (x₀ far from the points) and publish the resulting failure. It motivates a safeguard (the x₀ shift) that the reader might otherwise think unnecessary (p. 33).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The update is framed as the derivative-free analogue of the symmetric Broyden method (Fletcher 1987, p. 73) (p. 4–5).
  - The hope of fewer than O(n²) evaluations is tied to Broyden–Dennis–Moré 1973 (p. 5).
  - Common numerical-linear-algebra advice is challenged directly: "it is not unusual to read that unnecessary errors may occur if one works with inverses, so the updating of matrix factorizations is often recommended instead". The theory and Tables 1–3 support inverses (p. 35).
  - Other DFO methods are dismissed in one sentence each: direct search needs many evaluations, finite differences are sensitive to non-smoothness (p. 4).
  - The reference list has 8 items, 4 by Powell (p. 36).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The two motivating reasons are numbered in prose, and a section roadmap follows (p. 5–6).
  - Proof shortcuts are declared openly: "Probably the author's algebra is unnecessarily long", and "Therefore the details of that derivation are suppressed" (p. 14). A lemma "may be skipped by the reader without loss of continuity" (p. 26).
  - The origin of results is disclosed: Lemma 3 and the property behind it were discovered numerically (p. 26, p. 32).
  - Strong words are reserved for results that earn them: Test 5 is "a triumph for the stability of the updating formula" (p. 30).
  - The dedication to Fletcher ties the paper to the quasi-Newton lineage (p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory written for code: a variational formulation, a stable O((m+n)²) inverse update, x₀-shift and constant-suppression variants, and experimental evidence of stability. The limits are specific:
  - shifting x₀ costs O(n³) when m = O(n) (p. 19);
  - the value of m, the point-removal rule and the trust-region solver are left open (p. 6);
  - the precaution (7.5) sacrifices exact error cancellation (p. 31);
  - Test 7 shows the update fails without x₀ shifts (p. 33).

  Future work: Fortran software for general unconstrained minimization without derivatives "has begun" (p. 35); it became NEWUOA. A conjugate-gradient trust-region solver is expected (p. 13).
