# Paper cards · batch a2-03 (abstract / metadata level, 1976–1983)

Batch intent: Catalogue 30 works from 1976–1983 that have no open full text or are not research items. These are the first Cambridge years: conjugate-gradient restarts, sparse quasi-Newton updating with Toint, the variable-metric (SQP) methods for constraints, the watchdog technique, the approximation textbook, Powell–Sabin, and QP codes. The aim is to place each work in the trajectory and record what its abstract supports about Powell's craft.

Material roles: S024, S006, S023, S003, S013, S016, S009 and S022 are core in the batch list, and the rest are supplement. At this read level every card is a reduced supplement card.

### S051 · Some convergence properties of the conjugate gradient method (Mathematical Programming 1976, DOI 10.1007/bf01580369)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none
- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper starts from a conjecture that conjugate gradients converge superlinearly, which Crowder and Wolfe had already refuted with a single example (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: The result is stronger than a counterexample. On a convex quadratic with an arbitrary downhill initial direction, either the method terminates or the rate is only linear, and linear is the more usual case. The paper also studies relations between the starting point and the initial direction that are necessary and sufficient for termination in the quadratic case (abstract). The proofs were not read.
- **D8 Contribution**: A negative rate theorem for a whole class of starts, plus a study of necessary and sufficient conditions for termination (abstract).
- **Secondary**: The memoir says Powell showed that CG algorithms could never converge superlinearly unless periodic restarts along steepest descent were made (R007 p. 8). No reference number is attached to that sentence.
- **Method links**:
  - Existing candidate *negative result first, then relax the design parameter* ✅. This 1976 negative rate result comes one year before S006, whose abstract proposes restarts that adapt to the objective function. The link between the two rests on their abstracts only.
  - Existing candidate *numerics → conjecture → proof or counterexample* ⚠ variant. Here the conjecture is someone else's, and it is already refuted by an example. Powell's move is to turn that example into a general theorem (abstract).
- **Transferable technique** [D4]: When a conjecture has been refuted by one example, prove the general version: for every start in a natural class, the bad behaviour occurs unless a stated condition holds. Then characterize the exceptional set exactly, with necessary and sufficient conditions (abstract).
- **Quotes**:
  - "It has been conjectured that the conjugate gradient method for minimizing functions of several variables has a superlinear rate of convergence, but Crowder and Wolfe show by example that the conjecture is false." (abstract)
  - "Now the stronger result is given that, if the objective function is a convex quadratic and if the initial search direction is an arbitrary downhill direction, then either termination occurs or the rate of convergence is only linear, the second possibility being more usual." (abstract)

### S006 · Restart procedures for the conjugate gradient method (Mathematical programming 1977, DOI 10.1007/bf01593790)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: core (per the batch list; only the abstract is read here) · **Coauthors**: none
- **D1 Problem & entry**: CG needs no stored matrices, but it is only linearly convergent unless restarted. The usual rule restarts every n or n + 1 iterations, although the right frequency should depend on the objective function (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: Replace the fixed restart period by a restart procedure that takes the objective function into account automatically. A second aim is to argue for one of the proposed expressions for the multiplying factor in the search direction (abstract). The restart test itself was not read.
- **D8 Contribution**: An algorithm with an automatic restart rule, reasons in favour of one of the proposed expressions for the multiplying factor, and numerical examples in support (abstract).
- **Secondary**: The memoir credits Powell with a restart technique that performed better than restarting along steepest descent (R007 pp. 8–9). Its reference number [20] points to the Powell–Sabin paper in the list, so that mapping is unreliable (see card c06). The memoir adds that restarts are ineffective for very large problems (R007 p. 9).
- **Method links**:
  - Method 1 ✅ (outside DFO). CG and its storage-free design are kept, and the one limitation removed is the fixed, problem-independent restart period (abstract).
  - Method 4 ✅. Needing no stored matrix is the stated reason for using CG at all (abstract).
  - Method 5 ✅ (weak). Numerical examples are reported in support of the conclusions (abstract). Their scope was not read.
  - Existing candidate *negative result first, then relax the design parameter* ✅ (with S051, abstract).
- **Transferable technique** [D2]: When an algorithm has a schedule parameter that is fixed by convention (such as restarting every n iterations) but should depend on the problem, replace the fixed schedule by a test on quantities the iteration already computes (abstract, idea level only).
- **Quotes**:
  - "At present it is usual to restart every n or (n + 1) iterations, where n is the number of variables, but it is known that the frequency of restarts should depend on the objective function." (abstract)
  - "Therefore the main purpose of this paper is to provide an algorithm with a restart procedure that takes account of the objective function automatically." (abstract)

### S042 · On the estimation of sparse Hessian matrices (SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis 1979, DOI 10.1137/0716078)
**Read**: abstract (the same text is also returned by Crossref) · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. L. Toint
- **D1 Problem & entry**: Estimating second derivatives automatically from differences of first-derivative vectors, when the Hessian is sparse and its sparsity structure is known (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: Exploit sparsity *and symmetry* together to make the number of gradient evaluations small. Two algorithms are proposed: a direct method and a substitution method (abstract). How they work was not read.
- **D8 Contribution**: Two cheap algorithms that seem very successful in practice. The abstract also says that examples show they may not minimize the number of gradient evaluations (abstract).
- **Secondary**: The memoir calls this the first of three contributions on sparse quasi-Newton topics made with his PhD student Toint (S042, S107, S150). It describes this one as an extension to symmetric matrices of the Curtis–Powell–Reid sparse-Jacobian paper (R007 p. 15, refs [24, 26, 31] on pp. 28–29). The Curtis–Powell–Reid paper is S021 in card batch a2-02.
- **Method links**:
  - Method 3 ⚠ variant. The shared aim is to get derivative information from as few evaluations as possible, here by exploiting known structure rather than by least-change updating (abstract).
  - Method 4 ✅. Needing little computer arithmetic is a stated property of the algorithms (abstract).
  - Method 5 ✅. The abstract states the limitation itself: examples where the methods are not optimal (abstract).
- **Transferable technique** [D5/D8]: When you propose a heuristic for a combinatorial saving (here, the number of gradient differences), include examples where it is not optimal and say so in the abstract (abstract).
- **Quotes**:
  - "Our main purpose is to find ways of taking advantage of the sparsity structure and the symmetry of the second derivative matrix, in order to make small the number of first derivative vectors that have to be calculated." (abstract)
  - "Some examples show, however, that the given methods may not minimize the number of first derivative vector calculations." (abstract)

### S027 · The watchdog technique for forcing convergence in algorithms for constrained optimization (Algorithms for Constrained Minimization of Smooth Nonlinear Functions 1982, DOI 10.1007/bfb0120945)
**Read**: abstract · **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. M. Chamberlain, C. Lemaréchal, H. C. Pedersen
- **D1 Problem & entry**: In iterative optimization with line searches, the line-search objective function can forbid long steps. This is costly when a sequence of steps has to follow a curved constraint boundary (abstract).
- **D2 Key idea**: The watchdog is an *extension* of existing line-search algorithms. It lets some iterations take step-lengths much longer than the line-search objective would normally allow (abstract). The rule that decides which iterations may do so was not read.
- **D8 Contribution**: The technique, plus proofs of global and Q-superlinear convergence properties. The claimed benefits are large efficiency gains along curved boundaries and a Q-superlinear rate for some highly useful algorithms (abstract).
- **Secondary**: Published in Mathematical Programming Studies 16 (1982), pp. 1–17 (S050 p. 13, ref [6]). Powell and Yuan (1986) cite it among recent papers on choosing line-search functions so that the unit step-length is acceptable near the solution (S050 p. 1).
- **Method links**:
  - Method 1 ✅ (outside DFO). It is an add-on to working line-search algorithms that removes one limitation, the veto on long steps, and keeps everything else (abstract).
  - Existing candidate *make the theorem admit the practitioner's preferred choice* ✅. The long, efficient steps are allowed, and global and Q-superlinear convergence are proved with them (abstract).
- **Transferable technique** [D2/D4]: If a merit function blocks the fast local steps that you want, do not weaken the local method. Instead, relax the acceptance test on some iterations, and prove that global convergence survives and that the fast rate is recovered (abstract, idea level only).
- **Quotes**:
  - "The purpose is to allow some iterations to choose step-lengths that are much longer than those that would be allowed normally by the line search objective function." (abstract)
  - "Reasons for using the technique are that it can give large gains in efficiency when a sequence of steps has to follow a curved constraint boundary, and it provides some highly useful algorithms with a Q-superlinear rate of convergence." (abstract)
