### S062 · A note on the definition of a linear bilevel programming solution (Applied Mathematics and Computation 2006, 10.1016/j.amc.2006.01.043)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Haddad (Polytechnique Montréal), G. Savard (GERAD / Polytechnique) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This belongs to the bilevel line that starts in the PhD thesis (card b2-01, S070). It cites the JOTA 1997 links between BLP and mixed 0-1 programming [1] and a submitted branch-and-cut for BLP [2] (p. 6). It is a rebuttal note written during the DFO theory-building period (SKILL.md Research Trajectory, 2003–2009); the bilevel class returns in 2026 as bilevel DFO benchmarking (SKILL.md, Latest). **Read**: all 7 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Lu, Shi and Zhang [8,9,10] proposed an alternative definition of a BLP solution. Their motivation was an instance with a nonempty constraint set S but no solution, while a Pareto-optimal point exists; they called this a deficiency of the theory and claimed their definition "can solve a wider class of problems" (p. 4). The note enters by denying the premise: a BLP solution need not be Pareto optimal because the model is non-cooperative [11], so "no solution was found because no solution exists" (p. 4; Quote 2). The stated aim is to show that the deficiency "is not really a deficiency" and that the new definition changes the nature of the problem (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two moves.
  1. A minimal two-variable example. BLP1 has the first-level constraint y = 0 and the follower maximizing w subject to w ≤ x; in BLP2 the constraint y = 0 is moved into the follower's problem. Both have the same S, but the induced regions are {(0,0)} versus {(x,0): 0 ≤ x ≤ 1}, with optimal values 0 versus 1 (p. 3; Fig. 1). Under the new definition BLP1's "optimum" (1,0) is infeasible for BLP1 as stated, because x = 1 forces y = 1 (p. 4).
  2. An equivalence. Theorem 1 shows that the induced region of the Lu–Shi–Zhang model equals that of BLP′, the bilevel program in which the first-level constraints involving y are moved to the second level (p. 5). So the proposal is a relaxation of BLP, which is still NP-hard [12] (p. 4). Corollaries: the optimal sets coincide, the induced region is connected, and a nonempty bounded S gives an optimum at an extreme point (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Linear BLP with polyhedral X and P¹, follower feasible set S_BLP(x) = {w : Bw ≥ b − Ax} (p. 2). With first-level constraints involving y, the induced region may be disconnected or even discrete; without them it is always connected [12] (p. 2).
- **D4 Proof device (brief)**: Rewriting by definitions. Both induced regions are subsets of the same S, so it suffices to show the two follower argmin sets coincide for every x, which is a three-line set equality (pp. 5–6). The non-Pareto and connectedness facts are imported from Marcotte–Savard [11] and Savard's thesis [12] (pp. 2, 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A definitional correction: the proposed definition does not enlarge the solvable class but "relaxes the feasible region, allowing for infeasible points to be considered as feasible" (p. 1; Quote 1). No limitations or future work are stated; the note ends with the corollaries (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (outside DFO): step 4, "run the same audit on the theorem you are building on", applied to a published definition. The flaw is shown with a smallest example (p. 3) and the note also supplies the correct characterization, an equivalence theorem, rather than only the flaw (p. 5).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: constraint semantics in bilevel form. Which level "owns" a constraint is a modeling decision that changes the feasible set, just as the EB/PB choice does in DFO (pp. 3–4).
- new-pattern candidate: **equivalence proof unmasks a claimed generalization as a known relaxation**. Instead of arguing that a proposal is wrong in general, prove it equals a named, already-studied special case (p. 5).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence, variant): **Rival-failure toy examples** (c05, c2-02). Here the toy shows the rival definition's answer is infeasible for the original problem (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To refute a proposed reformulation or definition, build the smallest pair of instances that differ only in where one constraint sits, and compute both feasible regions explicitly (BLP1 vs BLP2, p. 3).
- (D4) Then prove the proposal is identical to a named special case (here BLP′), so readers can inherit everything already known about that case (Theorem 1 and Corollaries 2–4, pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "Thus, contrary to what is stated by the authors who suggested the new definition, this does not allow to solve a wider class of problems, but rather relaxes the feasible region, allowing for infeasible points to be considered as feasible." (p. 1)
- "no solution was found because no solution exists, and this may happen even if S is not empty." (p. 4)

### S031 · Filter pattern search algorithms for mixed variable constrained optimization problems (Pacific Journal of Optimization 2007, 10.21236/ada445031)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M.A. Abramson (Air Force Institute of Technology, first author), J.E. Dennis Jr. (Rice) (pp. 1, 24) · **Position in trajectory**: This merges two earlier Audet–Dennis lines, mixed-variable GPS (SIAM J. Optim. 2000, ref [6]) and the GPS filter method (SIAM J. Optim. 2004, ref [8]), for categorical variables with general nonlinear constraints (p. 3). The results differ significantly from Abramson's 2002 Rice thesis [1] (p. 11). The algorithm had already been applied to a load-bearing thermal insulation system [2] (p. 1, p. 10). Funding: AFOSR, Boeing, ExxonMobil; for Audet also FCAR and NSERC (p. 1). Received 6 May 2006, accepted 8 January 2007 (p. 24). The paper names MADS for mixed variables as the next step (p. 22), a line that later leads to CatMADS (SKILL.md Method 1). The DOI given in the batch list is the DTIC report DOI, not the journal's. **Read**: all 24 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Categorical variables "must take on values from a predefined list or set of categories, or else the problem functions cannot be evaluated. Thus, continuous relaxations are not possible" (p. 1). Their numerical codes carry no metric (steel = 1, aluminum = 2), and changing them can change the constraints, so the domain is a union of continuous polytopes indexed by the discrete values (pp. 1–2). The gap is that earlier pattern-search MVP work handled only bound or linear constraints ([6], [30]), and the general-constraint alternatives each have a drawback. Lewis–Torczon's augmented Lagrangian leaves it "unclear to us how to choose the parameters" (p. 3). Filter-SQP/SLP needs more than simple decrease (p. 3). The paper claims to be "the first algorithm with provable convergence results to directly target this class of problems" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: The algorithm is presented as MVPS [6] extended to general constraints, with the filter of [8] (p. 7). What is kept and what is added:
  - *Kept*: the separation of a free search step from a structured poll step. Any finitely terminating strategy may generate search points on the mesh (heuristics, surrogates), and "the search step contributes nothing to the convergence theory" (pp. 4–5; Quote 1).
  - *Mixed-variable local optimality*: defined relative to a user-supplied neighbor map N(x), finite, containing x, with neighbors required to lie on the current mesh (Definition 2.1, p. 4; p. 5).
  - *Poll*: continuous poll directions D_i = G_i Z_i per discrete setting, plus the discrete neighbors, plus an **extended poll** around any neighbor that "almost" improves (pp. 5–6). In practice the trigger is set as ξ_k = max{ξ, 0.05|f(x_k)|}, trading evaluations for solution quality (p. 6).
  - *Constraints*: linear constraints are handled by discarding infeasible points unevaluated, with directions that conform to the boundary (p. 7). Nonlinear ones go into a filter on (f, h) with h = ‖C(x)₊‖², chosen because h inherits the smoothness of C (p. 7). Only infeasible points enter the filter, h is capped by h_max, and feasible points are tracked separately (p. 8).
  - *New for mixed variables*: two extended-poll triggers, in f for feasible neighbors and in h for infeasible ones (p. 9; Fig. 1, p. 10). A temporary **local filter** runs each extended poll (p. 10). The poll center is either the best feasible or the least infeasible point, and not using the latter "is essentially the same as not using the filter" (p. 8). The full algorithm is in Figs. 2–3 (pp. 11–12).
- **D3 Assumptions**: A1: all iterates lie in a compact set. A2: for each discrete value, D_i includes tangent-cone generators for the linear constraints (p. 11). A2 is a condition on the constraint matrix; G_i = I and a rational A suffice [24] (pp. 11, 13). The mesh ratio τ must be rational, and the directions must factor as D_i = G_i Z_i (pp. 6, 13). The limit-point theorems need ŷ ∈ N(p̂) (p. 14). Results for f need an extra algorithmic hypothesis: the best feasible point is the poll center at all but finitely many iterations (p. 18).
- **D4 Proof device (brief)**: 
  - Mesh behaviour: a lower bound on the distance between distinct mesh points, ‖u^c − v^c‖ ≥ Δ_k/‖G_i⁻¹‖ (Lemma 5.1, p. 12). An upper bound on Δ_k, from finitely many discrete values under A1 (Lemma 5.2, pp. 12–13). Then Torczon's lattice argument: with τ = p/q rational, all iterates lie on one integer lattice, so a compact set holds only finitely many of them, which contradicts a positive lower bound on Δ_k. This gives lim inf Δ_k = 0 (Thm 5.3, p. 13). The text points to earlier counterexamples for each ingredient: a dense-mesh construction breaks Lemma 5.1 [7], and an irrational τ gives lim inf Δ_k > 0 [4] (p. 13).
  - Optimality ladder: at the limit of a refining subsequence, the Clarke derivative is bounded below by the lim sup of poll difference quotients, which are nonnegative because the poll failed (Thms 5.10–5.11, pp. 16–17; Thms 5.16–5.17, pp. 18–19). A new Theorem 5.7 turns this into KKT for the continuous variables under strict differentiability *relative to the tangent cone* of X^c (pp. 14–15).
  - The rungs, as summarized in §5.6: lower semicontinuity gives limit values; Lipschitz gives Clarke derivatives ≥ 0 on limit directions; strict differentiability gives stationarity for h, while for f only −∇f(p̂) lies in the polar of the cone of limit directions (pp. 19, 21).
  - Prop. 5.20 excludes infinitely many consecutive mesh-isolated iterations at a nonstationary point. The authors note it does not fully prevent stalling (pp. 20–21).
- **D5 Experiments**: none in this paper. Effectiveness is delegated to [2], where the design "achieved a 50% improvement over previous designs" (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a new algorithm class and a hierarchy that "unify the convergence theory of the previous work in pattern search methods" (p. 21). The limits are specific:
  - no first-order stationarity for f: "It is unfortunate that convergence of the algorithm to a first-order stationary point [...] cannot be guaranteed" (p. 21; Quote 2);
  - the f-results need poll-around-best-feasible (p. 18);
  - the algorithm can still stall (p. 21);
  - extra filter directions from [8] are omitted because explaining them "would require an extraordinary amount of additional material" (Remark 5.21, p. 21).
  Future work: apply MADS to mixed variables for stronger results (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the free search step is explicitly outside the convergence theory. Categorical intelligence (user neighborhoods, extended polls) sits in structured poll extensions whose finiteness is proved (pp. 4–6, 11).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a hierarchy of limit-point results indexed by local smoothness (lsc, Lipschitz, strict differentiability) and by which subsequence is used (§5.6, p. 21). The text points to counterexamples showing that D = GZ and a rational τ are needed (p. 13), and says plainly that KKT for f is out of reach (pp. 19, 21).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: constraint semantics split three ways, though not yet in the later EB/PB vocabulary. Linear constraints are never evaluated when violated, nonlinear ones are relaxable through the filter, and categorical values make the functions unevaluable outside their lists (pp. 1, 7).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence, second occurrence): **user-defined neighborhood defines optimality** (b2-02, S213). Local optimality is relative to a user map N(x), and the text notes that "special knowledge of the underlying engineering process or physical problem may be the only guide" for choosing it (p. 4).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **Generalize-and-recover**. The abstract promises new results "that reduce seamlessly to existing results for more specific classes of problems" (p. 1). §5.6 presents the hierarchy as unifying all pattern-search results to date (p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: **replace a performance-dependent hypothesis with an implementable algorithmic choice**. The weaker condition (poll points filtered by the best feasible or least infeasible point) is "dependent on the performance of the algorithm", so the paper assumes instead that the algorithm polls around the best feasible point, which "is easy to implement" (p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3) When a variable has no metric, define local optimality relative to a finite, user-supplied neighbor set N(x) with x ∈ N(x), and require N(x_k) to lie on the current mesh so that the finiteness arguments survive (Definition 2.1, p. 4; p. 5).
- (D2) Aggregate constraints as h = ‖C₊‖² rather than ‖C₊‖₁, so every hypothesis can be stated on C and h inherits its smoothness (pp. 7, 15).
- (D4) If a result needs a condition that depends on how the run goes, replace it with a sufficient condition the user can enforce, and say why (p. 20).
- (D2) Set an "almost improving" trigger as a percentage of the incumbent value bounded away from zero, ξ_k = max{ξ, 0.05|f(x_k)|}, and state its cost/quality trade-off (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "While the search step contributes nothing to the convergence theory of GPS (and in fact, an unsuitable search may impede performance), the use of surrogates enables the user to potentially gain significant improvement early on in the iteration process at much lower cost." (p. 5)
- "It is unfortunate that convergence of the algorithm to a first-order stationary point (with respect to the continuous variables) cannot be guaranteed." (p. 21)

### S040 · Extremal problems for convex polygons (Journal of Global Optimization 2007, 10.1007/s10898-006-9065-5)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen (HEC Montréal), F. Messine (ENSEEIHT-IRIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the survey at the center of the extremal-small-polygon side line (SKILL.md Research Trajectory, 2002–2013). It gathers the group's own results: the largest small octagon (JCTA 2002), Vincze's wife's octagon shown suboptimal (JCTA 2004), and the small octagon with longest perimeter (GERAD 2005) (refs 3–6, p. 17). It poses the unit-perimeter maximum-width problem that S080 then solves; S080 cites it as "the survey [3]" (S080 p. 2). The thesis card (b2-01, S070) holds the first octagon computation. Funding: NSERC and AFOSR for Audet (p. 1). **Read**: all 19 pages of text.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For a convex n-gon, fix one of five quantities (perimeter, diameter, area, sum of vertex distances, width) and extremize another. This gives ten pairs of problems, usually one per pair trivial or without solution (pp. 1–2). The framing is historical: problems solved "since the Greeks" by geometric reasoning are, in the last four decades, "increasingly complemented by global optimization methods", which allowed larger instances than either approach alone (p. 1). The survey has three stated audiences: geometers, optimizers, and developers of exact, heuristic and NLP codes who need test problems (p. 3; Quote 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: The organizing device is **Table I**, a matrix giving for which n each extremal problem has been solved (general polygons above the diagonal, equilateral ones below). Several cells say "open" (p. 2). Each problem is then covered in the same order: history, exact solutions, bounds for many sides, and approximate solutions (§3.1, pp. 4–10). New material in the survey:
  - Conjecture 2.1 on the sum of distances at unit perimeter, with both bounds approached by degenerate segments (p. 4);
  - a best-known unit-perimeter quadrilateral of width 1/4·√(6√3 − 9), with the problem called "unexplored" (p. 4);
  - Table II, a compilation of Moré et al.'s runs of seven NLP codes on the largest-small-polygon model with n = 6 to 200, to which the authors add three constructive reference rows: the regular n-gon, the regular (n−1)-gon, and the (n−1)-gon corrected by one added vertex (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Definitions only: convex, equilateral and regular polygons; "small" means unit diameter, following Graham; width is the minimum over directions of the distance between parallel supporting lines (pp. 1–3). The NLP benchmark model uses polar coordinates, with 2n − 2 variables and "a large number of local optima" (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof devices surveyed (brief)**: 
  - Diameter graphs, whose structure follows Erdős and Woodall (a caterpillar, or one odd cycle with pendant edges), are used to enumerate configurations: 10 for the hexagon and 31 for the octagon (pp. 5–6).
  - Most configurations are eliminated geometrically: 23 of the 30 octagon cases by reasoning, 7 numerically (p. 7). The optimal one is solved by the RLT branch-and-cut QCQP code, which took over 100 hours (p. 7).
  - For the longest-perimeter octagon: solve configuration 10, a relaxation of 29 (≤ instead of =), with the interval code IBBA in about 3 hours. The constraint turns out active. Adding first-order conditions and angle bounds then eliminates case 10 "in only 0.12 seconds" (pp. 12–13).
  - Equilateral octagon: an analytic solution with Maple under vertical symmetry. The full problem needs a proof that the four main diagonals are diameters, then RLT solves it in 45 s to 10⁻⁷ (pp. 13–14).
- **D5 Experiments**: No new runs; Table II is compiled from Moré et al. (p. 9). The authors read it as follows. Small n is fine. For large n, no code beats the corrected (n−1)-gon for n = 50, 100, 200. At n = 200 the returned areas are 16% and 14% below the regular polygon, so "these codes return a worse solution than the initial one". The problems are therefore called challenging and discriminating (pp. 9–10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A survey plus agenda. The conclusion counts the state of the ten problems: only one is solved for all n, one for n = 3, 4, 5 only, and five are "almost completely open" (p. 16). It proposes new quantities (circumradius, inradius, fatness, medians, mediatrices), star polygons and polytopes (pp. 16–17). It also records a self-correction: "two erroneous signs in the problem formulation" of the group's earlier octagon papers, corrected in a later report (p. 6; Quote 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (outside DFO): the survey turns extremal-geometry instances into "sets of test problems" for exact, heuristic and NLP codes (p. 3). It argues from compiled results that they are good benchmarks (pp. 9–10).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: in Table II the comparison currency is final value and CPU time from one starting polygon, not an evaluation budget. The authors add constructive baselines, so failures are visible against a trivial construction (p. 9).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a public correction of the group's own published formulation (p. 6). This matches the erratum habit seen in D001.
- new-pattern candidate (evidence; this is the source table): **catalogue open cells, then fill them** (s2-04: S093, S117). Table I (p. 2) and the open-problem list (pp. 15–17) are the agenda that S080, and later S093/S117, work through.
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **analytic pruning before a global-solver certificate** (s04, s2-04). Geometry removes 23 of 30 octagon cases (p. 7), and first-order conditions plus bounds cut a 3-hour case to 0.12 s (p. 13).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **extremal-geometry side line: numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later** (s03, b2-01). The survey says so outright: geometry is "increasingly complemented by global optimization methods" (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Structure a survey as a problem matrix (fix quantity A, extremize B), and put in one table which instances of each cell are solved. The open cells become a public agenda (Table I, p. 2; §4, pp. 15–17).
- (D5) When you report other people's solver results, add rows for simple constructive baselines, so readers can see when a solver returns worse than a trivial construction (Table II, p. 9).
- (D4) Solve a relaxation (≤ for =) to global optimality, check that the relaxed constraint is active at the optimum, then add first-order conditions and tight bounds to eliminate the remaining case in seconds (pp. 12–13).

**Quotes**:
- "We hope by this survey to make geometers more aware of the help that global optimization can bring them, to stimulate work of optimizers in applying global optimization to plane geometry as well as to provide sets of test problems for both exact and heuristic algorithms from that field (including methods of nonlinear programming)." (p. 3)
- "There were two erroneous signs in the problem formulation in [1] and [6]. A corrected version may be found in [4]." (p. 6)

### D001 · Erratum: Mesh adaptive direct search algorithms for constrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2008, 10.1137/060671267)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A.L. Custódio (FCT-UNL, Portugal), J.E. Dennis Jr. (Rice) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: A correction to the MADS flagship (SIAM J. Optim. 17, 2006; SKILL.md Signature Work Anatomy). S027 cites it as "To appear" (S027 ref [6], p. 27). Funding: NSERC for Audet, AFOSR/Boeing/ExxonMobil for Audet and Dennis, Coimbra and FCT for Custódio (p. 1). The file does not say who found the problem, so this card makes no claim about it. **Read**: both pages. This closes the SKILL.md Honest Boundary item that "The content of the 2008 MADS erratum [...] was not read."

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The notation of the MADS paper "evolved since the preliminary versions". The statement of Proposition 4.2, that LTMADS is an instance of MADS, is correct, but its proof is incompatible with the final notation (p. 1; Quote 1). The purpose is "to show that the proposition is valid" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Restate Proposition 4.2 as three explicit bullets (p. 1):
  1. every poll direction is a nonnegative integer combination of the mesh directions D;
  2. the frame-center-to-frame-point distance is bounded by a constant times the poll size parameter;
  3. limits of the normalized direction sets are positive spanning sets, in the sense of Coope and Price.
  Then prove each bullet in the final notation (pp. 1–2).
- **D3 Assumptions**: The LTMADS construction: L is lower triangular (n−1)×(n−1) with diagonal ±2^ℓ and lower entries drawn from {−2^ℓ+1, …, 2^ℓ−1}. B is formed by row and column permutations and a completing column b(ℓ). Two variants exist, a minimal positive basis [B −b′] and a maximal one [B −B′] (p. 2).
- **D4 Proof device (brief)**: 
  - Determinant counting: |det L| = 2^{ℓ(n−1)}, and B is a permuted lower-triangular matrix with diagonal ±2^ℓ, so |det B| = 2^{ℓn}. Column permutation preserves this (p. 2).
  - Integrality: the entries lie in a bounded integer interval, so each direction is a nonnegative integer combination of D = [I −I], and the frame lies on the mesh (p. 2).
  - A two-case bound on the frame size, minimal versus maximal basis (p. 2).
  - The Coope–Price route for bullet 3: the determinant of the normalized basis is bounded away from zero independently of k (the text states it equals 1). Condition (C1) holds for maximal bases, and (C2) holds for minimal bases because the structure constants satisfy −1 ≤ ξ ≤ −1/n (p. 2).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A proof repair. The proposition, and therefore the MADS convergence results for LTMADS, stand unchanged (p. 1). It is two pages long and states no limitation or future work.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: step 4 applied to the author's own flagship, already cited under Warning sign 2 in SKILL.md. The nuance is that the result was never wrong: the erratum repairs a proof that no longer matched the notation, which is weaker than the "needed an erratum" phrasing in SKILL.md's Inner Tensions (p. 1). The repair also turns an implicit claim into three separately checkable conditions (p. 1).
- new-pattern candidate: **public self-correction (errata and corrected formulations)**. It occurs twice in this batch: this erratum (p. 1) and the corrected octagon signs in S040 (S040 p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D7) When writing an erratum, restate the proposition in full, split the claim into separately provable bullets, and give one self-contained proof in the final notation. Say in the first sentence whether the statement or only the proof was affected (p. 1).
- (D4) To show that randomly generated integer bases give positive spanning limits after normalization, bound the determinant of the normalized basis below uniformly in k, then check Coope–Price condition (C1) or (C2) for the added columns (p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "The notation used in the paper evolved since the preliminary versions and, unfortunately, even though the statement of Proposition 4.2 is correct, its proof is not compatible with the final notation." (p. 1)
- "The proof that LTMADS is indeed an instance of MADS is not compatible with the notation used in the rest of the paper." (p. 1)

### S027 · Parallel space decomposition of the mesh adaptive direct search algorithm (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2008, 10.1137/070707518)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J.E. Dennis Jr. (Rice), S. Le Digabel (GERAD / Polytechnique) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The first parallel-decomposition variant of MADS, built on the "research version of the NOMAD C++ code" (p. 22). It cites the VNS search [4] and the progressive-barrier report [9] as concurrent work (pp. 26–27). PSD-MADS later becomes the NOMAD remedy for "many variables" (SKILL.md Workflow B, Method 6 step 3). Funding: FQRNT, NSERC, AFOSR, Boeing, ExxonMobil and the aerospace consortium CRIAQ (p. 1). **Read**: all 30 pages (references skimmed).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Direct search on expensive problems with "more than a few dozen variables may be impractical" (p. 2). The entry is a remark by Dennis and Wu that a combination of GPS and Parallel Variable Distribution (PVD) should be considered (p. 2). The existing PVD/block-Jacobi theory needs differentiable f, subproblems solved to optimality, a synchronization step, and block-separable or convex constraints. The paper calls these assumptions "not reasonable for our target class of engineering design problems" (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Make the parallel decomposition an **instance** of MADS, so the MADS analysis is inherited (pp. 2–3). The processes have fixed roles (pp. 8–12):
  - the **pollster** runs a *single-poll* MADS on all n variables: one direction b(ℓ), one evaluation, no search (p. 10);
  - **regular slaves** run MADS on random subsets N_p of fixed size n_s, with the other variables frozen at the incumbent. They stop at Δ^p_min or after bbe_max evaluations and keep N_p after a success (pp. 11, 17–18);
  - a **cache server** stores every evaluated point and serves a zero-cost "cache search" (pp. 11–12, 19);
  - a **master** keeps the nesting Δ¹_k ≤ Δ^M_k ≤ Δ^p_j between meshes (p. 9; Figs. 4–5, p. 13).
  Seen from the pollster, all the slaves' work is the Search step (the "apparent pollster algorithm", Fig. 6, p. 14). What is not changed: the MADS mesh, the update rules (τ = 4 as in LTMADS), and the extreme barrier (pp. 5, 17). What is given up: the zeroth-order result, because the poll is no longer a positive spanning set (pp. 7, 17; Quote 1).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f takes values in ℝ ∪ {∞}; Ω is known only through a feasibility oracle and satisfies a nonsmooth constraint qualification (nonempty hypertangent cone at the limit); blackboxes return, or are terminated, in finite time (pp. 2, 16). Trial points lie in a compact set, and x0 is feasible because of the extreme barrier (p. 5). Hypothesis 3.1 (mesh nesting: M(τ^ω Δ) ⊇ M(Δ)) holds when τ is an integer (pp. 9, 17). Density of b(ℓ) holds only "with probability one" (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof device (brief)**: **Interpret, don't re-prove.** A chain of inductions shows that every slave's trial point lies on the pollster's current mesh:
  - Prop. 4.1: every mesh size is τ^{−η}·Δ^user_0 with η ≥ 0 (p. 15);
  - Prop. 4.2: slave mesh = τ^β × master mesh with β ≥ 0 (p. 16);
  - Prop. 4.3: M(Δ^p_j) ⊆ M(Δ^M_k) ⊆ M(Δ¹_k) (p. 16).
  Finitely many such points arrive between two poll evaluations, so they form a valid Search step. The MADS Clarke-stationarity results then transfer verbatim (p. 16). Prop. 3.2, also by induction, shows that the master's update is always well defined (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two problems were tested:
  - Problem A is G2 from Hedar–Fukushima with n = 20, 50, 250, 500, a budget of 100n evaluations and q = 14 processes (pp. 22–23).
  - Problem B is the 60-variable MoVars problem in two versions (G ≥ 250, G ≥ 500) with 3000 evaluations and q = 6. Its feasible start was found by a pMADS violation run, and those evaluations are counted (pp. 22–23).
  The competitors were pGPS, pMADS and APPS 5.0, compared on black-box evaluations. There were 30 runs for the randomized methods but one run for pGPS and APPS, whose parallel nondeterminism "was ignored" (p. 23). The reported measures are z_best/worst/avg and an area-under-curve S_avg (p. 23). PSD-MADS has the best average and S_avg on every instance, but on A with n = 20 pMADS has the better z_best (−0.775 vs −0.761) (Table 1, p. 24). pMADS "is always the fastest to descend, but PSD-MADS overtakes it" (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An algorithm, an inherited convergence analysis, an implementation with two parameters (bbe_max, n_s), and results up to n = 500 (p. 24). It "was not conceived in order to reduce the time to obtain a solution" but to reach better solutions at large n; no speedup curves are given (p. 23). The limits are stated plainly: variable subsets are chosen at random, and "it is not clear how to do this in general or we would have done it here" (p. 24). The single-poll mesh may shrink too fast (p. 8), and the zeroth-order result is lost (p. 17). Future work: grouping by user knowledge or scaling, PVD "forget-me-not" terms, search steps in subspaces, an asynchronous recomposition step, and the progressive barrier to allow infeasible starts (pp. 24, 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ strong evidence: the extreme case of "free Search, rigid Poll". All parallel subspace work is Search, and the entire proof rests on a one-direction Poll (pp. 8, 14–16).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: it names exactly which rung of the ladder is lost (the zeroth-order result), and why (pp. 7, 17; Quote 1).
- Method 5 ✅ / ⚠: the evaluation currency is explicit (Quote 2, p. 23), and the competitors include another family (asynchronous APPS). But this 2008 paper predates data profiles and plots mean trajectories. The deterministic competitors got only one run each (p. 23).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (small): the feasible starting points, the Problem B source code and the best points were posted on Audet's website (p. 22).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: EB only, so a phase-1 feasibility run is needed. PB is listed as the fix (pp. 22, 26).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: implemented on the NOMAD research code (p. 22). It later became a documented NOMAD remedy (SKILL.md Workflow B).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **Framework theorem with plug-in instance** (c08, c2-02). A new algorithm is proved correct by showing that it *is* a MADS instance ("apparent pollster", p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **MADS-as-subroutine composition** and **Evaluation cache as shared memory and data source** (c02, S019). The cache server doubles as a free search step (pp. 11–12, 19).
- SKILL.md Inner Tensions, "small-n niche vs pressure to scale": evidence that scaling is pursued for solution quality at n ≤ 500, not for wall-clock time (p. 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To parallelize or decompose a provably convergent method without a new proof, designate one worker to run the certified algorithm's poll. Force every other worker's trial points onto its mesh, here by using an integer mesh ratio and nested mesh sizes, and interpret them as the Search step (pp. 9, 14–17).
- (D5) Count the phase-1 feasibility evaluations in the budget you plot (p. 22).
- (D5) With a mix of randomized and deterministic competitors, report best, worst and mean over 30 seeds plus the area between each z-vs-evaluations curve and a fixed reference level. The area summarizes anytime behaviour in one number (p. 23).
- (D8) State what the extension costs in theory in one sentence at the point where the design choice is made ("The price to pay [...]", p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "The price to pay for our new capability to handle a large number of variables is that this last convergence result will be lost." (p. 7)
- "The most representative cost of a black-box algorithm is the number of black-box evaluations." (p. 23)

### S076 · A new sequence form approach for the enumeration and refinement of all extreme Nash equilibria for extensive form games (International Game Theory Review 2009, 10.1142/s021919890900242x)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Belhaiza (Polytechnique Montréal), P. Hansen (GERAD / HEC Montréal) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the game-enumeration side line. The thesis's EEE algorithm for bimatrix games (card b2-01, S070, Ch. 4) led to EχMIP for bimatrix and polymatrix games (Belhaiza's 2002 MSc; JOTA 2006), and this paper carries it to extensive-form games (pp. 1–2). The reference list includes a 1998 conference paper by Audet, Hansen, Jaumard and Savard titled "Complete enumeration of equilibria for two-person games in strategic and sequence forms" (p. 14), but the body text never cites it (checked by search). The line continues in S134 (2014, card s2-04). **Read**: all 15 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Computing equilibria of extensive games usually goes through the strategic form, whose description is exponential in the tree size. Wilson and Koller–Megiddo keep supports small, and the sequence form (Romanovskii, Koller–Megiddo, von Stengel) is compact (pp. 2–3). The group's EχMIP enumerates all extreme equilibria of strategic-form games only (p. 2). The entry is therefore to carry EχMIP to the sequence form and see what it reveals.
- **D2 Core idea**: Write the sequence-form optimality conditions, the primal LPs (3), their duals (4) and complementarity (5), then linearize complementarity with binary vectors u₁, u₂ and constants L_i. Each L_i is the difference between the largest and the least entry of the payoff matrix (pp. 6–7). The result is a mixed 0-1 set Q (8), which the abstract says is expressed "for the first time to our knowledge" as a parametric linear 0-1 program (p. 1). The algorithm is re-implemented in **exact rational arithmetic** (BigInteger, Rational, a Simplex dictionary class and a Node class) because numerators and denominators overflowed INT_MAX (pp. 9–10). It uses principal branching on u and secondary branching x_k = 0 (pp. 10–11) and is released as the free XGame Solver (p. 10). The abstract mentions "a small procedure" that filters Ext(Q) down to Ext(P) (p. 1). The body does not state it formally; the examples filter duplicates (same X, different α) and convex combinations by inspection (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Finite two-person extensive games with perfect recall (pp. 3, 6). The simplex method returns vertices, which is what the enumeration relies on (p. 11).
- **D4 Proof device (brief)**: Theorem 1: P = Proj_x(Q) and Ext(P) ⊆ Proj_x(Ext(Q)) (p. 8). The equality follows from LP duality plus the exactness of the binary linearization. The inclusion is by contradiction: a non-extreme q ∈ Q projecting to an extreme X would write X as a convex combination of equilibria (p. 8). The authors then ask whether the inclusion is an equality and answer no with the two examples of §4 (p. 8). Completeness of EχMIP is taken from the 2006 JOTA proof (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two literature games, no timings.
  - von Stengel et al.: 3 extreme equilibria in the reduced strategic form (Table 1, p. 4) against 4 extreme points of Q, two of which share X (Table 2, p. 12).
  - Kohlberg–Mertens: 7 against 10 (Tables 3–4, p. 13). The sequence-form run exposes a subgame-perfect equilibrium that the strategic form missed (pp. 13–14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A background survey, the first mixed 0-1 formulation of the sequence form (as claimed), exact-arithmetic software, and a refinement insight (p. 14; Quote 1). Exact arithmetic "increases the overall enumeration time", and the authors deliberately set speed aside (p. 10; Quote 2). No future work is stated (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (outside DFO): an inclusion theorem followed at once by examples showing it cannot be strengthened to equality (p. 8, pp. 12–13).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: the algorithm ships as downloadable software, XGame Solver (p. 10). This is the solver-as-instrument habit in a non-DFO line.
- new-pattern candidate (evidence, second occurrence): **reformulation as a lens (transfer tests, not just solutions)** (b2-01, S070). The same enumeration engine applied to a different representation of the same game yields information (dual values at an information set) that selects subgame-perfect equilibria (pp. 13–14).
- new-pattern candidate: **exact rational arithmetic when the output is a complete list**. Correctness of an enumeration is put ahead of speed (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Linearize a complementarity condition x ⊥ s with a binary u: x + u ≤ 1 and s ≤ L·u, with L taken from the data range (here the payoff spread). This turns an equilibrium set into the projection of a mixed 0-1 polyhedral union (pp. 7–8).
- (D4) After proving an inclusion between two sets of extreme points, ask explicitly whether it is an equality, and settle it with the smallest literature examples (p. 8).
- (D5) Run the same enumeration on two representations of the same object and tabulate the extra points. The differences are themselves results (Tables 1–4, pp. 4, 12–13).

**Quotes**:
- "This example shows that when using the sequence form of the extensive game, extreme subgame perfect equilibria can be found while they might be ignored when only strategic form is used." (p. 14)
- "However, the use of exact arithmetics represented a very interesting challenge which made computing time not be considered as the main objective of this work." (p. 10)

### S080 · Isoperimetric polygons of maximum width (Discrete & Computational Geometry 2009, 10.1007/s00454-008-9103-9)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen (GERAD / HEC Montréal), F. Messine (ENSEEIHT-IRIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This paper answers the problem "posed in the survey [3]" (S040), where it was an open cell of Table I (S040 pp. 2, 4). The 2013 paper S117 (card s2-04) later chains this result with a maximum-perimeter result into a new bound. Received 17 July 2007, published online 22 August 2008 (p. 1). Funding: NSERC, AFOSR, ExxonMobil (p. 1). **Read**: all 16 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Which n-gon of unit perimeter has the largest width? "To the best of our knowledge, this problem is not mentioned elsewhere in the literature" apart from the survey (p. 2; Quote 1). The nearest known problem is Bezdek–Fodor's maximum width at unit diameter (p. 2). The authors describe the structure as "max min max" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The strategy is bound, then attainment, then the hard residue.
  1. An upper bound, w ≤ (1/(2n))·cot(π/(2n)), for every n (Theorem 1, pp. 3–5).
  2. Constructions that attain it: regular polygons for odd n (Prop. 2, p. 5), and clipped regular Reuleaux polygons for n = m·2^s with m odd (Prop. 3, pp. 5–6). Only powers of 2 remain.
  3. The quadrilateral. The subcase with a symmetry axis on a diagonal is solved analytically (Theorem 2, pp. 7–9), and the general case is certified numerically to 10⁻⁴ (Prop. 4, pp. 9–11).
  4. n = 2^s with s ≥ 3. Two formulations are given: a nonconvex QP with 3.5n² − 3.5n + 1 variables, and a concise NLP with n² + 1 variables and 2n + 1 constraints (Theorem 3 and (9), pp. 13–14). Near-optimal solutions come by four routes: adding a vertex to the regular (n−1)-gon, reusing Mossinghoff's unit-diameter polygons, global optimization on known diameter configurations, and local optimization of (9) from those starts (pp. 11–15).
- **D3 Assumptions**: WLOG convex and simple, since the convex hull followed by a homothety only helps (Prop. 1, p. 3). A limit argument allows assuming a unique farthest vertex per side, which licenses Bezdek–Fodor's lemma Σα_i = π (p. 3). The quadrilateral proof uses symmetry-based WLOG orderings of the vertex-to-line distances (p. 10).
- **D4 Proof device (brief)**: **Relax, equalize, collapse**, in four steps.
  1. Replace each side's height h_i by the upper bound (c_i/2)·cot(α_i/2) (p. 4).
  2. A two-side exchange shows that at the optimum all these bounds are equal: if two differ, redistribute c_i + c_j (p. 4).
  3. Sum the resulting tan(α_i/2) = c_i/(2h) identities, which leaves one constraint (pp. 4–5).
  4. Finish with Lassak's result (p. 5).
  Theorem 2 is a three-case KKT analysis (μ₁ = 0, μ₂ = 0, both positive) in which the first two cases collapse to the square or to contradictions (pp. 8–9). Prop. 4 is an interval branch-and-bound run of about 40 hours (p. 11). Theorem 3 encodes the inner max with convex multipliers λ_{i,k}, which gives the max-min-max a single-level form (pp. 13–14).
- **D5 Experiments**: Table 1 gives, for n = 8, 16, 32, the widths of five constructions against the upper bound, with gaps < 9.43×10⁻⁵, < 1.69×10⁻⁶ and < 1.43×10⁻⁸ (p. 12). fmincon started from Mossinghoff's polygons took 1 s, 20 s and 20 min, and random starts did not improve the results (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The problem is settled for all n except powers of 2, and the quadrilateral is certified to 10⁻⁴. For s ≥ 3, exact solutions "appear to be presently out of reach", so the paper supplies formulations and near-optimal values (p. 11). There is no conclusion section: the paper ends with Table 2 (p. 15). The abstract's extracted formula for the quadrilateral width reads "2(3√3 − 3)", which does not match the value ≈ 0.29498 in Theorem 2 and Prop. 4 (pp. 1, 7, 9). This may be a typo or an extraction artifact; it was not checked against the published PDF.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (outside DFO): exact results where a bound is attained. Where it is not, the paper reports a certified precision (10⁻⁴) or an explicit gap table, not an unqualified claim (pp. 9–12).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **catalogue open cells, then fill them**. The problem comes from the group's own survey (p. 2; S040 pp. 2, 4).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **analytic pruning before a global-solver certificate**. The symmetric subcase is solved by KKT first, then the 40-hour interval run certifies that symmetry loses nothing (pp. 7–11).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **extremal-geometry side line: numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later**. Here the order is reversed for n = 4: the analytic value comes first and the numeric certificate second (pp. 7–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) For an upper bound on a min over sides, relax each term to a closed-form bound, prove by a pairwise exchange that all terms are equal at the optimum, then sum to reduce to a single classical inequality (pp. 3–5).
- (D5) For the unsolved cases, publish a gap table: the value of each construction against the proven upper bound, per n (Table 1, p. 12).
- (D2) Solve the symmetric subcase analytically, then certify the general case with an interval global solver to a stated precision. The theorem is then honest about what is proved exactly and what numerically (pp. 7–11).

**Quotes**:
- "This problem, posed in the survey [3], consists in finding for all n which polygon with n vertices and unit perimeter has the largest width." (p. 2)
- "Observe the small values of the errors and their rapid decrease with increase of n." (p. 15)

### S103 · Simple polygons of maximum perimeter contained in a unit disk (Discrete & Computational Geometry 2009, 10.1007/s00454-008-9093-7)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen (GERAD), F. Messine (ENSEEIHT-IRIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: An extremal-polygon side-line note answering a question from Brass, Moser and Pach's *Research Problems in Discrete Geometry*, its only reference (pp. 1, 8). Received 18 September 2007, published online 20 June 2008 (p. 1). Funding: NSERC, AFOSR, ExxonMobil (p. 1). **Read**: all 8 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For a given n, find the simple n-gon inside a unit disk with the longest perimeter (p. 1; Quote 1). The question was posed on p. 437 of the problem book, which notes that for even n the answer is arbitrarily close to the trivial bound 2n, since a polygon can zig-zag across a diameter (pp. 1–2). The open part is odd n.
- **D2 Core idea**: Guess the shape from a construction, then prove matching bounds.
  - A lower bound, 2n − 2(2 − √2), comes from vertices alternating near (±1, 0) with one vertex at (0, 1) (p. 2).
  - A small displacement of the (±1, 0) vertices along the circle increases the perimeter, because the long edges have zero derivative and the short ones a positive derivative. This suggests the optimum is an isosceles triangle in the limit (p. 2).
  - An upper bound, minimized over one parameter v, gives the exact supremum p*_n in closed form. A sequence of polygons attains it in the limit (Theorem 1, pp. 7–8).
  - For n = 3 the answer is the equilateral triangle (p. 8). Corollary 1: p*_n/n is increasing over odd n (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions**: n odd. By relabeling, the longest edge is c_n ≥ p_n/n, the disk is centered at 0, and v₁, v_n are vertically aligned (p. 2). The answer is a **supremum**, not a maximum, because for n > 3 no optimal simple polygon exists (p. 2; Quote 2).
- **D4 Proof device (brief)**: A lemma chain.
  - Two zones U and L are cut from the disk. Two consecutive vertices in the same zone force p < p_n (Lemma 1, pp. 2–3).
  - A **parity argument**: with n odd, some three consecutive vertices are monotone in y (Lemma 2, pp. 3–4).
  - Convexity of the sum of distances puts the worst case on the boundary of the feasible region, x = u or the circle (p. 4). The first case is Lemma 3 (pp. 4–5); the second is solved by Lagrange multipliers, forcing a = 0 (Lemma 4, pp. 5–7).
  - Minimizing the bound over v leads to 2(n−2)²v² + v − 1 = 0 (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: "a complete answer" to a posed research problem (p. 1). No limitation or future work is stated, and there is no conclusion section (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (outside DFO): precision about what kind of optimum exists. The paper says "supremum" and constructs sequences that approach it, instead of claiming an optimal polygon (pp. 2, 8).
- new-pattern candidate: **problem-book sourcing**. Take problems from published open-problem collections (Brass–Moser–Pach here, p. 1; Croft–Falconer–Guy and Brass et al. in S040 p. 1 and S080 p. 2) and answer one completely in a short note.
- new-pattern candidate (evidence, variant): **catalogue open cells, then fill them**. The catalogue here is someone else's problem book rather than the group's own survey (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Before proving optimality, build a simple lower-bound construction and differentiate the objective along a small perturbation of it. If the perturbation helps, the direction points to the optimal shape (p. 2).
- (D4) With an odd number of objects around a cycle, a parity argument often gives three consecutive ones in monotone order, which is a foothold for a local exchange bound (Lemma 2, pp. 3–4).
- (D3) If the extremum is approached but not attained, state the result as a supremum and give the explicit sequence that approaches it (pp. 2, 8).

**Quotes**:
- "In this paper, we give a complete answer to the question posed by Brass, Moser and Pach [1, p. 437]: for a given integer n, find a simple n-sided planar polygon contained in a disk of radius 1 that has the longest perimeter." (p. 1)
- "We use the term supremum instead of maximum since we will see that there are no optimal simple polygons when n> 3." (p. 2)
