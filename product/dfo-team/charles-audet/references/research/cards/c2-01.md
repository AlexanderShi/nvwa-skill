### S010 · A branch and cut algorithm for nonconvex quadratically constrained quadratic programming (Mathematical Programming 2000, 10.1007/s101079900106)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen, B. Jaumard, G. Savard · **Position in trajectory**: This is Rice CRPC technical report CRPC-TR99783-S, dated January 1999 and submitted January 21, 1999 (pp. 1–2). Audet's address is Rice CAAM; his support is an NSERC fellowship, and the paper was partly funded by Ultramar Canada (p. 2), the same funder as the pooling paper S015 (card c01). The QQP reformulations of the test problems are detailed in Audet [5], his 1997 Polytechnique thesis *Optimisation globale structurée* (pp. 25, 27). So this is the thesis-era exact global-optimization work. Its code is the exact solver used later in S015 (c01). **Read**: all 30 pages via OCR of the PDF (text file garbled); references (pp. 27–30) skimmed. Formulas in Props 4.1–4.5 (pp. 13–16) and Tables 1–2 (p. 26) were checked against the page images. Figure 3, the tree drawing (p. 24), was not read beyond its caption and the surrounding text.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: QQP is presented as a structured problem that *encompasses* mixed 0-1, fractional, bilinear, bilevel and linear-complementarity programs (p. 4). Its difficulty is stated on two levels: finding a feasible point is NP-hard (it generalizes LCP), and optimizing over a polyhedron is strongly NP-hard (it subsumes disjoint bilinear programming). The authors conclude that a finite exact algorithm for large QQPs *is probably out of reach* (p. 4). The only assumption is that finite bounds on every variable can be obtained (pp. 4, 10). The entry is incremental and says so: the RLT approach *is not new, but is extended here in several ways* (p. 5). The extensions are parameterized cut classes including one new class, a *natural question* of how to pick the best cut in each class, the first branch and cut for QQP, and reuse of branching structure across nodes (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: when your method family is crowded, locate the paper on a question the family has not posed (which member of this cut family is best, and by what criterion) rather than on a new family.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Four linearization classes (pp. 10–12).
  - C_I is the tangent under-estimators of x_i², after Al-Khayyal–Falk.
  - C_II is new: tangent planes to the convex paraboloid (x_i + γx_j)², valid everywhere (p. 10).
  - C_III is the secant over-estimators of x_i² (p. 11).
  - C_IV is the four quadrant-wise convex/concave-hull inequalities for x_i x_j (p. 12).

  C_III and C_IV carry dichotomy variables δ_i(α) ∈ [0,1]. Their right-hand sides make each cut redundant while δ is free and active only when δ is fixed at 0 or 1. As a result, every cut is valid in the whole tree, and the best-first search can jump between branches without adding and deleting inequalities (p. 11; restated after the algorithm, p. 21). Cut parameters are chosen by a minimax criterion (§4, pp. 12–16):
  - For C_I, α = (√2−1)x̂_i + (2−√2)√v̂_i minimizes max(e₁, αe₂) (Prop 4.1, p. 13).
  - For C_II, γ = −τ_ij/τ_j gives the deepest cut (Prop 4.2, p. 14), and the tangent point is the midpoint (Prop 4.3, pp. 14–15).
  - Over-estimators follow the same pattern (Props 4.4–4.5, pp. 15–16).

  Branching picks the variable with the largest linearization error and prefers an existing branching value whose δ is still free. The whole structure is then reused (pp. 18–19), and the rule is *discrepancy based* (p. 19). Bound tightening runs only at the root. Running it at other nodes *would change the algorithm from a branch and cut one into a branch and bound one*, because some cuts would become local (p. 18). What is kept: the RLT relaxation and the Sherali–Tuncbilek range reduction (pp. 8–9). What is changed: global validity of cuts, reuse of structure, and a criterion for choosing each cut.
  - Transferable technique: to make node-local cuts globally valid, gate them with a switching variable that makes them redundant when free. Then one LP model serves the whole tree and a branching structure built at one node can be reused at another.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Variables are bounded, with x ≥ 0 present or implied, and valid upper bounds are computable (p. 4). The iterative bounding of §2.3 obtains them, and Example 2.2 converges to the exact interval [1,2] (p. 9). The solution concept is explicitly approximate. A point is ε_r-approximate if |x̂_i² − v̂_i| ≤ ε_r and |x̂_i x̂_j − ŵ_ij| ≤ ε_r; it is ε_r-ε_z-optimal if, in addition, it is within ε_z of the best value over ε_r-approximate points (Def 5.1, p. 17). The abstract advertises this double tolerance, *with respect to feasibility and optimality* (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Validity of the initial relaxation: substitute v* = x*², w* = x*x* into any optimum and it stays feasible with the same value (Prop 2.1, p. 8).
  - Every cut-selection result is proved by an equalization argument. One error term increases monotonically from 0 and the other decreases to 0 as the parameter moves across an interval, so the minimax is attained where they are equal. The optimal parameter is then the root, in that interval, of a quadratic (pp. 13, 15–16). The observation is stated once and reused (*Slight variations of the following observation*, p. 13).
  - Finiteness (Thm 5.2, pp. 21–22): pre-processing stops once no bound improves by ε_r. Each son linearizes an interval of length ε_r/u_i to within the tolerance, so each variable receives only finitely many cuts. Validity of all inequalities means the ε-optimum is never cut off.
  - There is no complexity bound and no rate.
  - Transferable technique: when a design parameter trades off two error terms that move monotonically in opposite directions, pick it by equalizing them. The resulting proposition has a one-paragraph proof and gives a closed form you can implement.
- **D5 Experiments**: C++ with CPLEX 4.0 on an Ultra-1/167 (p. 22).
  - Worked example: a quadratic reformulation of HS71 (Bartholomew-Biggs). Pre-processing (2.69 s) tightens the bounds of x5 and x6 to [1.33975, 18.6603]. The tree explores 69 nodes, adds 25 δ variables and 599 cuts in 4.49 s, and finds objective 17.014 (p. 23).
  - Ablation of the paper's own design choice: midpoint branching values instead of the error-minimizing ones give 189 nodes, 55 δ variables and 1076 cuts (p. 23).
  - Tree anatomy: cut counts per class at each node show that most cuts come from C_I/C_II, and nodes 6, 11, 20, 21, 18, 19 and 31 reuse existing branching structure (pp. 23–24).
  - Seven literature problems (Haverly pooling, Colville, Avriel–Williams, Bartholomew-Biggs, Bracken–McCormick alkylation, two Dembo membrane problems) are reformulated as QQP with Hansen–Jaumard techniques and monotonicity analysis (p. 24). The largest has 16 variables, 24 quadratic terms and 42 + 11 constraints (Table 1, p. 26).
  - Table 2 reports the number of variables not at a bound at optimality (a difficulty indicator taken from the survey, pp. 6, 26), δ variables, added constraints, nodes (7–2847) and pre-processing/tree/total seconds (0.65–7551 s). The tolerance is loosened from 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁵ for problems 5–7, and the table states this (p. 26).
  - There is no run of another code. The comparison is to published solutions (see D6), and no reformulation alternatives were compared (p. 25).
  - Transferable technique: ablate your own design choice (here, error-minimizing versus midpoint branching) on the worked example, and report structural counts (nodes, added variables, cuts), not only time.
- **D6 Positioning**: The survey (§2.1, pp. 6–7) is neutral and sorted by technique: the linearization/RLT line (Al-Khayyal–Falk; Al-Khayyal; Al-Khayyal–Larsen–Van Voorhis; Sherali–Alameddine; Sherali–Tuncbilek; Ryoo–Sahinidis), generalized Benders, and d.c. programming. None is criticized; each is summarized by what it linearizes and where it branches. The paper adopts Sherali–Tuncbilek notation [·]_ℓ (p. 8). Results are positioned against published solutions (p. 27). They confirm that the Hock–Schittkowski heuristic solutions of problems 3 and 4 are ε-optimal, slightly improve problem 6, and note that problem 5 was previously solved only to a 5% tolerance. They claim problems 6 and 7 are solved to ε-optimality *for the first time* (also in the abstract, p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution paragraph is an enumerated First/Second/Third list with the novelty marked (*contains a new type of linearization*, p. 5). One figure per error-minimization result shows both error terms graphically (Fig 1, p. 13; Fig 2, p. 17). The tree-anatomy observations are written as short declarative bullets (*This is a key element to keep reasonable the size of the relaxation*, p. 24). Hedging is low. The only hedge is the *probably out of reach* framing of large instances (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: The contributions are algorithmic (cut classes, global validity, structure reuse), theoretical (finite ε-convergence) and computational (first certified optima on two literature problems) (pp. 3, 5, 27). Limitations are stated concretely: large QQPs are *probably out of reach* (p. 4); difficulty tracks the number of variables not at their bounds (p. 26); reformulation choice was not studied (p. 25). The future work is generic: extend *such linearization approaches for other classes of problems, and to compare the results with alternate methods* (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder bounded by counterexamples): variant ⚠. The guarantee is stated precisely, with a double tolerance (Def 5.1, p. 17; Thm 5.2, pp. 21–22), but as a single finite-termination result. There is no ladder and no test of whether the assumptions are necessary.
- Method 5 (Budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking): variant ⚠. The currency is nodes, cuts and CPU seconds, not evaluations. The paper includes a self-ablation (p. 23) and a comparison to published solutions (p. 27), but no competitor run on the same machine.
- Method 4 (Real problems → benchmark artifacts): weak evidence ✅. Industrial-origin instances (Haverly pooling from the petrochemical industry, Procter & Gamble, alkylation, membrane separation; pp. 25–26) are reformulated and their data printed in full for the largest one (p. 25).
- New-pattern candidate: **minimax error equalization for parameter choice** (Props 4.1–4.5, pp. 13–16).
- New-pattern candidate: **globally valid local cuts via switching variables** (pp. 11, 21).
- New-pattern candidate: **certify or improve the published solutions of literature instances** (p. 27). Also seen in S015 (card c01), where literature instances are audited.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Gate node-specific cuts with a [0,1] switching variable so they are valid tree-wide and the branching structure can be reused (pp. 11, 21).
- Choose each cut or branching parameter by equalizing two monotone error terms, and prove it in one paragraph (p. 13).
- Report a problem-difficulty indicator next to the performance numbers: here, the number of variables not at a bound at optimality, borrowed from prior work (pp. 6, 26).
- Ablate one of your own heuristics on the showcase instance (error-minimizing vs midpoint: 69 vs 189 nodes, p. 23).

**Quotes**: none. The text file is garbled font output, so no sentence can be grep-verified.

### S011 · Pattern search algorithms for mixed variable programming (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2001, 10.1137/s1052623499352024)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J.E. Dennis Jr. · **Position in trajectory**: The file is the Rice CAAM technical report TR99-02 (the title-page footnote in the PDF OCR, p. 1, cites it as 2000). Funding: NSERC fellowship, DOE, AFOSR, Boeing, Sandia, ExxonMobil and NSF CRPC (p. 1). The application comes from collaborator M. Kokkolaras, who "suggested the thermal insulation problem" (p. 22). Its engineering companion is Kokkolaras–Audet–Dennis (ref [8], p. 22). This is the first Audet paper on categorical variables; SKILL.md's trajectory later leads to the 2023 mixed-variable framework and CatMADS. **Read**: all 22 pages. Text-file caveat: digits, Greek letters, ligatures (fi/fl) and much punctuation are missing, so formulas are unreadable in the text file. Theorem numbers and the numbers of §5.2 were read from OCR of the PDF. Figures 3.1–3.2 (p. 9) and 5.1 (p. 20) were read only through their captions and the surrounding text.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Engineering problems contain *a special kind of discrete variable that can be represented by a number* with no significance, such as a choice from an unordered list of categories (abstract, p. 1). The abstract draws two implications: continuous relaxation is unavailable, and local optimality *must be defined through a user-specified set of neighboring points* (p. 1). Branch and bound is not rejected on principle: when it can be used *it probably should be, but that is not the issue here* (p. 2). The blackbox argument decides it: variables go as input to a simulation that expects a discrete input (p. 2). The anchor application is a thermal-insulation design in which the number of shields n is itself a variable, so the dimension depends on a decision variable (pp. 4–5). Treating the two categorical variables gives an objective 65% better than the continuous-only Hilal–Boom design (p. 4, digits from PDF OCR).
  - Transferable technique: justify a new problem class by an input-type argument about the simulator (it cannot accept a relaxed value), and concede the classical method where it applies.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Local optimality is relative to a user-defined neighbor map N(x). The definition requires f(x̂) ≤ f(v) on balls around every neighbor, with the continuous part free (Def 2.1, p. 3). N must be continuous along converging sequences (p. 4). Constraints such as being a permutation can be encoded in N itself, as in the QAP example (p. 3). The GMVP algorithm (p. 10) keeps GPS's structure. The Search is any finite strategy on the mesh and acts as a "failsafe"-protected heuristic slot (pp. 5–6, 11). The Poll has up to three stages (p. 7):
  1. the continuous poll with positive spanning sets;
  2. a poll over the discrete neighbors N_k;
  3. an extended poll: a continuous descent from each neighbor y with f(y) < f(x_k) + ξ, ending at a point z whose whole mesh neighborhood is evaluated (p. 8).

  The mesh is a union of finitely many lattices in the continuous space times ℤ^{nd} (p. 6). Near a bound, the poll must use a maximal positive basis built from two diagonal matrices (p. 7). The mesh-reduction exponent may now vary with k, a relaxation of Torczon's rule (pp. 10–11). A costlier "strong extended poll" does complete polling with a fixed basis at each extended step (p. 16). What is kept: Lewis–Torczon GPS, which GMVP reduces to when nd = 0 (p. 2). What is added: N, ξ and the extended poll. The user "knobs" (N, ξ, the Search) are named explicitly as ways to "pay more function evaluations for a stronger local solution" (p. 11; also pp. 3, 8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: (A1) the level set L_ξ(x₀) is compact. (A2) f is C¹ in the continuous variables for fixed discrete variables (p. 10). The continuous variables are bound constrained, and infeasible trial points get a large value, as in Lewis–Torczon (p. 2). The analysis uses the mean value theorem, not Clarke calculus (pp. 14–17), so this paper sits before S002's nonsmooth reanalysis. The authors concede that the notation abuse xₖ ∈ ℝ^{nc} × ℤ^{nd} is for explication (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Δ_k is bounded above (Lemma 4.1, p. 12). The proof uses the finite number of discrete values in the compact level set.
  - liminf Δ_k = 0 (Thm 4.2, p. 12) comes from the rational-τ integer-lattice argument, "essentially identical to that of Torczon". The *key to our simpler proof is to conclude from this that there is an infinite number of unsuccessful iterations* (p. 11).
  - A limit x̂ of unsuccessful iterates with Δ→0, with converging neighbors and extended-poll endpoints, is extracted (Prop 4.3, p. 13).
  - f(x̂) ≤ f(y) for all y ∈ N(x̂) is shown by contradiction via continuity of N (Thm 4.4, p. 14).
  - Two lemmas (interior and boundary) use the mean value theorem and the positive-spanning property (Lemmas 4.5–4.6, pp. 14–15). They give first-order conditions at x̂ (Thm 4.7) and at the extended-poll endpoints z (Thm 4.8, p. 15).
  - f is constant at infinitely many points between y and z (Prop 4.9, pp. 15–16).
  - The strong version adds a sufficient-decrease-type bound for extended-poll steps (Lemma 4.10, p. 16) and a gradient-continuity lemma (Lemma 4.11, p. 17). The proof concludes with a telescoping-sum contradiction that gives ∇_c f(y) = 0 at neighbors with f(y) = f(x̂) (Thm 4.12, pp. 17–18).
  - The limits of the guarantees are delegated to the author's TR: no lim ‖∇f‖ = 0 and no second-order conditions (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: when adding a new algorithmic component (here the extended poll), prove what the cheap version gives (Prop 4.9), then show what extra evaluations buy (Thm 4.12), and exhibit an example that separates the two (§5.1).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Toy example: 2 continuous variables and 1 binary, no Search, ξ = 1 (pp. 18–19). Worked iterations and Table 5.1 show cycles of two successful iterations and one unsuccessful one. With the cheap extended poll, the iterates approach a segment on which the gradient is nonzero at y; the strong version finds an improving point at iteration 2 and converges to the global minimizer (pp. 19–20).
  - Thermal insulation (pp. 20–22), four runs: ξ = 1% or 5% of f, each with and without insulator knowledge used to prune the discrete poll. The initial mesh is 10 and is never increased. The Search is a single step further along the last successful direction (p. 21). The curves plot objective against function evaluations with the number of shields annotated (Fig 5.2, p. 21).
  - Results (digits from PDF OCR, p. 22). The best point was found after 2639 evaluations, and 2083 more certified it as a local mesh optimizer (4722 in total). The restricted neighbor set reached the same optimizer with 10% fewer evaluations (2345 + 1861). ξ = 5% reached a solution more than 6% better after 13329 evaluations, or 10053 with the pruned neighbors (about 25% fewer). All runs ended with 9 shields. The difference in objective values is read as evidence of local optima (p. 22).
  - One run per setting; no competitor. The authors warn that "it would be simple to construct examples showing the opposite behavior" to the ξ trade-off (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: split the reported cost into evaluations to find the solution and evaluations to certify it (as a local mesh optimizer). This exposes what the stopping rule costs.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues Torczon and Lewis–Torczon and aims "to revise and shorten the arguments" (p. 2), claiming *a much shorter proof than in [12] and [9]* (p. 11). Booker et al.'s surrogate framework is named as the intended Search content (pp. 10–11). No mixed-integer or metaheuristic literature is discussed beyond the branch-and-bound remark (p. 2) and the QAP illustration (p. 3). The reference list has 12 items (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper talks to the user. It asks *What property must the solution provided by the algorithm satisfy in order for it to be a satisfactory local solution?* (p. 3; wording from the OCR, because the text file drops the question mark), and it names the controls "knobs" (pp. 3, 8). A paragraph addresses reader confusion about "unsuccessful" iterations ("pejorative connotation") and floats inner/outer or minor/major as labels without imposing them (p. 11). A plain-spoken aside: *the purpose of notation is to explicate, not to be pedantic* (p. 2; OCR wording, since the text file drops the commas).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: The contributions are an algorithm class, theory (a first-order and neighbor-optimality ladder, with a stronger rung for extra cost) and an engineering demonstration (pp. 2, 4, 20–22). The limitations are explicit. Optimality is only relative to the user's N (pp. 3–4). Stronger guarantees cost more evaluations (pp. 3, 11, 16). No second-order conditions are possible for a function-value method (p. 15). Future work: vary the mesh-reduction exponent, *We plan to exploit this flexibility in subsequent work to increase the practical convergence speed* (p. 11, OCR wording).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll): evidence ✅. "Any strategy can be used", and "if this fails the next phase will provide a failsafe" (p. 5). The Search "determines in practice the global quality of the solution" while the Poll "guarantees theoretical convergence" (p. 6). User heuristics, interpolatory models and surrogates are admitted into the Search (p. 11).
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder): evidence ✅. Conditions (2.1)–(2.5) form a ladder in which each rung costs more polling (p. 4; Thm 4.12, p. 18). A separating example is included (pp. 18–20), and the tightness TR is cited (p. 15).
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first): early variant ⚠. Categorical variables get their own semantics (a neighbor map, not a relaxation) because the simulator cannot accept relaxed inputs (p. 2). Constraints on the categorical structure are encoded in N (p. 3).
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → benchmark artifacts): variant ⚠. There is a real collaborator problem (pp. 4–5, 22), but it is not released as an artifact in this paper.
- New-pattern candidate: **user-defined optimality notion as a priced knob** (N, ξ; pp. 3, 8, 11). It prefigures the CatMADS neighborhoods named in SKILL.md.
- New-pattern candidate: **report cost-to-find vs cost-to-certify** (p. 22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define local optimality for non-metric variables through a user neighbor map plus a continuity condition on that map, and prove optimality relative to it (Def 2.1, pp. 3–4).
- Add an optional, threshold-triggered extended poll (ξ) that buys a stronger guarantee only where a discrete neighbor is nearly as good (p. 8).
- Report evaluations-to-find and evaluations-to-certify separately (p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "It would be surprising if one could run the simulation code with a continuous variable where the simulation expects a discrete input" (p. 2)
- "which is as it should be for an algorithm that uses only function values and no derivatives" (p. 15)

### S002 · Analysis of generalized pattern searches (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2002, 10.1137/s1052623400378742)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J.E. Dennis Jr. · **Position in trajectory**: A preprint with pages numbered 1–15. Audet is at École Polytechnique/GERAD, with the work done during the NSERC postdoc at Rice (p. 1). S005 cites it as SIAM J. Optim. 13, 889–903 (2003) (S005, p. 27). It builds on two Audet reports: the 1998 tightness TR (ref [1], p. 14; see S025) and the mixed-variable paper (ref [2]; see S011). It announces the filter paper (ref [3]; see S005). SKILL.md treats it as the start of Method 3's ladder. **Read**: all 15 pages, references included.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is empirical success without theory. GPS with surrogates is "extremely effective" on expensive engineering problems (p. 1), yet in them the evaluation "may result unexpectedly in no value being returned even for a feasible x". An example is a helicopter-rotor design where that happens "roughly 66% of the time" (p. 2). Global smoothness, even continuity, cannot be assumed, and "previous convergence theorems do not apply to such problems" (p. 2). The stated goal is to "understand the successful behavior of the algorithm under hypotheses that are satisfied by many practical problems" (abstract, p. 1), and to give "a new simpler unified analysis" of Torczon and Lewis–Torczon (p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: motivate a reanalysis by a documented gap between practice and hypotheses. Here it is a quantified failure rate on a real design problem, plus an unpublished diagnostic plot showing lower semicontinuity at a limit point (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The algorithm is not changed. The paper uses "a slightly different, but equivalent version" (p. 3), with the mesh written as M_k = {x_k + Δ_k D z : z ∈ ℤ₊^{|D|}}, where D = GZ̄ (p. 4). The changes are in the lens:
  - Constraints and failures are folded into a barrier f_Ω = f + ψ_Ω, so the methods of Torczon and Lewis–Torczon become one algorithm applied to an extended-valued function (p. 2).
  - The results are restructured in the Newton style: a limit point exists without assumptions, and stronger conclusions follow from stronger local assumptions at that point (p. 2).
  - The analysis focuses on *refining subsequences* of mesh local optimizers with Δ_k → 0 (Def 3.5, p. 8).
  - The iteration terminology changes from successful/unsuccessful to *improved mesh point* / *mesh local optimizer*, because the unsuccessful iterations "were the important ones" (p. 3).

  The Search is defended as the practical engine: it is "a key point" that accommodates the user's heuristics; it is "definitely a theoretical liability", yet "must be retained" (p. 3). "The convergence analysis is independent of the search step, provided that it is finite and returns a point (or points) on the mesh" (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - A1: f_Ω and a start x₀ with a finite value.
  - A2: the constraint matrix A is rational.
  - A3: the iterates lie in a compact set (p. 6).

  Compactness of L(x₀) is deliberately not assumed, since f may be discontinuous or infinite; only boundedness is assumed (pp. 5–6). All smoothness assumptions are local at the limit point: lower semicontinuity, Lipschitz, regularity, strict differentiability (pp. 6, 10, 12). Continuous differentiability on a neighborhood is used only to recover earlier results as corollaries, "only to tie our results here to earlier results" (p. 11). For linear constraints, the direction rule must "conform" to Ω within ε (Def 3.13, p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Lemma 3.2 (p. 7): mesh points are separated by at least Δ_k/‖G⁻¹‖, because Z̄(z_u − z_v) is a nonzero integer vector. The dense-lattice example D = [−1, π] shows why integrality is needed.
  - Lemma 3.3: Δ_k is bounded above by compactness (pp. 7–8).
  - Prop 3.4: liminf Δ_k = 0 by contradiction (p. 8). With τ = p/q rational, all iterates lie on one translated integer lattice, so there are finitely many iterates and one repeats infinitely often.
  - Thm 3.6 (p. 8): a convergent refining subsequence exists.
  - The key result, Thm 3.7 (p. 9), is almost a one-liner. Failed polls at mesh local optimizers supply exactly the nonnegative difference quotients that sit under the lim sup in Clarke's definition, giving f°(x̂; d) ≥ 0 for directions polled infinitely often. The authors read this as "an intimate relationship between Clarke’s generalized directional derivatives and the directional algorithm GPS" (p. 10).
  - Thm 3.9: strict differentiability plus a positive spanning set gives ∇f(x̂) = 0 (p. 10).
  - Cors 3.11–3.12 recover Torczon's result (pp. 11–12).
  - Thm 3.14 gives KKT points under conforming directions, and Cor 3.16 recovers Lewis–Torczon (p. 13).
  - The whole result is summarized as a seven-rung hierarchy (i)–(vii) from lower semicontinuity to global C¹ (p. 12).
  - Each rung is backed by an example. Ex 3.8, f = x²(2 + sin(π/x)), is Lipschitz but not strictly differentiable at 0, with ∂f(0) = [−π, π]; a four-iteration cycle table shows convergence to 0 (pp. 9–10). Ex 3.10, from Hiriart-Urruty–Lemaréchal, is strictly differentiable at 0 but not C¹ nearby (pp. 10–11). The third example, f = |x₁| + |x₂| with ill-chosen D and x₀ = (1,0), never moves, "and describes exactly what happens" (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: if your algorithm's certificate at a failed iteration is a set of nonnegative difference quotients, match it to a generalized derivative defined as a lim sup of such quotients. The main theorem then follows from the definition, and the remaining work is only to show the right subsequence exists.
- **D5 Experiments**: None; the paper is theory with analytic examples. Evidence from practice is cited, not run: the Boeing surrogate-based GPS is "much more efficient" than Serafini's MDS/PDS implementation, "This is not to disparage the MDS algorithm" (p. 3). A plot of the heat-intercept objective was made "for our own understanding and not published" (p. 2).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues and unifies Torczon and Lewis–Torczon, giving "previous results as corollaries" (abstract, p. 1; pp. 11–13). Coope–Price is handled respectfully as an alternative trade-off: their framework relaxes the mesh but assumes the meshes become infinitely fine, which puts "the burden for ensuring that the meshes become infinitely fine into the implementation" (p. 7); they "apply the Clarke analysis given here" (p. 3). The paper closes by sorting routes to general constraints. The barrier is "appealingly simple" but insufficient, because a finite D cannot generate the tangent cone at every boundary point of a non-polygonal region. The alternatives are the authors' own filter GPS [3], Lewis–Torczon's augmented Lagrangian [26] and Coope–Price's work in progress (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is explicit and appears in the abstract: "The contribution of this paper is to provide a simple convergence analysis that supplies detail about the relation of optimality conditions to objective smoothness properties and to the defining directions for the algorithm" (p. 1). Counter-intuitive consequences are flagged as such: stationary points are "the least interesting locally smooth limit points" (p. 6). Overclaiming is disowned: "We certainly are not claiming that the weaker smoothness conditions we use imply that GPS methods always find a minimizer" (p. 11). All proofs are included "even if some of them essentially can be found in previous work", because simplification is the goal (p. 5). The ratio of theorems to intuition is high on the intuition side: every major result is followed by a discussion or an example.
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: The contribution is theoretical: a unified, simpler analysis and a smoothness-dependent hierarchy (pp. 1, 12). The limitations are concrete. Results hold only for directions used infinitely often (p. 9). With a finite D, the barrier cannot handle general constraints (p. 14). Nonstationary limit points can exist even for C¹ f (p. 12, item vii). There are no rates. The future direction is general constraints via the filter (p. 14).

**Method links**:

There is the hierarchy (i)–(vii) (p. 12), examples chosen to show that each result applies where earlier ones do not (pp. 7, 9–11), and the claim that the results are "sharp in that they predict the behavior of the algorithm" (p. 3).

### S005 · A pattern search filter method for nonlinear programming without derivatives (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2004, 10.1137/s105262340138983x)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J.E. Dennis Jr. · **Position in trajectory**: Preprint dated June 12, 2003 (running header, p. 2). Audet is at GERAD/Polytechnique (FCAR and NSERC grants plus the Rice postdoc fellowship, p. 1). It is announced in S002's conclusion as the route to general constraints (S002, p. 14). It is implemented in M. Abramson's MATLAB NOMADm (p. 3). Boeing's Design Explorer uses it "as a meta algorithm in the surrogate management framework" (p. 3). SKILL.md lists it as the 2004 filter GPS that the progressive barrier (2009) later superseded as NOMAD's default. **Read**: all 28 pages, references included. The figure coordinates (pp. 21–26) were read through the surrounding text.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min f(x) s.t. C(x) ≤ 0, x ∈ X, where X is a full-dimensional polyhedron. f and C may take the value ∞, and linear constraints hidden inside the blackbox stay in C (p. 1). The entry is a gap between two families.
  - Fletcher–Leyffer filter methods avoid penalty parameters but need derivatives, sufficient decrease and Fritz John points (pp. 1–2).
  - Lewis–Torczon's augmented-Lagrangian GPS needs C² functions, a full-rank constraint Jacobian, penalty parameters and multiplier estimates (p. 2).
  - Blackboxes "may fail to return a value a significant percentage of times" and noise prevents approximate derivatives (p. 2).

  The paper settles for "weakened local optimality convergence results" and lists six features: derivative-free, transparent generalization of GPS, filter acceptance with no penalty constants, assumptions that fit practical instances, simple decrease, no constraint qualification (pp. 2–3). The method is not oversold: "we do not claim that our method is to be preferred for every problem" (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: position a new method between two families by listing, in one table-like bullet list, exactly which requirements of each family you drop, and which weaker guarantee you accept in exchange.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - GPS keeps its mesh, positive spanning sets and Search/Poll division ("a key issue for us", p. 3). Only the step-acceptance test changes to a filter on (h_X, f).
  - Dominance is redefined in the "standard multiobjective" way, with the warning that filter readers "should read this section carefully" (pp. 5–6).
  - The filter holds only infeasible points. The best feasible point is a separate "single point filter", which avoids blocking entries and encourages moving "over an infeasible function ridge" (p. 6).
  - Linear constraints are kept by barrier through h_X = h + ψ_X, so points outside X are never evaluated (pp. 6–7). Linear equalities should be used to eliminate variables (p. 7).
  - There are two incumbents, the best feasible point and the least-infeasible point. The poll center may be either, and the choice rule is left to the user, though arguments are given against always picking one (pp. 8–9, 22).
  - The mesh becomes the union of meshes around all evaluated points S_k, which lets the Search poll around any trial point (eq. 4.1, p. 8).
  - Only simple decrease is required; no envelope is added to the filter to force sufficient decrease (p. 7).
  - Cheap filtering avoids spending evaluations: check x ∈ X first, then stop evaluating the c_i once a partial sum of the squared violations already exceeds h_max (p. 9).
  - The recommended violation measure is h = ‖C(x)₊‖²₂ (p. 6), justified in §5.2 and §7.2.
  - Transferable technique: before evaluating an expensive blackbox, test the cheap parts (explicit linear constraints, partial constraint outputs) that already decide rejection (p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: All trial points lie in a compact set, the standard assumption (p. 11). X has nondegenerate constraints (p. 1). Results are local: Lipschitz near x̂ for the Clarke results, strict differentiability for gradient results (pp. 12–19). No constraint qualification is assumed (p. 3). The authors admit that h itself is a modeling choice: "specifying a constraint violation function implies assigning relative weights to reducing each constraint" (p. 1). They also admit the Lipschitz framing is partly for analysis: "there are some optimization problems on which we would apply our algorithm where the functions are not Lipschitz", and "But this is beside the point" (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Mesh results are inherited: Lemmas 4.2–4.3 are stated with proofs omitted as "identical" to S002 (p. 11).
  - A new analytic apparatus is built:
    - Lemma 5.3: Clarke's derivative with a perturbed direction w → v (pp. 12–13).
    - Prop 5.4: necessary conditions on the contingent cone (p. 13).
    - Lemma 5.5: for any monotone nonincreasing sequence converging to x̂, the normalized limit directions have g°(x̂; v) ≥ 0. It turns any merit-decreasing sequence into optimality directions (pp. 13–14).
    - Prop 5.6: a generalized-gradient formula showing h₁ = ‖C₊‖₁ is rarely strictly differentiable (pp. 14–15).
  - Def 5.7 introduces *refining directions* R_g(K), differences of two polled points with ordered values, and *limit directions* L_g(K) (p. 15, Fig 5.1 p. 16). Thm 5.9 gives g°(x̂; v) ≥ 0 on cone(R ∪ L) (p. 16).
  - Results for h are stronger than for f, because feasibility has priority (Thm 6.1, Prop 6.2, Cor 6.3, pp. 17–18). For f, first-order conditions hold when x̂ is strictly feasible w.r.t. Ω (Prop 6.5, Cor 6.6, pp. 18–19). In general, −∇f(x̂) lies in the polar of C_f = cc(R_f ∪ L_f), which gives KKT when that cone contains the contingent cone (Prop 6.7, p. 19).
  - Prop 6.8 (p. 20) is an anti-stalling result: if f and h are strictly differentiable at the poll center and ∇f ≠ 0, the poll center cannot remain a mesh isolated filter point forever.
  - Transferable technique: when a single poll direction set is too weak, enlarge the certified set with "refining directions" built from pairs of evaluated points with ordered values, and with "limit directions" of monotone sequences. Both come free from points already evaluated (Def 5.7, Lemma 5.5).
- **D5 Experiments**: Four illustrations, each built to make one point (p. 3):
  - (7.1) The Lewis–Torczon LP, with the constraints treated as blackboxes (pp. 20–22). A barrier GPS stalls at the origin. An ℓ₁ exact penalty with any constant ≥ 3 also never moves, while a constant above 2 is needed for exactness. The filter reaches the optimum (1,0) and the KKT conditions, with iterates and filters drawn (Figs 7.1–7.2).
  - (7.2) An LP where ℓ₁ violation stalls at an infeasible point and squared ℓ₂ escapes once Δ_k < 2/3 (pp. 22–24).
  - (7.3) A "limitation" example (pp. 24–25). A legal but unlucky choice of directions produces three-iteration cycles converging to a feasible point with a feasible descent direction used infinitely often, so Prop 6.7 is sharp.
  - (7.4) A Boeing wing-planform problem with 17 variables and 13 nonlinear constraints (pp. 25–26). The start is the best point of a kriging surrogate built from 200 orthogonal-array Latin-hypercube points: infeasible, h = 0.426, f = 9.845. The initial point is filtered at the 3rd evaluation, the first feasible point appears at the 58th, and the run stops after 117 evaluations beyond the initial 200. The best feasible value is f = 9.75, and filter snapshots are shown at 15, 50 and 117 evaluations (Fig 7.5).
  - There is no competitor solver, a single run, and the counts are in evaluations.
  - The Discussion (§7.5, p. 27) says the academic examples "were designed to illustrate the tightness" and lists what a real run would add: a space-filling LH or surrogate Search, more poll directions, and alternating poll centers.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on Fletcher–Leyffer(–Toint) for acceptance and on S002 for analysis. It re-states rather than criticizes. Their dominance notion is replaced for consistency with multiobjective terminology, in anticipation of "a truly multiobjective GPS algorithm in later work" (p. 5). It defends filter theory's weak Fritz John results with history: "It is well to remember that the theoretical justification for quasi-Newton methods trailed their efficacy by several years" (p. 2). It treats Lewis–Torczon's augmented Lagrangian as a legitimate alternative for smoother problems (p. 2) and Coope–Price as shifting "the burden from the algorithm specification to the implementation" (p. 11). Practitioners are credited by name: Paul Frank at Boeing suggested polling around the next most feasible filter point (pp. 10, 27).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The features come as a bullet list in the introduction (pp. 2–3). The examples section opens by announcing what each example proves (p. 20). Limitations are written as confessions with a point: "We admit that the flexibility in the choice of polling directions is exploited to lead to a weak result, but our point is that it can happen" (p. 24). "The third example shows the limitations of our results; there is more left to do" (p. 20). The Search is deliberately under-described, and the reason is given: it "cannot be used to enhance the convergence theory", and "However, we are willing to pay this “theoretical” price" (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: The contributions are algorithmic (filter GPS), theoretical (smoothness-dependent conditions for h and f), software (NOMADm, Boeing Design Explorer; p. 3) and applied (planform; pp. 25–26). The limitations are specific:
  - The guarantees for f are weaker than those for h (p. 17).
  - The KKT-type result needs the refining and limit directions to generate the contingent cone, which is "not as constructive" as for linear constraints (p. 19).
  - A feasible descent direction can remain unexploited (pp. 24–25).
  - The key structural limit is stated in the Discussion: "essential to the convergence proof, is a finite total number of polling directions. It follows that one cannot attempt to obtain a dense set of polling directions" (p. 27).

  Future work: a multiobjective GPS (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first): evidence ✅. The paper treats explicit linear constraints and blackbox nonlinear constraints differently: barrier via ψ_X for the first, filter via h for the second (pp. 6–7). It eliminates variables with linear equalities (p. 7) and models blackbox failure as ∞ (pp. 1–2). It weighs ℓ₁ against squared ℓ₂ violation on analytic grounds and with an example (pp. 14–15, 22–24).
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll): evidence ✅. "A key feature of the new algorithm is that it preserves the useful division into global SEARCH and local POLL steps" (abstract, p. 1) and "a key issue for us" (p. 3). The Poll is "rigidly defined" to guarantee convergence, while "the largest improvements" come from the Search (p. 10). Heuristics such as alternating poll centers "can be part of a rigorously convergent algorithm" (p. 22).
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder bounded by counterexamples): evidence ✅. The results depend on local smoothness, and "Stronger optimality conditions are guaranteed for smoother functions" (abstract, p. 1). The sharpness examples (pp. 22, 25) include a self-admitted weak-result example (p. 24).
- Method 3 → MADS: evidence ✅ for SKILL.md's MADS origin row, which is currently marked *Link inferred from abstracts*. The finite-direction limitation that MADS removes is stated here in the authors' own words (p. 27).
- Method 5 (Budget-aware benchmarking): variant ⚠. The industrial run is reported in evaluations with filter snapshots (pp. 25–26), but there is no competitor and a single run.
- Method 6 (Solver as research instrument): early variant ⚠. There is an implementation in a coauthor's MATLAB package (NOMADm) and use inside Boeing's Design Explorer (p. 3), before NOMAD.
- New-pattern candidate: **a limitation example published alongside the theorem** (pp. 20, 24–25).
- New-pattern candidate: **unify by recovering prior theorems as corollaries** (the algorithm reduces to the linearly constrained GPS and the results to S002 when m = 0, pp. 11, 19). Also in S002 and S011.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Keep a single best-feasible point outside the filter so the filter can hold only infeasible points. This avoids blocking entries and lets the search cross infeasible ridges (p. 6).
- Offer two poll centers (feasible and least infeasible) and leave the choice rule open while proving results for any rule (pp. 8–9, 22).
- Reject trial points before full evaluation using explicit constraints and partial constraint sums (p. 9).
- Use squared ℓ₂ violation rather than ℓ₁ for h, and demonstrate the difference with a 2-D LP where ℓ₁ stalls (pp. 6, 14–15, 22–24).
- Close the paper with an explicit "Discussion" of how the method would really be run: which Search, more directions, alternating centers (p. 27).

- "We admit that the flexibility in the choice of polling directions is exploited to lead to a weak result, but our point is that it can happen." (p. 24)

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Status: UNREADABLE as named.** The text file is garbled font output. S002 presents its own examples as ones "that supplement those in [1]" (S002, p. 3).

- **D1 Problem & entry** (TR): Torczon (1997) showed liminf ‖∇f(x_k)‖ = 0 for C¹ f, and lim ‖∇f(x_k)‖ = 0 under stronger conditions. Lewis–Torczon extended the theory to bound and linear constraints (p. 3). The TR asks only whether these results can be strengthened without new assumptions. The contribution sentence: three small examples show *that Torczon’s [4] convergence results cannot be strengthened without introducing additional assumptions* (p. 3, OCR wording).
- **D2 Core idea** (TR): Use the algorithm's legal freedoms against it: the pattern matrix, the order in which the Search visits points, the step-control parameters λ and θ, and ties counted as failures. Build the smallest C¹ function on which a legal run exhibits the unwanted behavior.
  - Ex 1 (pp. 4–5): f = −x²y² on the unit disc, with (x² + y² − 1)² added outside only to make the level set compact. Coordinate polling P_k = [I −I 0], λ = 1, θ = 1/2, start at the origin. Every trial point (±1/2^k, 0), (0, ±1/2^k) ties f(0,0) = 0, so the iterates never move and converge to a *maximizer*: no second-order guarantee.
  - Ex 2 (pp. 5–9): a three-piece C¹ function g (f₁ = −26x³ − 32x²y + 7|y|³, f₂ = (7 − 8x²)|y|³, f₃ = f₂ + 8(x − ½)²(y³ + y + x − 1)), extended by (y ± 3)⁶ outside |y| ≤ 3. An extra pattern column (4, −2) and a rule-based Search order are used. Successful iterates (1, 1/2^{ℓ+2}) converge to (1,0), where ∇f = (1,0), and other accumulation points (1/2^ℓ, 0) have zero gradient. So lim ‖∇f‖ = 0 fails, while unsuccessful iterates still converge to zero-gradient points (p. 9).
  - Ex 3 (pp. 9–12): f = (x² + 1)y² for y ≥ 0 and ((1 − x)² + 1)y² for y < 0, under Torczon's *stronger* conditions (bounded pattern columns, Δ_k → 0, full polling with the best point taken; p. 10). Every point of {(j/2^ℓ, 0) : j, ℓ ∈ ℕ, j ≤ 2^ℓ} is an accumulation point (p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions** (TR): f is C¹ everywhere (verified for the piecewise definitions, p. 5), and the level sets are compact thanks to the extensions (pp. 4–5). Examples 1–2 are also valid for bound constraints, since the directions are orthogonal to the bounds (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices** (TR):
  - A piecewise objective plus a compactifying extension outside the region the algorithm ever visits (pp. 4–5).
  - Ties exploited so that polls fail (p. 5).
  - A *cycle* defined over iteration indices, e.g. k = ℓ² + 6ℓ to (ℓ+1)² + 6(ℓ+1) − 1 (p. 6). The closed-form iterates of every cycle are proved by induction (Prop 3.1, pp. 7–8; Prop 4.1, p. 11).

  - The odd-cycle case is *similar, and is omitted* (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments** (TR): none. Figures show the first iterates (Fig 1, p. 6; Fig 2, p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning** (TR): The TR is framed as sharpening, not refuting, Torczon's results. It has only four references (p. 12). Its practical hedge after Ex 1: *such behavior is improbable since the search phase of the algorithm usually evaluates the function in several other directions* (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing** (TR): The single contribution sentence is in the introduction (p. 3). Each section title states the negative result it establishes, for example *Inexistence of second order optimality conditions* (p. 4). The TR is ten printed pages with no conclusion section.
- **D8 Contribution & limits** (TR): This is a purely theoretical sharpness result. The examples rely on adversarial but legal choices of Search and pattern. No repair or new algorithm is proposed.
