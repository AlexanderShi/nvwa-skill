# Paper cards · batch c2-04 (core)

Batch intent: Four core papers that sit around the MADS theorem line rather than inside it: PBTR, a model-based trust-region method that borrows the progressive barrier (COAP 2018); an invited review of multiobjective performance indicators (EJOR 2021); a review of two decades of MADS/NOMAD applications (EURO J. Comput. Optim. 2021); and a student-led software paper on a blackbox-callable cellular-solid generator (2025). The question is how Audet's group builds and audits the infrastructure around direct search. Four sub-questions: which parts of the MADS toolkit travel to another algorithm family, and what has to be retuned (Methods 1 and 2)? How does the group vet the instruments it measures with (Method 5)? How do applications feed features back into NOMAD (Methods 4 and 6)? Does the Method 3 guarantee ladder survive when the group works outside MADS?
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all; core batch). Extra weight goes to D2 (what is borrowed and what is changed), D4 (what the guarantee actually says), D5 (benchmark protocol and failure reporting) and D6 (how prior work is sorted and criticized).

### S063 · A progressive barrier derivative-free trust-region algorithm for constrained optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2018, 10.1007/s10589-018-0020-4)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A.R. Conn (IBM T.J. Watson), S. Le Digabel, M. Peyrega (both GERAD/Polytechnique Montréal; p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The text file is a preprint dated June 28, 2016 (p. 1), not the published version. The batch list gives the title with a stray suffix naming C. Audet et al., dropped here. This is one of the two 2018 papers with Conn that SKILL.md lists under cross-lens collaborations. It is the first transfer of the 2009 progressive barrier (PB) out of MADS (p. 23). Peyrega is also a coauthor of the 2015 linear-equalities paper, cited as [11] (p. 24). The code is a Python prototype (p. 16), and the paper does not mention NOMAD integration. **Read**: all 27 pages, including the reference list (pp. 24–27). Text-file caveats: formulas are split across lines but readable. The plots of Figs. 4–7 (pp. 21–23) survive only as axis labels, so curve shapes are known only from the prose.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min f(x) s.t. c(x) ≤ 0, l ≤ x ≤ u, where f and c are blackbox outputs that may return +∞ (p. 2). The entry is a constraint-class statement, not an algorithm gap. The constraints are assumed quantifiable, relaxable, simulation-based and known ("QRSK" in the Le Digabel–Wild taxonomy), which explicitly rules out hidden constraints (p. 2). The paper then splits DFO into direct-search and model-based families and gives a rule of use: direct search for a badly behaved function, model-based methods for an adequately smooth one unless parallelism is essential (p. 2; Quote 1). It inventories constrained model-based DFO (p. 2–3): COBYLA; Conejo et al., which uses constraint gradients; augmented Lagrangian with a filter; trust funnel; DF-SQP; NOWPAC, which "requires feasible initial points"; inexact restoration. It does the same for direct-search constraint handling (p. 3): extreme barrier, filters, augmented-Lagrangian GSS, PB, progressive-to-extreme barrier. The stated goal is "to adapt the constraints handling PB technique to the DFTR framework" (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: open a constrained-DFO paper by naming the constraint class in a published taxonomy (QRSK here). The reader then knows which constraints the method does not cover before seeing the algorithm.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Keep the unconstrained derivative-free trust-region (DFTR) skeleton: interpolation models, a subproblem solved to a fraction of the Cauchy decrease, a ratio test and a radius update (Algorithm 1, p. 6). Replace its single iterate with the PB's two incumbents.
  - *Borrowed from MADS*: the constraint-violation function h (h2, h1 or h∞; p. 8); the barrier threshold h_max; the feasible and infeasible incumbents, labelled primary/secondary with trigger ρ = 0.1 (pp. 8–9); the three iteration types dominating/improving/unsuccessful (pp. 9–10); the speculative line search x + 2^j s (p. 10).
  - *Changed* (pp. 11–14):
    - Two trust regions, Δ_F and Δ_I, one per incumbent.
    - Two constrained subproblems, min f̃ s.t. c̃ ≤ 0 inside each ball, solved by Ipopt with 100 iterations and tolerance 10⁻⁸ (p. 19).
    - A second ratio ρ_h compares the true and the model decrease of h, where h̃ is built from the constraint models (p. 13).
    - The radius rules (4)–(7) use both ratios when the trial point is infeasible and only ρ_f when it is feasible (p. 14).
    - Booleans P_I and P_F (model gradient ≥ μΔ) replace the unconstrained ‖g‖ test (p. 13).
    - A minimal decrease on f is required to accept points (p. 11).
    - Model building is asymmetric: (n+1)(n+2)/2 interpolation points around the primary incumbent, at least 2 around the secondary (Step 1, p. 12).
  - *Retuning the borrowed mechanism*: the PB update runs on a filtered set of non-dominated points below h_max. The reason given is that PBTR "generates more points than MADS", so the barrier would otherwise decrease too slowly (p. 13). After an improving iteration the threshold becomes 0.9·h_max^original + 0.1·h(x*), where x* is the least-violating infeasible point. The reason given is that DFTR spends more evaluations per iteration than MADS (p. 20).
  - Three sample-set options are compared: quad_quad, quad_lin and lin_lin (pp. 19–20). When the secondary quadratic model is underdetermined, it is fixed by the minimum-Frobenius-norm Hessian (p. 20). Reader's note: Step 2 of Algorithm 2 builds only linear models around the secondary incumbent (p. 12), while the winning variant quad_quad builds quadratic ones there (pp. 19–21). The algorithm statement and the recommended implementation differ.
  - Transferable technique: when you transplant a mechanism from one algorithm family to another, list every parameter that implicitly assumed the donor's number of evaluations per iteration (here, the h_max update after improving iterations). Retune it, and report the original and the retuned rule side by side (p. 20, Figs. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Analysis*: f and all c_i are C² and f is bounded below. Model Hessians are uniformly bounded by κ_H. Each subproblem achieves a fraction κ_Cauchy of the Cauchy decrease, for f in the feasible subproblem and for h in the infeasible one (Assumption 1, pp. 14–15).
  - *Models*: they are certifiably κ-fully linear, and well-poisedness is checked at the start of every iteration (pp. 5, 8). The authors say they build fully linear models at every iteration "To simplify" (p. 5), and that fully linear models are needed only when the radius decreases or the model gradient is small (p. 7). This is a stated convenience choice.
  - *Constraints*: there are no hidden constraints (p. 2). In the experiments a point counts as feasible when h < 10⁻¹⁴ (pp. 17, 19).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The stated goal of §3.3 is only "to demonstrate that at least one sequence among the two trust region radii sequences converges to zero" (p. 14).
  - *The two theorems*: if a feasible point is ever generated, Δ_F → 0 (Thm 3.1, p. 15). If no feasible point is ever generated, Δ_I → 0 (Thm 3.2, p. 16).
  - *Proof skeleton, both theorems*:
    1. Suppose the radius does not go to 0.
    2. Since γ_inc > 1, infinitely many iterations do not decrease it, so on those iterations the ratio is ≥ η0 and P holds.
    3. The Cauchy decrease plus ‖g‖ ≥ μΔ > με/γ_inc gives a constant decrease C = η0 (κ_Cauchy/2) min{μ/κ_H, 1} μ ε²/γ_inc² of f (Thm 3.1) or of h ≥ 0 (Thm 3.2), infinitely often.
    4. This contradicts boundedness below (pp. 15–16).
  - Thm 3.2 first shows, by induction over the iteration types, that the infeasible incumbent is always defined (p. 16).
  - The authors state the gap themselves: if the algorithm generates both feasible and infeasible points, "nothing can be said" about Δ_I (p. 16). There is no stationarity result, no rate and no counterexample.
  - Reader's notes:
    - Assumption 1 is printed with h̃(x_I^k) − h̃^k(x̄) where x̂ is meant (p. 15), and the denominator of ρ̄_h uses x̂ instead of x̄ (p. 13). Both appear to be typos.
    - The proof of Thm 3.1 uses a decrease f(x_F^k) − f(x̄) ≥ C. Under rule (6), however, Δ_F can also stay the same or grow when x̄ is infeasible (p. 14). The proof does not say how such a step decreases the feasible incumbent's f.
    - §2.2 argues that DFTR radii go to zero because otherwise f would decrease by a minimal amount infinitely often (p. 7). Both theorems later reuse this device.
  - Transferable technique: a two-line template for any two-incumbent method. Argue *radius does not vanish → infinitely many non-decreasing iterations → uniform Cauchy decrease → contradiction with a lower bound*, once on f (bounded below) and once on h (bounded below by 0).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Problems*: 40 CUTEst problems with inequality constraints and bounds (Table 1, p. 18): n = 2–9, with 26 of the 40 at n ≤ 3; m = 1–13. The CUTEst x0 lies inside the bounds; it is feasible for 18 problems and infeasible for 22 (p. 17, Table 1). Unscaled CUTEst problems are excluded because PBTR has no dynamic scaling, unlike COBYLA (NLopt) and NOMAD 3.7.2 (p. 17). The selection is disclosed together with its reason.
  - *Realistic layer*: two MDO blackboxes, both started at the center of the bounds (p. 17). AIRCRAFT_RANGE has 10 variables, 10 relaxable constraints and a fixed-point loop over three disciplines. SIMPLIFIED_WING has 7 variables and 3 relaxable constraints.
  - *Currency and budget*: 100(n+1) evaluations per instance, which the authors say is of the same order of magnitude as in two earlier group papers (p. 17). Data profiles use τ = 10⁻³ and groups of n+1 evaluations. They are adapted to constraints by counting only feasible points (h < 10⁻¹⁴), so a problem on which no method finds a feasible point counts as unsolved (p. 17). Performance profiles are taken at 100(n+1) evaluations (pp. 17–19, 21–22). The time spent building models is ignored (p. 21).
  - *Internal ablation first*: 3 sample-set options × 2 threshold rules give 6 strategies. quad_quad_revised is best, and the revised rule beats the original for every model choice (pp. 21–22, Figs. 4–5).
  - *Competitors on their home ground*: COBYLA (linear models, ∞-norm penalty) and NOMAD 3.7.2 with quadratic models, both at default settings (p. 19).
    - Smooth set: PBTR matches COBYLA and is slightly better beyond 40(n+1) evaluations. Both model-based codes beat NOMAD, which the authors say they expected (p. 22).
    - AIRCRAFT_RANGE: COBYLA stalls near −1600, while NOMAD and PBTR reach about −4000, NOMAD faster (pp. 22–23, Fig. 7).
    - SIMPLIFIED_WING: NOMAD and PBTR overlap (p. 23).
  - *Gaps*: there is one run per solver, although PBTR samples random points when a sample set is short (p. 6); no seeds and no spread are reported. The legend of Fig. 4(b) says "variant" where the text says "revised" (p. 21). Fig. 5 is captioned "Data profiles" but its axis is "Performance ratio" (p. 22).
  - Transferable technique: adapt data profiles to constrained problems by scoring only points with h below a stated tolerance. Say in the setup that a problem where no solver finds a feasible point counts as unsolved for everyone (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Lineage*: the paper continues the authors' own PB (2009) and the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente DFTR framework. The description is taken from [18], with small changes to the criticality step (p. 4). It uses the fully-linear definition as reformulated by Bandeira, Scheinberg and Vicente (p. 4).
  - *Tone*: prior constrained DFTR methods are listed neutrally, each with one distinguishing feature (e.g., NOWPAC, p. 3). There is no direct criticism. Penalty and Lagrangian frameworks are acknowledged as always applicable but set aside in favour of a "direct treatment" (p. 2).
  - *Claim*: the positioning claim is a first: after MADS, PBTR is the first algorithm to deploy the PB (p. 23; Quote 2).
  - *Self-citation*: 16 of the 45 references have at least one of the four authors (pp. 24–27).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Structure*: about seven pages of recap precede the new algorithm on p. 11: DFTR definitions, the unconstrained algorithm, the PB and the speculative search (pp. 4–10). Figure 2 is "adapted from [8]" (p. 10).
  - *Claims*: the contribution sentence is plain: "The goal of this work is to adapt the constraints handling PB technique to the DFTR framework" (p. 3). Claims are hedged: "competitive" in the abstract (p. 1), "suggest" in the discussion (p. 23). The convergence section states its modest target in its first sentence (p. 14).
  - *Verdict*: two-sided. PBTR is preferable to NOMAD on smooth analytic problems and preferable to COBYLA on nonsmooth blackboxes (p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: give a two-sided verdict, saying where your method beats solver A, where it beats solver B, and where each beats you. Avoid a single "outperforms".
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: an algorithm (the PB inside a trust-region method), a weak convergence result and a Python implementation (pp. 11–16).
  - *Limits the authors name*, all concrete (pp. 23–24): no dynamic scaling; sample-set management without overdetermined models; how often to improve geometry is not analysed; a penalty treatment of the subproblem is not examined. Future work: bring the PB into "other nonlinear algorithms".
  - *Limits the paper leaves unnamed* (reader): the analysis gives no stationarity result; the mixed case, where both feasible and infeasible points are generated, is open (p. 16); the benchmark uses one run per solver.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the constraint class is fixed first, through a taxonomy (QRSK, no hidden constraints; p. 2). The relaxable and quantifiable class gets the PB (pp. 8–10).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: a 100(n+1) budget, data and performance profiles, and COBYLA on its smooth home ground plus NOMAD on MDO blackboxes. Competitors run at their defaults. The paper concedes up front that direct search should lose on smooth problems (pp. 17–23). This confirms the SKILL.md Heuristic 6 case for this paper. ⚠ The single-run protocol for a code with random sampling falls short of Method 5 step 5 (pp. 6, 17).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the realistic layer is the group's recurring MDO blackboxes, AIRCRAFT_RANGE and SIMPLIFIED_WING (p. 17).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: PBTR has no Search/Poll split; the model step drives every iteration. What travels from MADS is the constraint-handling module (the PB) and the speculative search, not the Free-Search/rigid-Poll architecture (pp. 10–12).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: outside MADS the ladder shrinks to a single rung, *one radius sequence goes to zero* (pp. 14–16). It has no stationarity rung and no counterexamples. The authors do state the open mixed case (p. 16).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: in this paper the method stays a Python prototype (p. 16), and NOMAD integration is not mentioned.
- New-pattern candidate: **port a module across algorithm families and retune its implicit rates**. The PB goes into DFTR, and the h_max update and the point set it filters are changed because the host spends more evaluations per iteration (pp. 13, 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Transplanting a mechanism: identify the parameters that encode the donor's evaluation rate, retune them, and publish the original-vs-retuned ablation (pp. 13, 20–22).
- Two incumbents, two trust regions, unequal effort: spend the full quadratic-model budget around the primary incumbent and build a cheap model around the secondary one (pp. 11–12, 19–20).
- For an infeasible trial point, require the ratio test to pass on both f and h before enlarging the radius (rules (4) and (6), p. 14).
- Constrained data profiles: count only points with h below a stated tolerance, and declare what happens when no solver finds a feasible point (p. 17).
- Disclose instance-selection filters with their reason, as with the unscaled CUTEst problems dropped for lack of dynamic scaling (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "Given a very badly behaved function we would use a direct-search method. If the function can be adequately approximated by a smooth function we would prefer a model-based approach unless it is essential to exploit some parallel architecture." (p. 2)
- "After MADS, it is the first algorithm to deploy the progressive barrier." (p. 23)

### S004 · Performance indicators in multiobjective optimization (European Journal of Operational Research 2021, 10.1016/j.ejor.2020.11.016)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. Bigeon (Grenoble INP, G-SCOP), D. Cartier, S. Le Digabel, L. Salomon (GERAD and Grenoble; p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: The paper is an "Invited Review" (p. 2), received 3 December 2018 and accepted 11 November 2020 (p. 2). Funding came from Le Digabel's NSERC Discovery grant, and from an NSERC CRD grant and an InnovÉÉ grant, both with Hydro-Québec and Rio Tinto (p. 21). SKILL.md lists Salomon as a PhD (2022, DMultiMads), so this review is plausibly the measurement groundwork for the group's later multiobjective solver. That link is inference: the paper itself contains no algorithm. SKILL.md's Method 5 cites it as the review of 63 indicators. **Read**: every page of text. The appendix tables (pp. 22–24) were read for structure and spot-checked; the reference list (pp. 25–27) was skimmed. Text-file caveats: many mathematical symbols are lost, including the weak-dominance and "is better" relation symbols and the names of the ε, Δ and Γ indicators (pp. 3, 6–7, 9–11). Table 1 comes out as one run-on line (pp. 4–5). Formulas are readable only together with the surrounding prose.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a measurement question. The quality of a single-objective solution is trivial to quantify, but the quality of a Pareto front approximation is not. It matters for comparing algorithms, defining stopping criteria and designing methods (pp. 2–3).
  - *Gap claimed*: existing surveys "focus only on some specific properties" (p. 2). Li and Yao (2019) is the closest, but it is oriented to the evolutionary community and written at a higher level of description (p. 3).
  - *Problem stated*: min F(x) = [f1 … fm] over X with m ≥ 2 (p. 3). Dominance is defined for objective vectors and for decision vectors, the latter taken from Audet, Savard and Zghal (2008) (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when a field has many competing measures, organize the survey around what the measures are used for (comparison, embedding in algorithms, stopping, diversity), not around their inventors (p. 3, §4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper classifies 63 indicators (abstract, p. 2) into four groups: cardinality, convergence, distribution and spread, and convergence and distribution (p. 7).
  - *One entry template for every indicator* (§3, pp. 7–19): a definition; which direction is better; known drawbacks, often shown with a small counterexample; computational cost; whether it needs the Pareto front, a reference set or a reference point; whether it suits continuous or discontinuous fronts.
  - *Appendix tables*: Table 3 gives parameters, comparison sets, complexity and monotonicity (pp. 22–23). Tables 4–5 give compatibility and completeness for unary and binary indicators (pp. 23–24).
  - *Formal backbone* (Zitzler et al.; Defs. 6–9, pp. 6–7): k-ary indicators; monotonicity and strict monotonicity; comparison methods; R-compatibility and R-completeness; and the impossibility result that no combination of unary indicators is equivalent to the "is better" relation.
  - *Constraints*: a front approximation contains only feasible vectors, and the iteration counter k is set to 0 at the first feasible point (p. 7).
  - *Scope*: §3.5 briefly covers indicators in decision space (p. 19). §4 covers four uses (pp. 19–21).
  - *The authors' own additions are small*: an external accuracy parameter ε for the error ratio, to absorb rounding (p. 8), and four properties a distribution/spread indicator should have (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: audit every measure against the same property row (monotone? needs the true front? external parameters? cost? discontinuous fronts?) and put the rows in one table. Readers then choose a measure from the table rather than from the prose.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Problem*: continuous multiobjective minimization. The objectives conflict, so the Pareto set is not a singleton; this is stated as an assumption in a Remark (p. 3).
  - *Approximations*: a front approximation is a mutually non-dominated set with distinct images (Def. 5, p. 6).
  - *Nadir*: approximated from the single-objective minimizers. The approximation is exact for m = 2 but not always for m ≥ 3 (p. 3).
  - *Spread indicators*: they make sense only when the Pareto set yields several distinct objective vectors (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: No new theorem. Three devices.
  - *One counterexample per indicator*, mostly taken from Knowles and Corne (2002):
    - ONVG: a million-point set scores higher than a single point that dominates all of it (p. 7).
    - Error ratio: a 100-point near-front set scores 0.99, a 2-point set 0.5 (p. 8).
    - GD: equals 0 for a single point on the front (p. 8).
    - MPFE: favours a one-point set over a ten-point set with nine points on the front (p. 9).
    - Additive ε-indicator: identical values for y1 = (0,1,1) and y2 = (1,0,0) (p. 10).
    - Spacing: ignores gaps (Fig. 3, p. 13).
    - HV: the ranking of two sets flips with the reference point (Fig. 6, p. 18).
  - *Property classification* against Zitzler's compatibility/completeness framework (Tables 4–5, pp. 23–24).
  - *Complexity accounting per indicator*. HV costs O(|Y_N|^{m/3} polylog |Y_N|) (Chan 2013), with the remark that no implementation of that algorithm is available (p. 17). DOA and IGD+ cost O(m |Y_N| |Y_P|) (pp. 10, 16).
  - Key fact recorded: HV and closely related metrics are the only known strictly monotonic unary indicators (p. 17).
  - Reader's notes: Table 3 places MDR in §3.1.3, while the text places it in §3.1.6 (pp. 8, 22). The abstract's count of 63 was not recounted here.
  - Transferable technique: before recommending a measure, find the smallest pair of sets on which its ranking contradicts Pareto dominance, and put that pair in the measure's entry.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: None; this is a review. §4 does prescribe practice.
  - *Comparing solvers*: in single-objective optimization the standard tools are performance and data profiles. Custódio et al. (2011) were the first to use them for multiobjective problems. The authors criticize that use because the cardinality and distribution indicators involved do not capture order relations between sets (p. 19).
  - *Recommended measures* (pp. 19–20): monotonic indicators or ≺-complete/compatible comparison methods, namely dominance move, G-metric, binary ε, the Hypervolume Sharpe-Ratio indicator (HSR) and volume-based metrics. Using GD or IGD, as Al-Dujaili and Suresh (2016) and Brockhoff et al. (2015) do, is "not a pertinent choice". IGD+ or DOA are suggested instead: weakly monotone, and cheaper than HV when there are many objectives. Attainment functions are suggested for stochastic solvers (p. 20).
  - *Stopping*: for scalarization methods, a predetermined evaluation budget is standard in blackbox optimization and indicators are "not relevant" (p. 20; Quote 1). For a posteriori methods, the authors favour monotonic or dominance-capturing indicators (p. 20; Quote 2).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Coverage*: Table 1 cross-references every indicator against nine earlier surveys (pp. 4–5).
  - *Tone toward the closest survey*: Li and Yao (2019) is framed as "a useful complement" (p. 3). The review addresses "the whole operational research community", in contrast with the evolutionary community (p. 3).
  - *Direct criticism*: of a fellow DFO group's benchmarking (the Custódio et al. 2011 DMS paper, p. 19), and of GD/IGD usage in two benchmarking papers (pp. 19–20).
  - *Self-citation*: the group's own multiobjective methods appear only in passing: the dominance definitions from Audet, Savard and Zghal (2008), budget stopping (p. 20), and NBI used in blackbox optimization (Audet, Savard and Zghal 2010, p. 21).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Structure*: definitions (§2), then the catalogue (§3), then uses (§4), then a verdict (§5).
  - *Entries*: highly templated. Almost every entry states which value is better, and most balance a drawback with a practical merit ("However, it is straightforward to compute", p. 8).
  - *Hedging*: recommendations are hedged: HV is called, "until now", the most relevant (p. 21); "We believe" (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: when writing a catalogue survey, fix one entry template and keep to it. The template then makes gaps visible, such as a missing cost or a missing counterexample.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: a taxonomy, property tables and recommendations. HV is the most relevant indicator; for comparisons it can be replaced by dominance move, G-metric, binary ε, HSR, IGD+ or DOA, which cost less (p. 21).
  - *Limits named*: HV's exponential cost in the number of objectives and its dependence on the reference point (p. 21).
  - *Future work*: new indicators that fix HV's drawbacks while keeping its properties, and indicators embedded in algorithms (p. 21).
  - *Unnamed limits* (reader): no empirical comparison of the indicators; the counterexamples are mostly borrowed; constrained multiobjective problems get only the convention that k starts at the first feasible point (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the measurement methodology is published as a contribution in its own right. It recommends Pareto-compliant indicators for comparing algorithms and criticizes a competitor's multiobjective profiles (pp. 19–21). The four indicator groups named in Method 5 step 4 (convergence, spread, distribution, cardinality) come from this paper (p. 7).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the counterexample habit is applied to measuring instruments rather than theorems. Each indicator's implicit claim (for example, that it measures convergence) is tested against small sets on which it contradicts dominance (pp. 7–10, 13, 18). Most of the counterexamples are borrowed, not built.
- Method 2 ✅ weak evidence: feasibility enters the definitions. Fronts contain only feasible vectors, and indicator traces start at the first feasible point (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: **audit the instrument before measuring**, i.e. a property table with counterexamples for the metrics you will use to compare algorithms, published before your own multiobjective solver (the DMultiMads link is inference).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A property table for measures, with columns for parameters, reference data needed, complexity, monotonicity, compatibility and completeness (Tables 3–5, pp. 22–24).
- Four requirements for any distribution or spread indicator (p. 21): works with scaled objectives; not based on nearest-neighbour distances when fronts may be discontinuous; no external parameters; easy to interpret.
- In data or performance profiles for multiobjective solvers, use a (weakly) Pareto-compliant indicator (HV, IGD+ or DOA) as the performance measure t_{p,s}, not GD, IGD or a cardinality count (pp. 19–20).
- Start indicator traces at the first feasible iterate (p. 7).
- When there are many objectives and HV is too expensive, fall back on IGD+ or DOA at O(m |Y_N| |Y_P|) (pp. 10, 16, 20).

**Quotes**:
- "Stopping at a predetermined number of function evaluations is often used in the context of blackbox optimization (Audet et al., 2008). The use of performance indicators also is not relevant." (p. 20)
- "We believe that the use of monotonic performance indicators or binary ones that capture the dominance property seems to be the most efficient one in the years to come to follow the behavior of population-based algorithms along iterations." (p. 20)

### S013 · Two decades of blackbox optimization applications (EURO Journal on Computational Optimization 2021, 10.1016/j.ejco.2021.100011)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Alarie (Hydro-Québec and IREQ), A.E. Gheribi (CRCT and Polytechnique Montréal), M. Kokkolaras (GERAD and McGill), S. Le Digabel (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: Received 29 October 2020, revised 17 September 2021 (p. 2). It was written in the year NOMAD 4 was released (p. 3). The three main sections match the coauthors' domains: Hydro-Québec energy (§2), materials science (§3) and engineering design (§4) (pp. 4–10). This is a reader's inference from the affiliations (p. 2). SKILL.md cites the paper under Taste mark 1 and Method 4. **Read**: all 14 pages. Tables 1–3 were read (pp. 4–6, 11); the reference list (pp. 11–14) was skimmed. Caveat: Figs. 1–2 are images, and only their captions were extracted (pp. 4, 9).

### S159 · Cesogen: cellular solid generator (Science and Technology of Advanced Materials: Methods 2025, 10.1080/27660400.2025.2570116)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: P.A. Patience (first author; GERAD and Department of Mathematics and Industrial Engineering, Polytechnique Montréal), B. Blais (CHAOS Laboratory, Department of Chemical Engineering; corresponding author) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: Received 30 July 2025, accepted 29 September 2025 (p. 3), published online 12 January 2026 (p. 2). The CRediT statement gives Audet conceptualization, funding acquisition, project administration, resources, supervision, and review & editing. Patience did everything else, including the software, the investigation and the original draft (p. 19). Funding: an NSERC CRD grant (538319-2018, Audet) with NRC, plus discovery grants (p. 19). This is a supervised software paper in a materials venue, not an optimization-methods paper. **Read**: all 21 file pages; the reference list (pp. 19–21) was skimmed. Text-file caveats: the SDF formulas of §2 are missing; only the prose survives (pp. 5–6). Table 2's expressions lost their minus signs and some operators (p. 10). Several numeric symbols are lost, including the sample-count symbol and the lower bound of the offset radius (pp. 11, 16). Figs. 1–13 are images with captions only.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an application gap. TPMS-like cellular solids (TPMS = triply periodic minimal surface) are spreading with additive manufacturing, and "Mathematical optimization of cellular solids is one aspect for which there is a dearth in the literature" (p. 4).
  - *The bottleneck is tooling*: an optimizer needs a generator it can drive with no user intervention, through a batch command line or a library. That requirement eliminates every evaluated free generator except Scaffolder, ASLI and Microgen (p. 4).
  - *Contribution*: Cesogen, "designed from the start to be adapted to computer-guided optimization" (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Cesogen is a signed-distance-function (SDF) processor with three phases: convert meshes to SDFs, combine the SDFs, and contour the result with a robust marching-cubes-like algorithm (p. 7).
  - *Interface*: a mixfix, stack-oriented domain-specific language (DSL) on the command line (pp. 7–8). SDF specifiers: ball, cylinder, box, tpms.*, expression, file. Unary operators: complement, offset, shell, move, scale, rotate, orient. Binary operators: union, intersection, difference. Options: -b, -s, -o and @file.
  - *Three guiding principles* (p. 9): suitability for blackbox optimization, which requires a text interface; ergonomics, which requires a word-based syntax without parentheses; and composability, which requires a stack so that SDFs can be combined without limit.
  - *Key design choice*: hierarchical cellular solids are built by direct intersection, with SDFs combined at runtime. The alternative, sequential intersection, writes and rereads meshes (pp. 5, 11–12).
  - *Implementation*: written library-first in Common Lisp, with the command line as a thin layer (p. 7); open source under the MIT license (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: derive each interface decision from a named principle, then reuse the same principles as elimination criteria when comparing with competitors (pp. 9–10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Geometry*: TPMS-like surfaces come from the first terms of their Fourier nodal approximations (p. 6). After operations, SDFs are only approximate (p. 5). The sign convention is negative inside (p. 5).
  - *Optimization blackbox*: f maps X to R ∪ {+∞} and c maps X to (R ∪ {+∞})^m (p. 16). The categorical "kind" variable is handled with one NOMAD run per value (p. 16).
  - *Convenience assumptions stated*: no mesh-convergence analysis was done, because this is "a qualitative demonstration" (p. 16). Parallelism is left to the solver (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: No theory. The devices are numerical-robustness diagnostics.
  - *Error measures*: four of them, all relative to the grid diagonal: the L2 and the maximum distance to the reference SDF, for mesh points and for cell centres (p. 12).
  - *Failure traced to its cause*: computing the distance from a point to a skewed triangle is numerically unstable, and marching cubes without smoothing produces many narrow triangles (p. 12).
  - *Roundtrip test*: write an SDF to disk and read it back. Ideally this is repeatable "ad infinitum", but it fails at the 28th roundtrip (pp. 12–13).
  - Transferable technique: test a lossy file-format or geometry pipeline with repeated roundtrips (serialize, deserialize, measure the error against the reference), and report the step at which it breaks (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The machine specifications are listed (p. 10).
  - *Robustness*: sequential hierarchical intersection fails at the second step with a division by zero, and ASLI likewise crashes after a few combinations (p. 13). The roundtrip test stops at 27 successful trips (p. 13). It is worse when starting from the Stanford bunny (p. 13).
  - *Speed*: gyroids were generated at sample counts from 25³ to 800³ (timed with hyperfine). The 800³ case gives 16.2 million cells in 79 s; finer grids need more than 16 GiB (p. 14). Filling a mesh with a TPMS-like solid takes 1.3 s and 4.2 s at the two tested resolutions (p. 13).
  - *Blackbox-optimization demo* (pp. 15–18):
    - Task: minimize the solid fraction α of an hourglass-shaped block filled with an exoskeletal TPMS-like solid, under compression.
    - Pipeline: Cesogen → Gmsh (tetrahedral meshing) → MFEM (linear elasticity).
    - Variables: kind ∈ {gyroid, IWP, FRD} (categorical); offset radius (upper bound 3; lower bound garbled); scale κ0 ∈ [0.5, 2].
    - Constraints: the average displacement of the top surface and the maximum displacement (p. 16).
    - Solver: NOMAD 4 at defaults, initial point chosen from 20 LHS evaluations, budget 50 per kind including the LHS (p. 16).
    - Results: IWP α = 0.31 (2.9 h), FRD 0.31 (2.9 h), gyroid 0.38 (1.8 h) (Table 3, p. 17). The text gives 7.5 h in total (p. 16), while the table sums to 7.6 h, probably a rounding matter.
    - Failures: infeasible points can exceed 50% of evaluations (p. 17; Quote 2). Their causes are non-physical simulations and mesh-generation errors, "Both are handled by NOMAD" (p. 18). About 150 meshes were tested (p. 18).
    - What is missing: no competing optimizer, and one run per kind.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Comparison*: Table 1 compares 18 free standalone generators by language, CLI/GUI/library form and features, and commercial tools are listed separately (p. 4). The features were determined "from a cursory inspection" (p. 4), an honest hedge.
  - *Elimination by principle* (p. 10): GUI-based tools fail the first principle. ASLI has a predetermined library and takes input only through configuration files. Scaffolder and Microgen require writing code. The claim is that only Cesogen satisfies all three principles (p. 10).
