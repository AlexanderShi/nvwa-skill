### S184 · Two-Way Blade Modeling Method for Structural Redesign of Compressor Blades (AIAA Journal 2023, 10.2514/1.j062619)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Kojtych, A. Batailly (both Mechanical Engineering, Polytechnique Montréal; Audet at GERAD) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: This is an application paper outside Audet's home field. Its only Audet-group references are MADS [36] and NOMAD 3 [39] (p. 38). Funding comes from Audet's NSERC Discovery grant RGPIN-2020-04448, Batailly's Canada Research Chair and Kojtych's FRQNT merit scholarship (p. 34). The paper builds on Batailly's earlier blackbox blade optimization [20] (Lainé, Piollet, Nyssen, Batailly, J. Eng. Gas Turbines Power 2019), which "has already been successfully employed" MADS (p. 5). Card c03 records DiscoMads (Audet, Batailly, Kojtych, SIAM J. Optim. 2022), a discontinuity-handling MADS by the same three authors. This paper does not cite it: a grep for "discontinu" in the reference list returns nothing.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an industrial need. Redesigns triggered by nonlinear structural interactions (blade-tip/casing contact) have "no design guidelines" (p. 2). Predictive contact simulations exist but "have never been integrated within blades’ design procedures", having been used only for a posteriori discrimination of profiles (p. 4). The authors split a redesign into three steps: (1) modelling, (2) update and (3) performance check (pp. 3–4). They locate the gap in step 1. Few published methods both extract intuitive engineering parameters from an existing blade *and* regenerate a CAD model from them (p. 3). The earlier contact-optimization parameterization simplified the blade tip with planar surfaces (p. 3). Because of confidentiality, the input is assumed to be a CAD model, not a proprietary parameterized model (p. 4). The paper is framed as a "proof of concept" for redesign toward contact robustness (pp. 2, 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The core is a two-way map that turns an existing blade into a starting point for blackbox optimization in an interpretable parameter space (Fig. 1, p. 5). It has three modules: PAR (CAD → parameters), GEN (parameters → CAD) and MSH (CAD → FE mesh). They are written in Python on the open-source Salome platform (p. 5).
  - *Parameterization.* The blade is cut into n_sec conical sections. Each section has 5 stacking parameters (radius, lean, sweep, conicity, stagger) and 14 profile parameters (two ellipses, a trailing-edge circle and four cubic splines) (pp. 7–10).
  - *DFO use 1 (inverse identification).* The profile parameters are found by solving min e(x) subject to l ≤ x ≤ u (Problem 13). Here e(x) = 0.9·d̄(x) + 0.1·|δp(x)|, the mean point-to-profile distance plus the relative perimeter difference, taken from a prior MSc thesis [38] (p. 15). The problem is solved with NOMAD 3.9.1 defaults and 1500 evaluations per profile. The starting point and bounds are computed from the target point cloud. The suction point and chord are read off the data, which cuts the dimension from 14 to 11 (pp. 16–17, Table 2).
  - *DFO use 2 (redesign).* The objective is to minimize the dynamic clearance c_d,u*(m(x)) of the first bending mode, used "as surrogate" for contact robustness (p. 23). There are only 4 variables: sweep and lean at sections 3 and 5. Profile parameters are frozen to preserve aerodynamics. Sections 2 and 4 are set by quadratic interpolation "to ensure that realistic blade shapes are generated", and bounds are ±2 mm and ±1.5° (Table 5, p. 23). MADS is chosen because it suits time-consuming simulations and tolerates failed ones (p. 23).
  - *What changed vs. [20].* Conical sections replace the planar-tip simplification, an extraction step is added so that industrial blades can be the input (pp. 3–5), and the robustness of the geometry generator is engineered. The sewing tolerance starts at Δz/200 and is doubled after each failure, with at most three attempts (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - *Input.* The blade is a CAD model given in millimetres, with a stated rotation and flow orientation (pp. 4, 13).
  - *Profile curves.* Shape restrictions "reduce the range of admissible parameters": horizontal tangents at s and p, an ellipse axis ratio fixed to 2, and the trailing-edge circle centre on the abscissa (p. 10).
  - *Why derivative-free.* In Problem 13, e(x) is differentiable wherever δp(x) ≠ 0, but its gradient is unavailable "because of the discretization of the profiles" (p. 16). In Problem 15, one evaluation takes about 30 s on a standard PC and no gradient is available (p. 23).
  - *Constraints.* Both problems have bounds only (Tables 2 and 5, pp. 17, 23). Failures of CAD or mesh generation act as hidden constraints (p. 23).
  - *Surrogate.* The dynamic clearance is "assumed to provide a computationally inexpensive estimate" of contact robustness (p. 23).
  - No convergence hypotheses (smoothness, Lipschitz) are stated anywhere in the parts read.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - There is no theorem or convergence analysis. The only proof is a geometric derivation of the cone-origin formula in the change-of-basis appendix (Eq. 17, p. 27, skimmed).
  - *Budget justification is empirical*: "asymptotic convergence" was checked on 17 profiles of rotor 37, showing that 1500 evaluations reach low error values (p. 16).
  - *Acceptance threshold is inherited*: e(x) ≤ 0.05 counts as "well-represented", from [38, p. 35] (p. 19).
  - *Regularity claim*: the CAD side surface is G² at face intersections, and this was "checked numerically" (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: Fidelity is validated on the public NASA rotor 37 and rotor 67 CAD models (p. 18) with n_sec ∈ {2, 3, 5, 9, 17}, a nested "dichotomous" set (p. 18). The criteria are volume (Table 3, p. 18), e(x) < 0.05 for all 17 sections (p. 19), the first 10 natural frequencies (p. 20), dynamic clearance (Table 4, p. 21) and nonlinear frequency-response curves (NFRC) under contact (pp. 21–22); n_sec = 5 is the best compromise (p. 22). The redesign is a *single* NOMAD run with defaults and 400 evaluations. It moves the dynamic clearance from about −0.46 to −0.0089 mm ("98 %"), and 3 of 400 simulations failed from CAD/mesh errors (p. 23). An a posteriori NFRC check shows the maximum displacement amplitude down 75 % (p. 24). There is no competing optimizer and no replication. Timing: GEN+MSH ≤ 13.5 s against 30 s per simulation, PAR about 5 min per profile, about 3.5 h in total (p. 33). A stress test with extreme stacking variations produced no generation errors (pp. 33–34).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - *Contribution level*: a tool and method (the two-way modelling method is "the original contribution", p. 4), a fidelity-validation protocol that goes beyond geometry (p. 17), and a proof-of-concept redesign (pp. 22–24).
  - *Limits are stated concretely*: the remaining input/parameterized differences are "likely to be caused by the limits of the profile parameterization" (p. 22), and fidelity "could be improved further" by changing it (p. 26). Aerodynamics are deliberately left out, and the results call for "a new balance between aerodynamics and structural dynamics" (pp. 24–25). The criterion covers only the first bending mode (p. 24).
  - *Future work*: extend the clearance criterion to other modes such as first torsion (p. 24), redesign other blades, add other criteria, and modify the optimization algorithm for "discontinuous quantities" in contact simulations (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (hidden constraints). CAD/mesh generation errors are treated as failed simulations that MADS handles "without stopping the optimization", and the failure count is reported (3/400, p. 23). Failures are also reduced at the source: an adaptive sewing tolerance (pp. 12–13) and an extreme-parameter stress test (pp. 33–34).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. Both problems are bound-constrained only. There is no EB/PB classification because there are no general output constraints. Realism is enforced by *reducing the design space* (frozen profiles, quadratic interpolation of sections 2 and 4), not by constraints (p. 23).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. NOMAD (v3.9.1, the NOMAD 3 line [39], not NOMAD 4) is used off the shelf with default parameters in two roles: inverse identification (1500 evaluations) and redesign (400 evaluations) (pp. 16, 23).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The evaluation currency is reported: 1500 and 400 evaluations and 30 s per evaluation (pp. 16, 23, 33). But there is no competing solver, no replication and no profiles; the optimizer is a tool, not the object of study (pp. 22–24).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. The validation uses public NASA rotor 37/67 CAD models (p. 18, footnote 4), and the earlier blade-geometry program [37] is a HAL source-code deposit (p. 38). But this paper states no release of PAR/GEN/MSH or of the dynamic-clearance blackbox (a grep for "github", "available" and "source code" finds only [37]).
- Research Taste, mark 1 ✅ evidence. The blackbox's pathologies are named: cost (≈30 s), a gradient unavailable because of discretization, and failures (pp. 16, 23).
- Taste quick-check ("Is n small enough, or can it be reduced by fixing or decomposing variables?") ✅ evidence. Identification goes from 14 to 11 variables by computing the suction point and chord from the data (p. 16). Redesign uses 4 variables after freezing the profiles and interpolating intermediate sections (p. 23).
- Heuristic 10 ✅ evidence (reader's inference, via card c03). The application names discontinuous quantities as the algorithmic open issue (p. 26), and the same three authors' DiscoMads addresses discontinuities. This paper does not make that link itself.
- new-pattern candidate: *Build the missing blackbox-callable tool to open a new application field to DFO* (the same name as S159 in c2-04). Here the missing tool is the two-way parameterizer. It makes the redesign of existing industrial blades an optimization problem (pp. 4–5, 25–26).
- new-pattern candidate: *DFO inverse fit seeds DFO redesign*. A first bound-constrained DFO problem fits the parameterization to the existing design, and its solution becomes x0 of the design optimization (pp. 15–16, 22).
- new-pattern candidate: *fidelity judged on the quantity of interest*. A reduced geometric model is validated with criteria of increasing strictness: geometry, then natural frequencies, then the optimization criterion, then the full nonlinear response (pp. 17–22). This is related to S160's "validate the blackbox physically before optimizing it".
- new-pattern candidate: *cheap proxy objective, expensive a-posteriori check*. The optimizer sees only the dynamic clearance, and the expensive nonlinear contact simulation is run once on the initial and optimized designs (pp. 23–24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To optimize around an existing design available only as geometry, first fit your parameterization to it by DFO: minimize a data-misfit e(x) under bounds, with x0 and bounds computed from the data. Then start the design optimization from the fitted parameters (pp. 15–17, 22).
- (D2) Before optimizing, compute every parameter that can be read directly off the data and remove it from the variable vector (14 → 11, p. 16).
- (D2) To keep a redesign low-dimensional and realistic, freeze the parameters owned by another discipline and interpolate intermediate sections from a few control sections (4 variables, p. 23).
- (D5) Justify a fixed evaluation budget by checking convergence on a representative set of instances (17 profiles → 1500 evaluations, p. 16).
- (D5) Run a model-fidelity study on nested resolution levels (n_sec = 2, 3, 5, 9, 17) and rank the criteria from cheapest to strictest, ending with the full downstream simulation (pp. 18–22).
- (D5) Report the blackbox failure count and its cause next to the optimization result (3/400, CAD/mesh errors, p. 23). Report the timing of each module against the cost of one simulation (p. 33).
- (D7) List the requirements of an optimization-facing model up front: fidelity, resolution, robustness, interpretability, versatility and computational efficiency (pp. 4–5). Then point each to its validation section: fidelity in §3, robustness and efficiency in appendix §5.4 (pp. 17, 33–34).
