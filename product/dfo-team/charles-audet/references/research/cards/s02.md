### S116 · A derivative-free trust-region augmented Lagrangian algorithm (Les Cahiers du GERAD 2016, G–2016–53; no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Le Digabel, M. Peyrega (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: companion of the progressive-barrier trust-region report PBTR (cited as [3], p. 5, p. 12), which appears as COAP 71(2) 2018 in S144's reference list (S144 p. 17). The Discussion names merging with the progressive barrier as future work (p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Problem (1) has equalities h, inequalities g and bounds. f, h and g are C², but their derivatives are unavailable because a simulation is involved (p. 5). The entry is a gap in the literature. COBYLA uses an l∞ merit with linear models, and its NLopt version replaces each equality by two inequalities. Trust-funnel, SQP-DFTR, NOWPAC, inexact restoration and PBTR each cover part of (1) (p. 5). The paper names solving the subproblems and updating the trust-region radius as the two design elements (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The PHR augmented Lagrangian (the one in Algencan) is used twice (p. 6–7):
  - to solve the quadratic-model subproblem inside the trust region, with Algencan (p. 7);
  - to define the ratio r^k as actual over predicted decrease of L_ρ, with the multipliers and penalty (λ̃, μ̃, ρ̃) frozen at the values the subproblem solver returns (p. 7).
  - What is kept: the unconstrained DFTR update rules (η0, η1, γinc, γdec), "with minor modifications" (p. 4, p. 7).
  - Models: quadratic interpolation on the (n+1)(n+2)/2 most recent points within 2Δk, with a geometry-improvement step when there are too few (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Only C² functions are assumed (p. 5). No convergence hypotheses are listed, because there is no analysis. As printed, the parameter condition reads 0 < γinc < 1 < γdec, but a successful iteration sets Δk+1 = min{γinc Δk, Δmax} (p. 7), so the two symbols look swapped.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no convergence result. The design is justified only by the remarks that the subproblem's augmented Lagrangian L̃ is itself a model of L_ρ (p. 7) and that DFTR update rules are "simply adapted" from derivative-based trust regions (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: 83 small CUTEst problems (40 with inequalities only, 43 with at least one equality), budget 100(n+1), against NLopt-COBYLA and HOPSPACK with defaults and with HOPSPACK's explicit linear-constraint handling disabled for fairness, data and performance profiles at τ = 10⁻³ (p. 8). DFTRl is close to COBYLA but solves 10% fewer inequality problems (p. 8–9). It beats COBYLA on problems with equalities and dominates HOPSPACK (p. 9). Only analytic problems are used. The counts disagree: 83 problems (p. 8), "80 problems" (p. 10), and a Table 2 titled "40 analytical problems with at least one equality" that lists 43 rows (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm plus a short numerical study. Limits appear only as future work: other penalty functions, other sample-set management, and adapting the progressive barrier to the inequalities (p. 10). The missing theory is not flagged as a limitation (pp. 5–10).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the budget is in groups of n+1, with profiles, a competitor from another family (COBYLA) and an explicit fairness adjustment (p. 8). The test set is analytic CUTEst only, which is the Taste warning sign 4 (p. 8).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: all constraints, equalities and inequalities alike, are folded into one augmented Lagrangian (p. 6–7), with no EB/PB split. The progressive barrier is left to future work (p. 10) and to the companion PBTR (p. 5).
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction (by absence): an algorithm paper with no convergence analysis at all (§§1–5, pp. 5–10).
- new-pattern candidate: *merit function as the bridge that lets unconstrained machinery run on constrained problems* (p. 4, p. 7). The same move appears in S144 (weighted h inside PB) and S158 (c09).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To extend an unconstrained trust-region method to constraints with minimal change, compute the ratio on a merit function whose parameters are frozen at the values the subproblem solver returns. True and predicted decrease then use the same merit (p. 7).
- (D5) If a competitor has a feature your method lacks (here HOPSPACK's explicit linear constraints), switch it off and say so, so that algorithms rather than feature sets are compared (p. 8).
- (D5) Report inequality-only and equality-containing subsets separately. Here the advantage shows only on the second (p. 8–9).

**Quotes**:
- "Using an augmented Lagrangian, and more generally a merit function, to design the DFTR update rules with constraints is shown to be an efficient technique." (p. 4)
- "Finally, the progressive barrier [4] could be adapted to this new algorithm to improve the treatment of inequalities." (p. 10)

### S068 · Locally weighted regression models for surrogate-assisted design optimization (Optimization and Engineering 2018, 10.1007/s11081-017-9370-5)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: B. Talgorn (McGill, first author), S. Le Digabel, M. Kokkolaras (McGill) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: follows the order-error paper for ensembles of surrogates ([8], JOGO 2018; p. 6), and the text is the post-peer-review GERAD G–2016–113 version (p. 1). S114 later cites it as a source of dynamic models (S114 p. 6, p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The blackboxes are expensive, possibly nonsmooth, and may crash on hidden constraints (p. 5). Surrogates should assist the optimization, not substitute for it (p. 5). The entry is the limits of the group's earlier surrogates: polynomial response surfaces fit multimodal data badly, and kernel smoothing undershoots (p. 5). Two questions follow: how to measure a surrogate's accuracy for optimization, and which technique to use (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: LOWESS builds a local polynomial regression at each prediction point. It generalizes response surfaces (constant weights) and kernel smoothing (one basis function) (p. 6, p. 9). There are three contributions (p. 6–7):
  - All m+1 outputs are predicted with one linear solve, A_ξ u_ξ = e1, because only the first coefficient is needed. The solver is warm-started conjugate gradient, or Cholesky when the initial residual exceeds 10⁻⁴ (p. 9).
  - The scaling distance d_q(ξ) is chosen so that the expected number of data points within it is q, under a Gamma fit (moment estimates) to squared distances. This makes the model differentiable (p. 10–11).
  - The single shape parameter λ minimizes a leave-one-out aggregate order error plus log(λ)/p³ (p. 12–13).
  - Where it is inserted: the surrogate problem is solved in the MADS Search, the surrogate also ranks poll candidates, and true evaluations are opportunistic, in NOMAD 3.8 (p. 5, p. 13–14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - The regression basis grows with the number of points (Table 1, p. 8). Tikhonov regularization uses r = 10⁻³ with J11 = 0 to keep scale invariance (p. 7). A Gaussian kernel replaces the tri-cube because the latter gave ill-posed systems (p. 10).
  - The order-error premise: (P) and (P̂) share minimizers if the surrogate preserves the order of f and the sign of each c_j (conditions (7)–(8), p. 12). The outputs are aggregated through h = Σ max(0, c)², squared "to preserve the smoothness", and a lexicographic order (h first, then f) (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No new convergence analysis; the paper cites the hierarchical MADS analysis (p. 5). The devices are computational: the single-solve multi-output prediction (p. 9), and a tie-break log(λ)/p³ that makes the piecewise-constant metric prefer smoother models while staying an order of magnitude below it (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: Four engineering problems (TCSD, Vessel, Lockwood, Solar 6; n ≤ 6, m ≤ 6) with budgets from 100(n+1) to 1000(n+1) set by blackbox cost (Table 2, p. 13). Seven strategies: Dakota DiRect, SOGA and EA, quadratic models, kernel smoothing, LOWESS linear and quadratic (p. 14). 50 LHS starts, each with its own seed (p. 15). Reported: median relative discrepancy, data profiles at three tolerances, performance profiles both per evaluation and per wall-clock time with tmax = budget × mean blackbox time (p. 14–15), and infeasibility rates per strategy (Fig. 5, p. 16). The best final score is always a LOWESS method, with up to 22% more problems solved (p. 22). The advantage disappears in CPU time when the blackbox is cheap (p. 15, p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithmic contribution (surrogate modeling) plus software (NOMAD 3.8, p. 22).
  - Stated limits: the O(n⁶) cost per prediction restricts the method to small n or very expensive blackboxes (p. 6). It is "less advantageous for inexpensive blackboxes or large problems" (p. 22).
  - Future work: use order error to tune RBF or Kriging models, extend to multiobjective problems, and use the gradients of the now-differentiable model (p. 22).
  - Internal tension: the results paragraph calls linear LOWESS "particularly useful for expensive blackboxes", while the conclusion recommends linear for inexpensive blackboxes and quadratic for expensive ones (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the surrogate goes into the Search and into poll ordering, and the paper calls the approach surrogate-assisted, not surrogate-based (p. 5, p. 14, p. 22).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: baselines from another family (Dakota), engineering blackboxes, evaluation and wall-clock views, 50 starts with seeds, and a stated reason for using medians (p. 13–15).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: Solar 6 is used as a test problem with its first best-known value ("There was no previously best known objective function value for the Solar 6 problem"), and the best-known Vessel value is improved (Table 2, p. 13).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the framework ships in NOMAD 3.8 (p. 22).
- new-pattern candidate: *judge surrogates by ranking fidelity (order error) rather than by accuracy* (p. 6, p. 12–13). The same idea appears in S114 (poll ordering) and S144 (ordering by h̃).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Tune a surrogate hyperparameter by leave-one-out pairwise order error: count the pairs (xi, xj) whose better-of-the-two verdict (lexicographic in violation, then objective) flips between truth and cross-validated prediction (p. 12–13).
- (D2) Break ties in a piecewise-constant selection metric with a small smoothness term scaled to stay below the metric's resolution, here log(λ)/p³ (p. 13).
- (D2) Replace a non-differentiable k-th-nearest-neighbour distance with a quantile of a fitted distribution, so the surrogate is differentiable (p. 10–11).
- (D5) When the method has real overhead, report profiles in evaluations and in wall-clock time, with tmax = budget × mean blackbox time (p. 14–15).
- (D5) Use the median of relative discrepancies when values can be 0 or ∞ (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "However, surrogate optimization should be conducted carefully; it should be used to assist the process of optimizing the original problem, not substitute it entirely." (p. 5)
- "The use of the median is motivated by the wide ranges of magnitude that the discrepancy values can take; moreover, discrepancy can take the value of zero or infinity, which makes the use of the geometric or arithmetic means, respectively, impossible." (p. 15)

### S095 · Tuning Runge-Kutta parameters on a family of ordinary differential equations (International Journal of Mathematical Modelling and Numerical Optimisation 2018, 10.1504/ijmmno.2018.088992)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (single-authored, p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: the text is GERAD G–2017–18 (p. 1). It is an instance of treating the algorithm as a blackbox, citing OPAL and automated tuning frameworks (p. 5, p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: parameter tuning of fourth-order Runge–Kutta for one family of ODE systems. The stated aim is "to illustrate the high-level simplicity of parameter tuning" (p. 5). The RK4 class has 10 parameters bound by 8 nonlinear order conditions (Gill) (p. 5). The published choices optimize storage (Gill) or the truncation-error coefficient (Ralston), not the error on a given family (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - The 8 equations are eliminated analytically: given (β1, β5), all other parameters follow in closed form, with β4 = 1 (eq. 1, p. 6).
  - The blackbox ψ(β1, β5) is the root of the summed squared two-norm errors over 24 training systems (ℓ = 4..7 equations, n = 145..150 steps) (p. 7).
  - ψ = ∞ when the closed form is undefined (null denominator, negative γ) or when the RK iterates diverge (p. 7).
  - Result: an unconstrained problem in R² (p. 7), solved by NOMAD with default options from the classical point (0.5, 0) with 100 evaluations, through a one-line Matlab call (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: two families A and B with analytic solutions, C∞ in the region of interest (p. 7). ψ is shown to be nonsmooth, with holes where it is infinite (Fig. 1, p. 7–8). The choice of training sizes is admitted to be arbitrary (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none. The paper relies on the MADS analysis based on Clarke calculus (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - Training error: gains by a factor above 300 (A) and 5 (B) (p. 9).
  - Held-out validation: 24 disjoint systems (ℓ = 3..8, n ∈ {120, 140, 160, 180}), with average and worst error normalized to the classical parameters. Average gains are about 40 (A) and 4 (B), and the worst case also improves (p. 9).
  - Extrapolation: NOMAD_A solves ℓ = 9, 10, where all other parameter sets diverge, but fails at ℓ ≥ 11. NOMAD_B dominates on B up to ℓ = 25 (p. 9).
  - Cross-family transfer fails: the parameters tuned on B are worse than the classical ones on A (ψ 1.834 vs 1.364, Table 2, p. 9).
  - Other: Matlab's adaptive ode45 gives large errors on family A (p. 10). Only a single deterministic run per family was made.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a methodological demonstration. The stated limit is that tuned parameters are family-specific (p. 10). There is no future-work paragraph (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence: the algorithm is treated as a blackbox and tuned with direct search (p. 5, p. 8). The SKILL already cites this case.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: undefined parameters and numerical failures return ψ = ∞, handled by the extreme barrier rather than by a finite penalty (p. 7).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: a fixed evaluation budget, and literature baselines compared on held-out instances (p. 8–9), but no profiles and no replications.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: SKILL Method 4 lists a RUNGEKUTTA benchmark taken from a 2018 paper. This text defines ψ (p. 7) but says nothing about releasing it (not stated).
- new-pattern candidate: *train/held-out split for tuned algorithmic parameters* (p. 5, p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Before handing a tuning problem to a DFO solver, solve the algebraic consistency conditions in closed form, so the solver sees a low-dimensional unconstrained blackbox. Return ∞ wherever the closed form is undefined (p. 6–7).
- (D5) Tune on training instances, then report average and worst error on disjoint held-out instances, normalized so the default parameters score 1 (p. 9).
- (D5) Probe extrapolation outside the training range (larger systems) and report where the tuned parameters break (p. 9).
- (D7) Print the exact solver call to show how little setup the method needs (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Of course, these values could have been chosen differently, thereby leading to a different analysis." (p. 7)
- "The optimized parameters are specifically tuned for each family and may not be pertinent for use with other families." (p. 10)

### S124 · Algorithmic construction of the subdifferential from directional derivatives (Set-Valued and Variational Analysis 26(3) 2018, 10.1007/s11228-016-0388-1; arXiv:1609.02928)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: W. Hare (UBC Okanagan) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a stand-alone theory paper linking derivative-free approximations of the subdifferential (Bagirov, Kiwiel, Hare) to Geometric Probing (p. 2).

Text note: the file is arXiv v1 (stamp on p. 1) dated "September 27, 2018" (p. 1). Page 17 contains a stray block headed Algorithm 5.3, with a Theorem 5.4, that refers to "Algorithm 4.1", "Lemma 4.6" and "Theorem 4.3". It looks like a differently numbered version overlaid during extraction. The card follows the main numbering (Algorithms 3.1, 4.1, 4.3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: how many directional derivatives are needed to reconstruct a subdifferential. DFO methods approximate subdifferentials from approximate directional derivatives (p. 2). The paper takes the easiest case: a polytope subdifferential (a finite max of C¹ functions, where ∂f is the convex hull of the active gradients) and exact directional derivatives (p. 2–4). Two cases: a bound nf on the number of vertices is known, or it is not (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Abstraction: find the vertices of a polytope X from the support-function oracle D(d) = max over v in X of vᵀd. This is Hyperplane Probing without the contact location (p. 5).
  - R¹: one or two calls suffice (p. 6).
  - R² (Algorithm 3.1): keep an outer polytope P, initialized by three halfspaces (e1, e2, −e1−e2), and an inner vertex set S. Each probe along a direction d with dᵀa = dᵀc < dᵀb, where a, b, c are consecutive vertices of P, has three outcomes: b is confirmed, a and c are confirmed, or b is cut into two new candidates (p. 6–7).
  - Rⁿ with nf = 2 (Algorithm 4.1): a box from the 2n probes ±e_i, then one probe per coordinate to pair the bounds (p. 13–14).
  - Rⁿ with nf = 3 (Algorithm 4.3): lift from R² to Rⁿ one coordinate at a time, using the fact that each vertex of a projection is the projection of a vertex (p. 14–15).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: the subdifferential is a nonempty polytope, the oracle is exact, and nf ≥ nv is given (nf = ∞ allowed) (p. 4–5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 3.3 is proved by counting. Each oracle call moves some true vertex one stage along interior(P) → edge(P) → vertex(P) → confirmed, so at most 3nv calls are needed, plus one to remove a leftover candidate when nf > nv (p. 10–11). Example 3.2 shows the bound is attained (p. 12).
  - Lemma 3.6: any positive spanning initialization set costs at most m − 3 extra calls (p. 12–13).
  - Theorem 4.2 gives at most 3n − 1 calls (p. 14). Theorem 4.4, by induction on the dimension, gives 2n − 1, 5n − 3 and 5n − 1 calls for nv = 1, 2, 3 (p. 15–17).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Solved: R¹ and R² (Table 1, p. 18). Open: Rⁿ with an arbitrary nf (p. 18).
  - The results are "loosely viewed as providing a lower bound" on the approximate directional derivatives DFO would need (p. 3).
  - Next step: inexact oracles. The R¹ and nf = 1 algorithms adapt trivially, the main algorithms do not, and Figure 9 shows Algorithm 3.1 stalling when run with an inexact oracle as if it were exact (p. 18–19).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the theory takes the form of exact oracle-count bounds shown to be tight by an example (p. 10–12), not an asymptotic ladder. The scope boundary (exact oracle, nf ≤ 3 in Rⁿ) is stated, and the inexact case is shown to fail (p. 18–19).
- new-pattern candidate: *cross-field abstraction that imports known bounds*. The paper recasts the question as Hyperplane Probing, imports "3nv−1 probes are necessary, and 3nv probes are sufficient", and prices the missing information at one extra call (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D1/D2) Strip the question down to an oracle abstraction (here the support function of a polytope), and search other fields for the same abstraction before proving anything (p. 5).
- (D4) Prove oracle-count bounds by stage counting: define the finitely many states each true object passes through, then show every call advances some object one stage (p. 10–11).
- (D4) Reduce n-dimensional reconstruction to repeated low-dimensional ones through projections, and count calls by induction on the dimension (p. 14–17).
- (D8) Close a theory paper with a picture of how the algorithm breaks under the realistic, inexact oracle (p. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "Interestingly, this reduction of information has very little impact on the algorithm or convergence." (p. 5)
- "However, the more interesting algorithms (Algorithm 3.1, 4.1 and 4.3) are not so trivial to adapt." (p. 18)

### S125 · Selection of variables in parallel space decomposition for the mesh adaptive direct search algorithm (Les Cahiers du GERAD 2018, G–2018–38; no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Alarie (IREQ), N. Amaioua, S. Le Digabel, L.-A. Leclaire (IREQ) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: revisits PSD-MADS (2008) ten years later, taking up that paper's own concluding suggestion (p. 5). It reuses the variance-based ranking of STATS-MADS ([1], p. 9–10), and S144 cites it as a model for learning constraint importance (S144 p. 17). The title is printed truncated on the cover as "... for the mesh adaptive" (p. 1, p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: constrained blackboxes with large n (up to 4,000 variables), whereas NOMAD is recommended for up to 20 variables (p. 5). PSD-MADS selects each subproblem's variables at random, and the original paper suggested improving that (p. 5). The work is funded by an NSERC CRD grant with Hydro-Québec and Rio Tinto (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Sensitivity: for each variable and output, a variance-based coefficient is computed from the cache, with each variable's range binned into 10^Pvr intervals. The coefficients form a sensitivity matrix (p. 10).
  - Clustering: variables are grouped with k-means (LHS-initialized centroids, k searched in a √n-based range, Q or H), and the clusters are queued for the slave processes (p. 11–12). Four output-grouping strategies S1–S4 decide which outputs the clustering sees (p. 11).
  - Pns becomes an upper bound on subproblem size, so sizes are dynamic (p. 12).
  - Hybrid: after 3 queue refills without improvement, switch to random selection for 5n subproblems, then switch back (p. 12–13).
  - What is kept: the master, slaves, cache server and pollster. The pollster polls in the full space with directions that grow dense, which keeps the MADS convergence (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: problem (1) with blackbox c_j (p. 5). All tests start from feasible points, which rules out a strategy that splits violated from satisfied constraints (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no new analysis. Convergence rests on the pollster (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - Setup: 12 problems with n = 60 to 4,000 (B250, B500, CRESCENT, DISK, G2), budget 100n (60,000 for B250 and B500), 30 runs each, on 500 processes of Hydro-Québec's CASIR supercomputer. Evaluation is by data profiles (p. 14).
  - Parameter selection: one-factor-at-a-time sweeps from the base setting (5, 20, 3, Q, S1) give the defaults (2, 10, 4, Q, S2) (p. 14–17).
  - Hybrid: tested with the pre-default parameters because supercomputer access was limited (footnote, p. 17). It solves almost twice as many problems as k-means alone at τ = 10⁻³ and has the best worst-run value on 9 of 11 problems (p. 18).
  - G2-4000: average objective −0.079 (Original) vs −0.272 (Improved) and −0.277 (Hybrid) (Table 5, p. 19).
  - Scope: only PSD-MADS variants are compared.
  - A result the prose does not cover: on DISK-500, -1000 and -2000, Improved-5-20-3QS1 has a worse average than Original-5-20 (Table 4, p. 18). The sentence "all the versions of the improved method outperform the original" (p. 16) does not mention this. The hybrid repairs it (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a new algorithmic component plus parameter defaults. Future work: a surrogate Search inside the subproblems, principal feature analysis (PFA) on a dedicated slave, strategies for machines with few processes, grouping violated vs satisfied constraints for infeasible starts, and correlations between outputs (p. 19–20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the learned heuristic (sensitivity plus k-means) only decides which subspace the slaves explore, while the full-space pollster carries convergence (p. 8, p. 10–12).
- Heuristic 9 ✅ evidence: discovered structure (variable influence) is exploited inside the direct-search backbone (p. 10–11).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: data profiles and 30 replications at up to 4,000 variables (p. 14). However, only same-solver variants are compared, and the jointly chosen defaults are never rerun on the 11-problem set, only on G2-4000 (p. 17, p. 19).
- new-pattern candidate: *learned selection with a random fallback for diversification* (p. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When a learned selection rule starts repeating itself (the sensitivity matrix stabilizes and proposes already-evaluated points), switch to random selection for a fixed budget, then return (p. 12–13).
- (D2) Get variable–output sensitivities at no extra cost from the evaluation cache by binning each variable's range into 10^P intervals (p. 10).
- (D5) Tune several algorithm parameters one at a time from a baseline, one figure per parameter, each figure including the original method as reference (p. 14–17).
- (D7) Say in a footnote when a comparison used non-final parameters, and why (limited compute access) (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "This process is important since it allows to benefit from the convergence analysis of MADS [5]." (p. 8)
- "Access to the supercomputer is limited, and we could not use additional access time to perform additional runs." (p. 17)

### S114 · Dynamic improvements of static surrogates in direct search optimization (Optimization Letters 13(6) 2019, 10.1007/s11590-019-01452-7)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Côté-Massicotte (Polytechnique Montréal) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: the text is GERAD G–2019–10 (p. 1). It sits in the surrogate-management line: the surrogate management framework (SMF), quadratic-model ordering ([16]), and S068's LOWESS cited as [37] (p. 6, p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: surrogate-assisted direct search uses either static surrogates (simplified physics or relaxed internal tolerances, which need not approximate well) or dynamic models (quadratic, RBF, Gaussian process, ...) (p. 5–6). Earlier combinations of the two were only additive or multiplicative corrections ([24]) (p. 5–6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - The hybrid quadratic model (HQM) is a quadratic in n+1 inputs, (x, x0 = g̃(x)), fitted by regression or by minimum Frobenius norm with t+1 = (n+2)(n+3)/2 coefficients (p. 7–8).
  - It generalizes additive and multiplicative corrections and needs no derivative of the static surrogate (p. 7).
  - Where it is inserted: at each poll, the static surrogate is evaluated at the poll points. Cache points inside B∞(μ; ρr), a ρ-scaled bounding box of the poll set, are used to build m+1 HQMs, with the raw static surrogate as fallback when fewer than n+2 points are available. The HQMs then order the poll points, which are evaluated opportunistically (Algorithm 2, p. 10).
  - The Search step is left standard (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: X is unrelaxable (the simulation cannot be launched outside it), and the c_j are relaxable (p. 5), handled with PB (p. 10). Test problems must come with a static surrogate and be free of numerical noise, "otherwise the quadratic models would be irrelevant" (p. 10). The value of ρ used in the experiments was not found in the text; ρ = 2 appears only in the illustration (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Proposition 1: a set is poised for minimum-Frobenius hybrid modelling if and only if the KKT matrix F(φ, Y) is nonsingular (p. 9). A five-case analysis covers the number of points (p. 8–9). A 1-D step-function example shows that only the HQM captures both the discontinuity and the curvature (p. 9). No convergence statement is made, and the change is confined to poll ordering (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - Setup: NOMAD 3.8.0 with four poll orderings: success direction, quadratic, static and hqm (p. 10–11).
  - Simple MDO (10 and 16 variables, fidelity set by the MDA tolerance and iteration cap), 100 starts × 10 seeds = 1,000 runs per problem. hqm solves 25% and 55% more problems than static at τ = 10⁻⁷ (p. 11).
  - Simplified Wing: a slight preference for static, "not always dominant" (p. 11).
  - Lockwood, with a static surrogate at one fifth of the cost and 100 starts, profiled in evaluations and in CPU time: the two orderings that use the static surrogate dominate, and hqm is best (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a short algorithmic contribution. In the authors' reading, the static surrogate finds the basin and the quadratic part accelerates convergence inside it (p. 13). Future work: other dynamic models (such as LOWESS) in the Search or the Poll (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the model idea goes into poll *ordering*, another slot that cannot affect convergence (p. 10).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper reuses group blackboxes that come with a fidelity knob (Simple MDO, Simplified Wing, Lockwood) (p. 11–12).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: 1,000 seeded runs per MDO problem with data profiles, and CPU-time profiles on Lockwood (p. 11–12). The baselines are NOMAD orderings only, and the case where the new method does not win is reported (p. 11).
- new-pattern candidate: *surrogate value as an input feature of the model* (p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate (shared with S068 and S144): *order-first surrogate use* (p. 6, p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To combine a cheap static surrogate with a local model, add the surrogate's value as an extra input coordinate of the model. This covers additive and multiplicative corrections and needs no surrogate derivatives (p. 7).
- (D2) Build local models from cache points inside a scaled bounding box of the current poll set, and fall back to the raw surrogate when there are too few points (p. 10).
- (D5) State the test-problem selection criterion explicitly (here: comes with a static surrogate, noise-free) (p. 10).
- (D5) When the surrogate itself costs noticeable time, add data profiles against CPU time (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "Instead of simply applying an additive or multiplicative correction, the hybrid model treats the static model as an input." (p. 7)
- "These results suggest that the correction brought by HQM is not always dominant." (p. 11)

### S144 · Constraint scaling in the Mesh Adaptative Direct Search algorithm (Les Cahiers du GERAD 2019, G–2019–65; no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: G. Caporossi (HEC Montréal), S. Jacquet (UQAC) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: adds scaling of outputs to the earlier dynamic scaling of variables (Audet, Le Digabel, Tribes 2016, [8]) (p. 5). It builds on PB (2009) and on the Le Digabel–Wild constraint taxonomy (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: multiplying a constraint by a positive scalar leaves Ω unchanged but changes PB's aggregate violation h, so MADS-PB can return different solutions (p. 4–5). The example given is 1 km vs 1000 m (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - The violation becomes h(x; a) = Σ max(0, c_i/a_i)² (Definition 6, p. 8), with one of three dynamic weightings starting from a = 1:
    - the value of the first violation (p. 8);
    - the median of the first n violations (p. 8–9);
    - the running maximum violation, which maps violations into [0, 1] and gives binary constraints weight 1 (p. 9).
  - Each weighting's drawbacks are listed: an unrepresentative first violation, tiny weights, 10²⁰ error outputs from hidden constraints, frequent updates (p. 8–9).
  - Barrier fix: after a weight change, h_max is re-anchored so that the barrier still passes through the same cache point. Otherwise every point could be rejected and MADS would terminate (p. 9).
  - Optionally, the surrogate h̃ used for ordering is weighted too (p. 12–13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A1 (a user point x0 in X with finite f and h) and A2 (trial points in a compact set) are taken from PB (p. 7). A3 (each c_i bounded above on X) is added for uniform convergence (p. 12). The constraints c are relaxable and quantifiable, and X holds the unrelaxable ones (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - First and median weightings: the weights change at most once per constraint, so h is fixed after k_max and the PB analysis applies from there (p. 10).
  - Maximum weighting: a 1-D counterexample (f = −x on [0, 2], c = 0 on [0, 1] and 3 − x on ]1, 2], x0 = 0.9) rescales infinitely often (p. 10). Weaker results follow:
    - h(·; a^k) converges, because the weights are eventually monotone (Theorem 3, p. 10–11);
    - h_max is non-increasing from some rank (Theorem 4, p. 11);
    - an evaluated point's h never increases (Theorem 5, p. 11);
    - the convergence is uniform under A3 (Theorem 6, p. 12).
  - Left open: the PB result for the limit h(·; a) is stated as a hope ("Ideally ...") and not proved (p. 12).
  - Printed slips: Theorem 5's proof ends with "non-decreasing" (p. 11), and the convergence test (1) is printed with "≤" (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: NOMAD 3.8.0 with OrthoMADS and 1,500 evaluations (p. 13), evaluated with data profiles.
  - Problems: 5 analytic problems (500 instances from 100 feasible LHS starts) and 3 blackboxes (Styrene, MDO, Lockwood; 30 feasible starts), plus "unbalanced" twins in which constraint i is multiplied by 10^{j_i} (Table 3, p. 13–15).
  - Results: the maximum weighting wins on the analytic problems (58% vs under 35% at τ = 10⁻⁵, p. 14). The blackbox results are inconclusive (p. 15). With quadratic surrogates on the unbalanced blackboxes, weighting both h and h̃ dominates (p. 16).
  - Scope: only NOMAD variants are compared.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithmic idea with partial theory, and an honest report that the blackbox results are inconclusive (p. 17). Future work: learning constraint importance, for example by a sensitivity analysis like the one in [2] (= S125) (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: relaxable and quantifiable c vs unrelaxable X, with h = +∞ outside X (p. 5–6). The maximum weighting's weakness when hidden constraints return 10²⁰ (p. 9) shows why crashes must not be disguised as large finite values.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the counterexample comes first (p. 10), then the strongest properties that survive (Theorems 3–6, p. 10–12), with the remaining gap stated (p. 12).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: a synthetic stress test (rescaled twins) plus realistic blackboxes (p. 13–15), but no solver from another family, and the blackbox conclusions are reported as inconclusive (p. 15, p. 17).
- new-pattern candidate: *eventually-static adaptation*. An adaptive rule that changes only finitely often lets the existing analysis apply after the last change (p. 10). Compare S158's finite barrier migrations (c09).
- new-pattern candidate: *invariance stress test via rescaled twin problems* (p. 13–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) If an adaptive component changes the merit, make it change at most finitely often (for example once per constraint). Then invoke the existing analysis from the last change (p. 10).
- (D2) When the scale of a merit changes, re-anchor the acceptance threshold on the same stored point so that at least one candidate survives (p. 9).
- (D5) Test an invariance claim with "unbalanced" twins: multiply each constraint by 10^{j_i}, list the j_i in a table, and reuse the same starting points (p. 13–14).
- (D4) Before proving a property of an adaptive rule, build the 1-D example where the rule adapts infinitely often. Then prove the weaker monotonicity properties that still hold (p. 10–12).

**Quotes**:
- "In an optimization problem, multiplying an inequality constraint by a positive scalar has no effect on the domain. However, such a transformation might have an effect in practice." (p. 4)
- "The results on the blackbox problems were inconclusive." (p. 17)

### S166 · Low-cost and representative surrogate hydrological models. Part II – Use within calibration frameworks (Les Cahiers du GERAD 2019, G–2019–08; no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P.-L. Huot (ÉTS, first author), A. Poulin (ÉTS), S. Alarie (IREQ) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: continues Huot et al.'s hybrid DDS-MADS (2017) and Part I, which built the reduced-fidelity HYDROTEL models (p. 5). Audet is third author on a hydrology-led PhD project funded by an NSERC scholarship (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: calibration of HYDROTEL, a computationally intensive distributed hydrological model, in 10- and 19-parameter versions on 3 Québec watersheds (p. 5). The paper combines two routes, an efficient optimizer (DDS-MADS) and low-cost surrogates (p. 5). The stated challenge is to manage when to use the reduced-fidelity surrogate and when the original model (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: DDS-MADS runs the DDS global heuristic, then MADS for local refinement with its own stopping criterion (p. 7). Five transition features link the two (p. 7–8):
  - a shared cache;
  - a stagnation switch from DDS to MADS;
  - an initial mesh size set from the spread of good DDS points;
  - transfer of the remaining budget;
  - rounding the objective to damp numerical noise.

  Five calibration frameworks (p. 9–10):
  - A: DDS on the reduced-fidelity model, then MADS on the original.
  - B: everything on the reduced-fidelity model, with one final true evaluation.
  - C: B, then MADS on the original.
  - D: calibrate HYDROTEL 19 through the HYDROTEL 10 reduced model, then MADS on HYDROTEL 19.
  - E: like D, but through HYDROTEL 17, which drops the 2 costly parameters.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: the reduced-fidelity models use fewer meteorological stations, a shorter calibration period, or fewer spatial units. Each is qualified by its CPU-time ratio and by its representativeness (Spearman Rs and R² on 1 − NSE) (p. 8). HYDROTEL 10 surrogates are 16–44 times cheaper, HYDROTEL 19 surrogates only about 2 times (p. 9). The representativeness of the HYDROTEL 17 surrogate was never evaluated (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none; the paper is empirical.
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: 32 trials per watershed–framework pair, shown as boxplots of the final 1 − NSE. Time is compared with DDS-MADS-Bench and quality with DDS-Bench (p. 11). Framework B cuts time by 94–98% on HYDROTEL 10 (p. 11–12) but does not give a local optimum of the original model (p. 12). D and E are the best compromise on HYDROTEL 19, with 18–51% and 38–58% time reductions (p. 14–15). The contents of Tables 1–4 are not readable in the extraction.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - The contribution is a methodology that offers a menu of time–quality tradeoffs rather than one winner (p. 4, p. 15–16).
  - A CPU-time ratio above 15 is "ideal", while a ratio of about 2 does not justify a surrogate (p. 16).
  - The paper questions Toal's 0.9 representativeness threshold for reduced-fidelity models (p. 16).
  - Stated limits: the constructions are specific to HYDROTEL. Future work: other hydrological models, more watersheds and more objectives (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the heuristic and the rigorous parts run in sequence (DDS globally, then MADS locally with its stopping test) instead of being nested as Search and Poll (p. 7–8). The MADS phase on the true model is what restores local optimality, which framework B lacks (p. 12).
- Taste warning sign 6 ✅ evidence (heuristics are wrapped, not sold alone): DDS is given a MADS backbone (p. 7).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: wall-clock time is the currency, with 32 trials per setting (p. 11). However, time and quality are measured against two different benchmarks (p. 11), and no profiles are used.
- new-pattern candidate: *parameter-reduction fidelity*: build the cheap model by removing the few parameters that drive the cost (p. 10, p. 16).
- new-pattern candidate: *tradeoff menu instead of a single winner* (p. 15–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When chaining a global heuristic with MADS, share the cache, switch on stagnation, and set each mesh coordinate's initial size from the spread of the heuristic's good points (p. 7–8).
- (D2) Round the objective to the precision that matters for the application, to stop noise-driven mesh oscillation (p. 8).
- (D5) Before using a surrogate, report its CPU-time ratio and its rank correlation with the true model, and classify the ratio (ideal above 15) (p. 8–9, p. 16).
- (D5) End a surrogate-only run with one true evaluation, and state that the result is not a local optimum of the true model (p. 9, p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "It is important to keep in mind that framework B does not provide a local optimal solution for the original model." (p. 12)
- "No experiments have been completed to evaluate the level of representativeness and the CPU time ratios of the reduced-fidelity HYDROTEL 17 models, but this version is expected to yield good performance on CPU time ratio and representativeness." (p. 10)
