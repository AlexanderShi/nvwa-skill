**Batch intent**: In all eight entries MADS/NOMAD, or Audet's certification toolbox, is combined with another component:
- an ensemble of surrogates inside the Search (S046);
- a hydrology heuristic run before MADS (S073);

- whether the Kojtych–Batailly turbomachinery thread (S090 → S107) follows the "real blackbox → public artifact" route of Method 4.

### S046 · Order-based error for managing ensembles of surrogates in mesh adaptive direct search (Journal of Global Optimization 2018, 10.1007/s10898-017-0574-1)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Kokkolaras (McGill), S. Le Digabel, B. Talgorn (McGill) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: this is the *surrogate ensembles (JOGO 2018)* entry that SKILL.md cites as evidence for Method 1 and Taste mark 3. Its order-error metric is reused in S068 (s02.md) and S099 (s04.md). Funding: FRQNT and an NSERC/Hydro-Québec grant, with thanks to S. Alarie (IREQ) (p. 4). **Read**: pp. 1–30, with Appendices B–C and the references skimmed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A surrogate-based Search in MADS needs a choice of model, and "no single surrogate modeling technique can be deemed the best on a consistent basis" (p. 6). An ensemble is therefore built, with no fine-tuning of each model (p. 6). The entry point is a critique of the usual selection metrics:
  - RMSE and PRESS penalize outliers that do not matter to the optimizer;
  - a surrogate af(x) + b with a > 0 is perfect for surrogate-based optimization (SBO) despite a large RMSE (p. 6).

  The problem is (P): bounds plus blackbox constraints c_j ≤ 0 (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *Metric*: the order error E_OE has two parts. For the objective, it counts the pairs (x_i, x_l) for which f and f̂ disagree on which point is better (an XOR of signs). For each constraint, it counts the points whose feasibility verdict differs. E_OECV computes the same with leave-one-out predictions (pp. 12–13). E_OE lies in [0, 1], and a value above 0.5 means the model is worse than its opposite (p. 13).
  - *Ensemble*: 17 models: 6 polynomial response surfaces, 5 kernel smoothers and 6 RBFI models ("I" for "Incomplete") with greedy centre selection (Table 1, p. 11; pp. 9–10).
    - Selection is WTA2 (the best model per output), because optimizing the weights "tends to be equivalent to WTA2" and adding a diversity term did worse (p. 11).
    - Leave-one-out values have a closed form for linear models: ŷcv = y − diag(P)⁻¹Py (p. 9).
  - *Where it lives*: everything is in the Search.
    - An inner MADS with 10,000 model evaluations solves the surrogate problem (pp. 5, 15).
    - Its solution is projected onto the mesh by a new randomized projection (p. 8): min{2ⁿ, 100n} random perturbations, a greedy filter down to at most 100n candidates, and the candidate that is best on the surrogates.
    - The surrogates also order the poll points for opportunistic evaluation (p. 7).
  - *What changes in MADS*: only the mesh update. A Search success keeps the mesh size and skips the Poll, a Poll success multiplies it by 4, and a failure multiplies it by 0.25. The paper notes "This scheme is permitted in MADS" (p. 7).
  - Reading note: the text says the inner MADS uses no search step (p. 15), but also that it "strongly relies on LH sampling and VNS search" (p. 16; see also p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: There is no new theory. Convergence is kept because the Search returns finitely many mesh points (p. 7), and the conclusion says the projection "allows to conserve the convergence properties of MADS" (p. 22). The global-optimization claim is hedged: convergence toward a global minimizer "is contingent upon the accuracy of the chosen surrogate models" (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There are no theorems. The case for the metric rests on a toy function: x² for x ≤ 1/2 and 1 otherwise, sampled at ±1/k, which "emulate the sequence of iterates" (p. 13).
  - PRESS picks a quadratic whose minimizer is pulled away by the outlier.
  - OECV picks a kernel-smoothing model whose minimizer is correct (pp. 13–14).

  Figs. 2–3 show that the four metrics rank the 17 models differently (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 10 analytic problems (Table 4), plus the simplified wing (n = 7), aircraft range (n = 10) and Lockwood (n = 6) problems with 50 LH starting points each. The budget is 1000(n+1), or 100(n+1) for Lockwood. Seven MADS variants run in NOMAD 3.7.1 with defaults: None, Quad, VNS, and Select RMSE/PRESS/OE/OECV. The measures are the median relative discrepancy against groups of n+1 evaluations, plus data profiles (pp. 14–16, 26). OECV is best on the analytic set (best solution on 8 of 10 problems) and on Lockwood, where 72% of runs beat the literature's best value within 700 evaluations. On the wing problem, VNS overtakes it at the very end (pp. 17–20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The main contribution is the metric. The RBFI model and the projection are secondary (p. 22). The paper also explains *why* RMSE and PRESS lose: they keep selecting polynomial models, which cannot represent multimodal outputs (p. 22). Model-selection maps on Lockwood show this (Fig. 8, pp. 20–21). The only stated limit is that more expensive cross-validation variants were left out (p. 12). There is no future-work paragraph beyond a pointer to a fourth application, a trumpet, in [69] (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll) ✅ evidence: surrogates enter only through the Search and through poll ordering. The Search must return finitely many mesh points to keep MADS's convergence properties, so the surrogate solution is projected onto the mesh, even though off-mesh surrogate optimization "is often more efficient" (p. 7). Step 4 ✅: the variant with no Search ("None") is the baseline and "yields poor performance" (p. 16).
- Taste mark 3 ✅ evidence: surrogates speed up MADS at no cost to the Poll-based theory (pp. 5, 7, 22).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the simplified-wing and aircraft-range MDO problems and Lockwood are reused as realistic tests, with best-known values reported (pp. 18–20).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant:
  - the budget axis counts evaluations, there are 50 starting points and data profiles (pp. 15–16, 26), and the discrepancy is defined so that it works for constrained runs (p. 16);
  - but every competitor is a MADS variant, and no solver from another family is included (p. 15).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: all runs use NOMAD 3.7.1, with default values for every parameter not being tested (p. 16).
- new-pattern candidate: *surrogates judged and used as rankers* (existing name). S046 is where the corpus introduces OE/OECV (pp. 12–13), and S068 and S099 reuse it.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Score a model by the decisions it will drive, not by its fit: count pairwise order flips for the objective and feasibility flips for each constraint, using leave-one-out predictions (pp. 12–13).
- (D2) If a surrogate is optimized better off the mesh, let it run off the mesh and project afterwards. Project onto the submeshes around all cached points, using random perturbations, and keep the projection that is best on the surrogate rather than the nearest point (pp. 7–8).
- (D4) Motivate a new metric with a 1-D function sampled on an iterate-like sequence (±1/k) where the classical metric selects a model with the wrong minimizer (p. 13).
- (D5) For constrained benchmarks, normalize each run by the best value and the worst first-feasible value, plot the median over runs, and put data profiles in an appendix (pp. 16, 26).

**Quotes**:
- "Our main concern in SBO is to find the correct optimizer, not necessarily the correct optimum." (p. 6)
- "The poll rigorously ensures global convergence toward a solution satisfying some local optimality conditions, while the search can implement any method likely to improve the efficiency and global exploration of the optimization process in order to escape local optima." (p. 5)

### S073 · A hybrid optimization approach for efficient calibration of computationally intensive hydrological models (Hydrological Sciences Journal 2019, 10.1080/02626667.2019.1624922)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P.-L. Huot (first author, ÉTS), A. Poulin (ÉTS), S. Alarie (IREQ/Hydro-Québec) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a hydrology application within the Hydro-Québec collaboration, with Audet as third author. The future-work paragraph announces surrogate models of HYDROTEL (pp. 41–42). Reader's link from titles only, not stated in this text: this may be the topic of the *Low-cost and representative surrogate hydrological models* papers S167 and S166 (s03.md, s02.md). Funding came from an NSERC PhD scholarship, and C. Tribes and S. Le Digabel are thanked for help with NOMAD (p. 42). **Read**: all 42 pages. Tables and figures are lost (see the reading note).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Calibrating the distributed hydrological model HYDROTEL is a blackbox problem in which only objective values are available. One simulation takes about 1–3 minutes (pp. 11–12), and operational use needs recalibration "up to daily" (p. 7). The entry point is a comparison of three standard calibrators:
  - DDS finds good solutions fastest, but then spends simulations for little gain;
  - MADS and SCE-UA need 500–1,000 simulations to match what DDS reaches in about 250 (pp. 19–20).

  The objective is 1 − NSE, minimized over 10 or 19 bound-constrained parameters (HYDROTEL 10 and 19) (pp. 8, 10).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: DDS explores globally, then MADS refines locally and supplies its automatic stopping criterion (p. 21). Neither source code is changed. Instead, five "transition features" are attached around the two codes (pp. 21–22):
  1. a shared cache, which avoids repeated simulations and records sensitivity information (p. 22);
  2. an anisotropic initial mesh size for MADS, taken from the spread of the good cached points (those within 10% of the worst–best gap), with three rules (pp. 22–23):
     - if a parameter is constant across these points, 10% of its range;
     - if it covers the range, 50%;
     - otherwise, the ratio of its spread to its range;
  3. a stagnation counter that stops DDS after 2N consecutive non-improving simulations, chosen because MADS refines its mesh after 2N failures (pp. 23–24);
  4. a single total budget, with MADS getting whatever DDS leaves (pp. 24–25);
  5. rounding 1 − NSE to 3 digits, because differences below 0.005 are hydrologically meaningless and the mesh would otherwise keep adapting to noise (p. 26).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The only constraints are bounds, and the objective is described as nonsmooth, discontinuous and noisy (p. 3). MADS is presented with its Clarke-derivative optimality condition (eq. 4.2, pp. 15–16). The hybrid is said to provide "final solutions that satisfy necessary optimality conditions" (p. 30). What the rounding of feature 5 does to those conditions is not discussed.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no analysis. The settings are justified by small sensitivity studies on the Ceizur watershed: 4 trials per number of digits for the rounding (Fig. 4.4, pp. 26–27), and a sensitivity analysis for the 2N threshold (p. 24).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 32 trials per method from the same 32 random starting points, a budget of 2,000 simulations, default settings "in the interest of fair comparison", and DDS, MADS and SCE-UA as benchmarks, shown as boxplots at 250, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 simulations. The hybrid was developed on two watersheds and validated on a third, "completely independent" one, then on a 10-year validation period and against equal-budget baselines DDS-600 and DDS-1200. It saves about 70% (HYDROTEL 10) and 40% (HYDROTEL 19) of the simulations, with final values within 0.02 of 1 − NSE of the full-budget DDS runs (pp. 16–18, 27–38). The whole study used more than 16,900 hours of computation (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The stated contribution is the set of transition features (p. 6). The claimed advantages are: no tuning of algorithm parameters, an automatic stop, and a single core (p. 39). The stated limits are methodological choices forced by the cost of the experiments, a single objective function (NSE) and a single model (p. 41). Future work: surrogates (low-fidelity physical models and response surfaces) and biobjective and parallel versions (pp. 40–42).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the heuristic is not placed in the Search; it runs *before* MADS. MADS supplies the refinement and the stopping rule, and it rescues DDS runs trapped in poor regions (p. 35). The handover is tuned to MADS's own unit of failure, 2N (p. 24).
- Taste warning sign 6 ✅ evidence: heuristics "do not guarantee convergence to local nor to global optimal solutions", while direct-search methods "are not heuristics" (p. 5). The hybrid gives the heuristic a convergent finisher.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence:
  - the currency is the number of simulations: "it is the number of simulations that is decisive" (p. 17);
  - the baselines come from other families (DDS, SCE-UA) and run with their default settings (p. 16);
  - all methods use the same starting points (p. 18);
  - equal-budget baselines are added "In the interest of fair comparison" (p. 37).

  ⚠ Results are shown as boxplots at checkpoints, not as data or performance profiles (pp. 18–19).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: NOMAD 3.5 is used as is (p. 16), and the paper advertises NOMAD's features and support (p. 40).
- new-pattern candidate: *train/held-out split for tuned algorithmic parameters* (existing name): the third watershed, Toulnustouc, was kept out of the design phase "to avoid overfit" (p. 6; p. 34).
- new-pattern candidate: *Evaluation cache as shared memory and data source* (existing name): the DDS cache sets MADS's initial anisotropic mesh (pp. 22–23).
- new-pattern candidate: *round outputs to the decision-relevant precision*: rounding the objective stops the mesh from chasing differences that users cannot act on, and saved about 300 simulations (p. 26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When chaining a global heuristic and a convergent local method, express the handover threshold in the local method's own unit: here 2N non-improving evaluations, the cost of one failed MADS poll (p. 24).
- (D2) Set the local method's initial step size per coordinate from the spread of the near-best points in the first method's cache (pp. 22–23).
- (D5) Design the algorithm on some instances and validate it on a held-out instance that played no part in the design (p. 6; p. 34).
- (D5) After reporting savings, add baselines that get exactly the budget your method used (DDS-600, DDS-1200) (p. 37).

**Quotes**:
- "are not heuristics since they are supported by a convergence analysis" (p. 5)
- "no changes were made in an effort to maintain a high level of simplicity and to respect the original optimization strategies of both algorithms." (p. 22)

### D003 · Numerical certification of Pareto optimality for biobjective nonlinear problems (Journal of Global Optimization 83(4):891–908, 2022, 10.1007/s10898-022-01127-1)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: F. Messine (ENSEEIHT-LAPLACE, Toulouse), J. Ninin (ENSTA-Bretagne) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the extremal-polygon side line, extended to two objectives. The Audet–Hansen–Messine polygon papers are refs [4]–[8] (pp. 23–24), and related polygon papers are carded as S104 and S146 (s2-03) and S113 and S142 (s04). This is not DFO, since the functions are explicit. S132 is its correction. Funding: NSERC grant 239436-01 (p. 2). **Read**: all 27 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper does not generate Pareto fronts; it certifies a conjectured one. The setting is a user who "has what he believes to be the Pareto front, and wishes to certify numerically that it is true" (p. 3). This is the biobjective analogue of a global-optimality certificate, except that a whole set of solutions must be validated (p. 3). The conjecture comes in one of two formats (pp. 3–4):
  - explicit: one objective given as a function of the other;
  - parameterized: both objectives given as functions of one scalar.

  The test class is new: maximize the area and the perimeter of hexagons of unit diameter, equilateral or not (pp. 8–9).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *Definition*: an ε-Pareto approximation is a set in which each conjectured point is attained and no feasible v beats it by ε in both objectives (Def. 2, p. 4). A front may be a union of parts on disjoint intervals (Def. 3, p. 5).
  - *Reduction*: the conjecture b = g(a) on I is exact if and only if max f2(x) − g(f1(x)) subject to f1(x) ∈ I equals 0 (Prop. 4, pp. 5–6). For a parameterized conjecture, q becomes a variable and the constraint is f1(x) ≥ g1(q) (Prop. 5, p. 7).
  - *Certificate by infeasibility*: in that reduced problem every Pareto point is a global optimum, which makes it numerically hard. So the paper adds the constraint f2 − g(f1) ≥ ε. The question becomes a constraint-satisfaction problem that a complete solver with rigorous rounding must prove infeasible (Problem 3, p. 6; Problem 5, p. 7). Any such interval-based solver can be used. The paper uses IBBA, the interval branch and bound developed in Messine's and Ninin's PhD work (pp. 6, 18).
  - *Making the problem tractable*:
    - symmetry-breaking constraints (E4–E5, E8–E9, pp. 11–12);
    - Lemma 6, which bounds every side of a Pareto hexagon by 0.978 with an area argument (p. 13);
    - when IBBA still does not finish, Prop. 7 splits the Pareto hexagons into three diameter-graph cases (3, 2 or 1 main diagonals are diameters), which gives 6 subproblems (pp. 19–20).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Every conjectured point must be attained by some feasible x (hypothesis of Props. 4–5, pp. 5, 7). For IBBA, equalities are relaxed by δ = 10⁻¹⁰, and infeasibility of the relaxed problem implies infeasibility of the original one (p. 18). The certificate depends on ε: one bound constant was rounded up, so a too small ε would leave a feasible point; ε = 10⁻⁶ is large enough (Remark, p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The propositions have short "if and only if" proofs (pp. 6–7). The proof of Prop. 5 reuses the wording of Prop. 4 and refers to "Problem (2)" where Problem (4) is meant (p. 7). Prop. 7 is a case analysis on diameter graphs that uses two facts (pp. 19–20):
  - every vertex is an endpoint of a diameter, because moving it outward increases both objectives;
  - any two diameters intersect [7].

  Lemma 6 is an area-containment bound (p. 13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: IBBA certifies the equilateral front in 0.46 s (p. 19). The two-part non-equilateral front takes 23 h 50 min over the 6 subproblems (Table 3, p. 21). Tolerances are ε = 10⁻⁶ and δ = 10⁻¹⁰ (pp. 19, 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a general certification method, a new class of extremal problems, and certified fronts for hexagons (pp. 21, 23). The non-equilateral front is discontinuous; its endpoints are Graham's hexagon and the clipped-Reuleaux hexagon (p. 12). Future work (p. 23):
  - certify that the front satisfies multiobjective first-order conditions [14];
  - handle more than two objectives. The named obstacles are surfaces instead of curves, parameterizations in more than one variable, and the loss of the ordering property.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder, bounded by counterexamples) ⚠ variant: the guarantee is a finite-precision rigorous certificate (ε-Pareto, obtained with a complete interval solver), not an asymptotic ladder. The "if and only if" propositions do the job that counterexamples do for convergence theorems: they show that the reduction loses nothing (pp. 5–7).
- new-pattern candidate: *ε-uniqueness by exclusion constraint* (existing name, S142), here in biobjective form: add an ε-improvement constraint and prove the problem infeasible (pp. 6–7).
- new-pattern candidate: *analytic pruning before a global-solver certificate* (existing name, S142): Lemma 6 and the Prop. 7 case split are added exactly because IBBA did not finish without them (pp. 13, 19).
- new-pattern candidate: *extremal-geometry side line: numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later* (existing name): "Preliminary numerical experiments suggest that the Pareto front is composed of two disjoint parts" (p. 13) before the conjecture is certified. Here, though, the certificate is numerical, not algebraic.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To certify a conjectured set of optimal trade-offs, write the statement that no feasible point beats the conjecture by ε in both objectives as a feasibility problem, and prove it infeasible with a complete solver that controls rounding (pp. 4–7).
- (D2) If the certifying solver stalls, prove structural lemmas (a bound on the side lengths, a case split on combinatorial configurations), add them as constraints, and solve one subproblem per case (pp. 13, 19–21).
- (D3) State the tolerance of the certificate, and check that it is large enough not to be defeated by constants you rounded (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "The goal is now to apply a global optimization algorithm to this problem to show that there are no feasible solutions." (p. 6)
- "Indeed IBBA can guarantee that an optimization problem has strictly no feasible solution." (p. 18)

### S090 · Escaping unknown discontinuous regions in blackbox optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 32(3):1843–1870, 2022, 10.1137/21m1420915)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. Batailly and S. Kojtych (Mechanical Engineering, Polytechnique Montréal) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**:
- This paper introduces DiscoMads and its "revealing poll".
- The Math. Program. 2024 counterexample note (S138, c03.md) reuses that revealing poll and adapts Lemma 4.12. The covering-step paper carded in c06.md places DiscoMads' revealing step at the start of its discontinuous-DFO chain.
- The paper cites Vicente & Custódio (2012) neutrally, as deriving optimality conditions for discontinuous functions (p. 5). S138 later corrects a result of that same paper.
- The motivation and the third application come from Batailly's turbomachinery group, and S107 continues that line.

Funding: NSERC RGPIN-2020-04448, the Canada Research Chairs program and FRQNT (p. 26). **Read**: all 28 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Nonlinear mechanical systems, such as blades in contact with their casing, have nonlinear resonance regions where the simulated outputs jump. A design must stay *away* from these regions, because the computed discontinuity does not match the true resonance region exactly (p. 4).
  - *Formulation*: a constrained blackbox problem with an extra constraint d(x) ≤ 0, meaning "away from discontinuities" (1.1). This d "requires an infinite number of evaluations to be deemed feasible" (p. 5).
  - *Unsafe set D*: the points where one of the selected outputs changes faster than rate τ within radius r_d. *Margin M*: the r_e-neighbourhood of D. The problem becomes min f over Ω̂ = Ω \ M (1.2) (p. 6).
  - *Gap in prior work*: earlier discontinuous-optimization methods (Birgin et al.; Wechsung and Barton) "do not give control on the distance to the discontinuity" and do not treat discontinuities in the constraints (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: DiscoMads is Mads plus the following mechanisms (p. 8):
  - *Revelation*: after each evaluation, the new point is compared with cached points within r_d. If a rate |c_j(y) − c_j(z)|/‖y − z‖ exceeds τ, both points become revealing points. The set D^k of revealing points grows monotonically (§3.1, p. 8).
  - *Exclusion*: a relaxable, quantifiable constraint d^k(x) = 1 − dist(x, D^k)/r_e is imposed inside the r_e-ball of each revealing point (0 elsewhere). It is added to the violation function h and handled by the progressive barrier (§3.2, pp. 8–9).
  - *Revealing iteration*: when a new revealing point is found, the iteration stops opportunistically. The mesh and frame sizes are kept, d and h are recomputed on the cache, and h_max is reset so that the number of infeasible undominated points below the threshold stays the same (§3.3, pp. 9–10).
  - *Revealing poll*: n_rnd random points are drawn in a ball of radius r_m > r_d + r_e around an incumbent, rounded to the mesh, and evaluated before the classical poll (§3.4, p. 10). The reason given: once the frame size falls below r_d + r_e, the classical poll "can no longer detect revealing points at a sufficient distance to ensure the convergence results" (p. 10).
  - With r_d = 0 the algorithm "is identical to Mads" (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Three assumptions (pp. 11–12):
  - A4.1: x⁰ ∈ X, with f(x⁰) and h(x⁰) finite;
  - A4.2: all iterates lie in a bounded set;
  - A4.4: the outputs are piecewise continuous, in a tailored sense (Def. 4.3).

  The outputs are deterministic (p. 11). The stated restrictions are one rate bound τ shared by all revealing outputs and mandatory input scaling, since the anisotropic mesh is disabled (pp. 17, 25).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The analysis follows the Mads analysis (p. 10).
  - Thm 4.7 shows that the mesh becomes infinitely fine. The proof is by contradiction, using a point that would be visited infinitely often (pp. 12–13).
  - Table 4.1 maps each case to a theorem and to its assumptions (p. 14). A case is a feasible or infeasible refining sequence together with the location of the limit x̂: in Ω̂, in X \ M, or in M.
  - Thms 4.10–4.11 are Mads results carried over, because d^k = 0 outside M (pp. 13–14).
  - The new Thm 4.14 shows that x̂ is a *local minimizer* of ĥ on X, "a stronger optimality condition than those derived for Mads" (p. 16). Its engine is two lemmas:
    - Lemma 4.12: the revealing poll makes the trial points dense in B_{r_m}(x̂), through a positive volume-ratio probability (pp. 14–15);
    - Lemma 4.13: h^k → ĥ near x̂, proved in three cases (pp. 15–16).
  - Reader's observation: Lemma 4.12 is stated without an almost-sure qualifier, although its proof is a positive-probability argument (p. 15). According to card S138 (c03.md), the lemma adapted in the 2024 note is stated as almost-sure density.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The runs use NOMAD 3.9.1 with r_m = 1.01(r_e + r_d) and n_rnd = n. Quadratic models are turned off as "ill suited to representing discontinuous functions", and only the speculative search is kept (p. 17). The test cases (pp. 18–25):
  - a 2-D analytic problem, with 100 seeds and a sweep over r_e;
  - a two-bar truss that can buckle, with 100 seeds, feasible and infeasible x⁰, and budgets of 100, 200 and 2000 evaluations;
  - styrene, with 8 variables and the hidden-constraint regions turned into jumps, with 100 seeds;
  - a turbomachine blade on an interpolation of 45,000 simulations: 4 instances, one seed, and a stress-field check of the returned designs.

  No competing solver is run.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The claim is "an algorithm with proven convergence" that returns a design safe from weak discontinuities whose positions are unknown (p. 6). Observed behaviour:
  - the best feasible f is not monotone, because a revealing iteration can make the incumbent infeasible (p. 19);
  - with small budgets, some returned solutions still lie in D (Fig. 9, p. 21).

  Named limits and future work (p. 25): mandatory input scaling, a single τ for all outputs, a better revealing mechanism, a reliability index for early stopping, and surrogates, which were left out "for clarity".

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence:
  - a case-by-case guarantee table (Table 4.1, p. 14);
  - the old results are kept, and one new, stronger rung is added (Thm 4.14, p. 16);
  - each assumption is tied to the rung that needs it: A4.4 is used only for Thm 4.14 (p. 14).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the new mechanism goes *into the Poll*. The random revealing poll runs before the classical poll because the stronger theorem needs dense sampling that does not shrink with the frame (p. 10), and Def. 4.8 counts it as part of the poll step (p. 13). The Search is kept minimal, with only the speculative search (p. 17). Card S138 records that the 2024 note treats the same step as a specific Search step (c03.md).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence:
  - the new constraint is declared "quantifiable and relaxable" and handed to the progressive barrier (p. 9);
  - points outside X (extreme barrier) are rejected and never used for detection (p. 10);
  - in styrene, the binary constraints go to the extreme barrier and the relaxable ones to the progressive barrier (p. 22).

  ⚠ Variant of step 4 and of the SKILL.md anti-pattern about wrapping crashes with a large finite penalty: in styrene, the blackbox is modified "to return an artificially high value" of the objective and relaxable constraints when a hidden constraint is violated. Failure regions then become jumps that DiscoMads can detect (p. 22). This works here because the solver's job is to detect jumps, and the quadratic models are turned off (p. 17).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence:
  - the truss blackbox is released online (footnote, p. 19);
  - styrene is reused from bbopt (p. 21);
  - the turbomachine problem comes from an industrial finite-element model; for development purposes, the paper replaces it with a piecewise linear interpolation of 45,000 runs (p. 24).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: seeds are replicated (100 runs) and the effect of the budget is shown (pp. 19–21), but there is no competitor and no profile. Success is judged by the distance to the discontinuity and by a count H(x, σ) of nearby hidden-constraint violations (p. 22).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: DiscoMads is implemented in NOMAD 3.9.1 (p. 17).
- new-pattern candidate: *fixed-radius revealing sampling* (existing name, S138). This paper is where it first appears in the corpus (§3.4, p. 10; Lemma 4.12, pp. 14–15).
- new-pattern candidate: *Generalize-and-recover* (existing name): r_d = 0 gives back Mads (p. 6), and the Mads theorems are recovered outside the margin (pp. 13–14).
- new-pattern candidate: *infinite constraint approximated from the cache*: a constraint that no finite number of evaluations can certify is replaced by a sequence d^k built from cached points. The sequence d^k(x) is nondecreasing and converges (Property 4.6, p. 12), and near refined points it converges to the true d (Lemma 4.13, pp. 15–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Turn the requirement to stay away from an unknown bad region into a relaxable constraint d^k(x) = 1 − dist(x, D^k)/r_e, where D^k is a growing set of detected points, and hand it to the progressive barrier (pp. 8–9).
- (D2) When a detected event changes the constraint, freeze the mesh and reset the barrier threshold so that the number of infeasible undominated points below it does not change (pp. 9–10).
- (D4) Write the convergence section as a table: the case of refining sequence and the location of its limit → the theorem → the assumptions used (Table 4.1, p. 14).
- (D5) Check the returned design physically, including the points at distance r_e toward the nearest discontinuity, not only its objective value (stress fields, pp. 24–25).

**Quotes**:
- "The constraint d requires an infinite number of evaluations to be deemed feasible." (p. 5)
- "When the parameter rd is fixed to 0 by the user, the region D is empty and the behavior of the algorithm is identical to Mads." (p. 6)

### S186 · Second order sliding mode twisting controller tuning based on two-level optimization process (2022 IEEE 61st Conference on Decision and Control (CDC), 10.1109/cdc51059.2022.9993282)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: D. Monnet (first author, GERAD/Polytechnique Montréal), A. Goldsztejn (CNRS-LS2N), F. Plestan (Centrale Nantes-LS2N) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: a control-engineering application of NOMAD. The less conservative convergence conditions come from Monnet, Goldsztejn and Plestan, Automatica 2022 [9] (p. 3). The work is supported in part by the NSERC Alliance grant with Huawei-Canada, and S. Le Digabel is thanked for help with NOMAD (p. 6). **Read**: all 7 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Finite-time convergence proofs for sliding-mode controllers rely on bounds on perturbation terms. Over-approximated bounds lead to conservative, large gains that amplify chattering, so in practice "the control law is often tuned by simulation rather than derived from the convergence conditions" (p. 2). Earlier optimization-based tuning used SIP solvers, which handled only first-order sliding mode (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: a two-level process (Fig. 1, p. 5).
  - Lower level: for given gains (r1, r2), a branch-and-bound global solver (IBEX) computes guaranteed bounds m_i, M_i on σ̈ in each of the four quadrants of the (σ, σ̇) plane (pp. 4–5).
  - Upper level: NOMAD minimizes f(r1, r2) = r1 + r2 subject to the conditions of Theorem 2: M1, M2 < 0; m3, m4 > 0; M1m3 < m2M4. These constraints have no explicit expression and no derivatives (pp. 3, 5).
  - Why the upper level is local: the twisting controller has more constraints, more variables and more complex expressions than first-order sliding mode. This makes "a global approach intractable", so a locally convergent DFO method is chosen (p. 4).
  - Theorem 3 replaces the unbounded time t with a box P of perturbation values. The resulting bounds are conservative but computable (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The assumptions are stated on pp. 2–3:
  - σ̈ is affine in the control u;
  - σ, σ̇, h and g have known explicit expressions, which is "needed to employ optimization tools";
  - the perturbation p(t) stays in a known box P;
  - the state stays in a known compact set X.

  The output is a pair of gains "guaranteed to ensure finite time convergence" and "locally optimal" among such gains (p. 4). The text does not say how the constraints are declared to NOMAD (extreme or progressive barrier).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 3's proof: a minimizer over t ≥ 0 is feasible for the problem posed over p ∈ P, so [l⁺, u⁺] ⊇ [l*, u*] (p. 4). Remark 1 shows that the classical Theorem 1 conditions are Theorem 2 with the crude bounds (14) plugged in, so Theorem 2 is never more conservative (p. 3).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: A pendulum with varying arm length. IBEX with relative precision 0.01 gives C = 26.65, k = 0.82 and K = 4.04. A 100 × 100 grid, run in 70 minutes, maps the set of gains that satisfy Theorem 2. One NOMAD run (defaults, 500 evaluations, 8 minutes) starts from the best Theorem-1 gains (129, 96.5) and reaches (47.1, 36.6). Simulation shows less chattering: the L2 error drops by 59.99% during sliding (pp. 5–6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a method that returns certified, less conservative gains (p. 6). The limits are computational cost and local optimality only (pp. 4, 6). Future work: even less conservative conditions, and other second-order sliding-mode controllers (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 7 ⚠ variant: direct search tunes the parameters of a controller, and each blackbox evaluation is itself a rigorous global optimization whose outputs are certified bounds. Feasibility of the answer is therefore guaranteed, not just estimated (pp. 4–5).
- Heuristic 9 ✅ evidence: the inner global solver exploits the known structure (explicit h, g and σ̈), while the outer level stays derivative-free (pp. 2, 5).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the guarantee that matters, finite-time convergence of the controller, comes from control theory and is enforced through guaranteed bounds. The DFO guarantee is only local optimality (p. 4).
- Method 5 ✗: one NOMAD run, with no seeds and no other DFO solver. The grid only illustrates the feasible set (p. 6).
- new-pattern candidate: *feasibility-by-relay warm start* (existing name, S204): NOMAD starts from the optimum of the conservative linear conditions, a point already certified feasible (p. 6).
- new-pattern candidate: *singular/smooth variable split* (existing name, S151), in a variant that could be called *rigorous inner solver as the blackbox*: DFO works outside on the few design variables, and a global solver works inside to produce the certificate (Fig. 1, p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When a sufficient condition involves the sup or inf of explicit functions, compute these with a rigorous global solver inside the blackbox, and let a DFO solver optimize the design subject to the certified values (pp. 4–5).
- (D2) Replace an unbounded time dependence with a box on the time-varying inputs. It is conservative, but it gives a bounded, solvable problem (Theorem 3, p. 4).
- (D5) Warm-start from the best design under the older, conservative sufficient condition, so that any improvement comes from the sharper condition (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Local minima or maxima cannot be used because inequalities would not be guaranteed to hold." (p. 4)
- "As a consequence, the control law is often tuned by simulation rather than derived from the convergence conditions." (p. 2)

### S107 · Methodology for the redesign of compressor blades undergoing nonlinear structural interactions: Application to blade-tip/casing contacts (Journal of Engineering for Gas Turbines and Power 145(5):051002, 2023, 10.1115/1.4055681)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Kojtych (first author), F. Nyssen, A. Batailly (Mechanical Engineering, Polytechnique Montréal) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: same group and funding as S090 (NSERC RGPIN-2020-04448, Canada Research Chairs, FRQNT; p. 20). It extends an earlier study of blade parameterization and blackbox optimization [18] (p. 4). Audet is the only optimization specialist among the authors. **Read**: all 22 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Blades are still designed with empirical, often linear, criteria. These can fail under nonlinear contact, and the resulting redesigns are very costly, since planes may be grounded (p. 4). Avoiding them needs two things: an easy way to update an existing blade, and physically relevant design criteria (p. 4). The paper builds a three-step pipeline (p. 2; Fig. 1, p. 5):
  1. parameterize an existing blade;
  2. update it against a surrogate performance criterion;
  3. check the optimized blade with full nonlinear dynamic analysis.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *Two-way parameterization*: each section is described by 5 stacking parameters and 14 profile parameters (Table 1, p. 6).
    - Parameters are extracted from a CAD model by fitting each section with NOMAD: the error is e(x) = 0.9 d̄ + 0.1|δp|, with 11 free variables and 1500 evaluations (about 5 minutes), and convergence was checked on 17 profiles (p. 9).
    - A CAD model and a finite-element model can then be regenerated from any parameter vector (pp. 9–10).
  - *Update*: minimize the clearance consumption at the leading edge along one vibration mode (p. 10). There are 4 variables, the sweep and lean at sections 3 and 5, each evaluation takes about 45 s, and the run stops after 400 simulations with NOMAD 3.9.1 defaults (p. 11). MADS is chosen because it handles time-consuming simulations and "situations in which the model generation returns an error or fails" (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The only constraints are bounds, (7) (p. 11). Profile parameters are fixed to preserve aerodynamic properties (p. 11). The bounds are small enough to assume that the first two modes remain first bending (1B) and first torsion (1T) (p. 13). The parameterized model is checked before use: 0.06% error on the first eigenfrequency and less than 2% on average over the first ten (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no mathematical analysis. The results are judged physically, with nonlinear frequency response curves and modal contribution maps computed by time integration on a Craig–Bampton reduced model (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: NASA rotor 37 (instances A and B) and rotor 67 (instances C, D and E). Each run cuts its target criterion by 58–98% (pp. 14, 17). On rotor 37, the peak amplitude drops from about 20 mm to about 5 mm (p. 15). On rotor 67, every optimized blade reaches a higher maximum amplitude than the initial blade (pp. 18–19). There is one run per instance and no other optimizer.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are the pipeline and a test of its criterion. Clearance consumption works when one mode dominates the dynamics (rotor 37) and fails when several modes contribute (rotor 67) (pp. 19–20). Future work: clearance consumption over several nodes and modes, centrifugal effects, other nonlinear interactions, and intentional mistuning (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Taste mark 1 ✅ evidence: a real industrial finite-element blackbox whose pathologies are named, such as non-periodic responses and failures of model generation (pp. 11, 16).
- Method 2 ✅ weak evidence: model-generation failures are a stated reason for choosing MADS (p. 11). There are no other constraints.
- Method 4 ✅ weak evidence: the input CAD models of rotors 37 and 67 are "freely available online" (footnote, p. 15).
- Method 5 ✗: one run per instance and no baseline optimizer. The verdict comes from high-fidelity physics, not from profiles (pp. 11–19).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: NOMAD with defaults is used twice, for model identification and for the update (pp. 9, 11).
- new-pattern candidate: *surrogate-to-truth reversal report* (existing name, S074): on rotor 67 the criterion improves by up to 98%, yet the true vibration peaks get worse, and the paper reports and explains this (pp. 17–19).
- new-pattern candidate: *validate the blackbox physically before optimizing it* (existing name, S160): the fidelity of the parameterized model is checked on eigenfrequencies before optimization, and its contact responses were also compared (p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Use the same DFO solver twice: first to fit a parameterized model to an existing geometry, then to optimize the fitted model (pp. 9, 11).
- (D5) Optimize a cheap proxy criterion, judge the result with the expensive analysis that the proxy stands for, and report the case where the two disagree (pp. 15, 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "It is well-suited for inequality constrained blackbox optimization involving time-consuming simulations and handles situations in which the model generation returns an error or fails." (p. 11)
- "Thus, presented results indicate that the relevance of a local clearance consumption as performance criterion is highly dependent on the dynamics of the initial blade." (p. 19)

### S163 · Hierarchically constrained multi-fidelity blackbox optimization (Groupe d'études et de recherche en analyse des décisions 2023, arXiv:2312.13128v1)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Alarie, M. Diago (Hydro-Québec), S. Le Digabel, X. Lebeuf (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: this is v1 of the paper published in COAP 2025. SKILL.md cites that paper as Inter-DS under Heuristic 9, and card S112 (c05.md) covers the published version. It continues the interruptible extreme barrier and hierarchical-satisfiability algorithms of ORL 2022 [3] (p. 4), and it builds on Lebeuf's master's thesis [30] (p. 12). Funding: the NSERC Discovery grants of Audet and Le Digabel, and an NSERC/Mitacs Alliance grant with Hydro-Québec (p. 20). **Read**: all 24 pages. The data-profile curves are not in the text.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Hydro-Québec's PRIAD asset-management blackboxes are expensive, and "the violation of some constraints can be predicted with low fidelity evaluations" (p. 3). Parallelism alone "would require thousands of processors" (p. 3).
  - Entry: combine preemption [37] with increasing fidelity [36, 40] (p. 3).
  - Gap: the multi-fidelity literature is mostly unconstrained, uses two fidelities, and uses low fidelity to guide sampling (pp. 2, 5).
  - Problem P: bounds X = [ℓ, u] are unrelaxable, the c_j are quantifiable, f and c take values in ℝ ∪ {∞}, and the fidelity ϕ lies in [0, 1], with evaluation time increasing in ϕ (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: three steps.
  1. A Latin-hypercube sample, centred on x⁰ with a sizing factor ρ, is evaluated at every fidelity. From it, the method estimates, for each fidelity and constraint, the probability that the fidelity is representative (r̂_ij), the probability of violation (p̂_ij), and the mean time (t̂_i) (pp. 8–9).
  2. A biadjacency matrix B assigns each constraint to the lowest fidelity that can be trusted for its feasibility verdict. B minimizes the expected evaluation time subject to a bound ε on misidentification (Problem Q, pp. 10–11).
  3. Any solver then runs with the fidelity controller (Alg. 3.1) as its blackbox (pp. 6–8):
     - sub-evaluations proceed in increasing fidelity and stop at the first violated assigned constraint;
     - a point about to become the best solution is re-evaluated at ϕ = 1.

  The mechanism is "an intermediary component bridging a direct search solver and a blackbox" (p. 1).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The model's hypotheses are spread through the text:
  - a constraint satisfied at its assigned fidelity stays satisfied at higher fidelities (p. 11);
  - violations of different constraints are independent (p. 10);
  - p_ij decreases with i (assumption (18), p. 12), with the note that "The theorem can remain valid without this assumption" (p. 12);
  - evaluation time increases with ϕ (eq. 3, p. 9).

  The abstract claims the approach preserves "the efficiency and convergence properties of the chosen solver" (p. 1). No convergence statement or proof appears in §§3–5. The paper also acknowledges that interruptions risk "misidentifying the feasibility of a point" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The theory covers only the design subproblem Q.
  - The y variables are eliminated (p. 11), and Q is rewritten as Q1 using i(j), the lowest fidelity allowed for constraint j (pp. 11–12).
  - Lemma 1 shows that moving a fidelity's constraints down one level keeps B feasible, and Lemma 2 shows that the expected time does not increase, via a four-term split. Together they give Theorem 3: only the fidelities in I_F are needed (pp. 12–14).
  - Finally, the a priori constraints are filtered out and the remaining assignments are enumerated (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: NOMAD 4 runs in three variants: Inter PB, Inter EB, and a base case at ϕ = 1. The instances are solar2/3/4 without x⁰ and solar2/3/4/7 with a feasible x⁰, 20 runs each, with n_H = 10⁴, ε = 0.05 and 18 listed fidelity levels. Data profiles use blackbox time only, and the LH sampling time is charged to the method. Results:
  - the method succeeds on solar2;
  - without x⁰, it fails completely on solar3 and solar4: "every evaluated point is systematically considered infeasible";
  - with x⁰, Inter EB is best on solar3 and solar4;
  - on solar7 the method assigns all constraints to ϕ = 1 and reproduces the base case (pp. 15–19).

  The authors call this "not an in depth analysis, but rather a proof of concept" (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is the algorithm, the matrix model, and its reduction to an exhaustive search (pp. 5, 14–15). The favourable conditions listed are scarce feasible points, accurate violation assessments at low fidelity, and constraint behaviour that is homogeneous in the LH box (p. 19). The extreme barrier suits rare feasible points and the progressive barrier suits common ones (p. 20). Future work: a dynamic B, the PRIAD application, and machine learning for the behaviour analysis (p. 20).
- **Version note (v1 → published, checked against the S112 text file)**:
  - The published title reads "Fidelity and interruption control for expensive constrained multi-fidelity blackbox optimization" (S112 text, p. 1).
  - v1's claim that the approach preserves "the efficiency and convergence properties" (p. 1) becomes "The convergence guarantees of Algorithm 3.2 are inherited from those of the solver" (S112 text, p. 14). The claim is reworded but still not proved.
  - The fidelity set gains ϕ = 0 (S112 text, p. 15; v1 p. 16).
  - The tolerances shown in the no-x⁰ solar2 profiles change from τ = 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻³ (v1, p. 17) to τ = 0.05 and 0.5 (S112 text, p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: outputs are classified. The bounds are unrelaxable, the constraints quantifiable, and hidden-constraint failures return ∞ (p. 2); "a priori" constraints are those at ϕ = 0 (p. 6). ⚠ Variant: the choice between extreme and progressive barrier depends on the wrapper, not only on the meaning of the constraints. Interrupted points do not return their true outputs, so "the EB might be more adapted" (p. 15). It also depends on how scarce feasible points are (p. 20).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the mechanism sits neither in the Search nor in the Poll but at the blackbox interface (p. 1). Card S112 records the same for the published version.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the guarantee is asserted in the abstract (p. 1) but never analyzed, even though feasibility can be misidentified with probability up to ε (pp. 7, 10). The published version softens the wording without adding a proof (S112 text, p. 14).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: an industrial motivation (PRIAD, p. 3) is tested on SOLAR, described as "the only benchmarking blackbox collection of problems where constraints that are affected by fidelity can be found" (p. 15).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the currency is blackbox time, and the preprocessing time is charged (pp. 16, 18). There is no solver from another family (p. 15).
- Heuristic 9 ✅ evidence: the fidelity structure is exploited around an unchanged direct-search solver (pp. 1, 6).
- new-pattern candidate: *Cheap-first evaluation gating* (existing name): stop an evaluation at the lowest fidelity that already shows a violation (Alg. 3.1, p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate: *Report the degenerate-to-baseline and failure instances* (existing name): the solar7 degeneration and the solar3/4 failures without x⁰ are both reported (pp. 17, 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Put a cost-saving mechanism in a wrapper around the blackbox, so that the solver and its theory are untouched. Re-check any point at full fidelity before it can become the incumbent (pp. 6–8).
- (D2) Replace a two-objective design choice (time against misidentification) with a threshold ε, and minimize the expected time alone (p. 10).
- (D5) Start the curves of the new method at the time its preprocessing took, so that its cost is charged on the same axis (p. 18).

**Quotes**:
- "all while preserving the efficiency and convergence properties of the chosen solver." (p. 1)
- "The results show that when using Algorithm 3.2, every evaluated point is systematically considered infeasible." (p. 17)
