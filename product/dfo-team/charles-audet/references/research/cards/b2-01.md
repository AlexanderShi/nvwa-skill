### S070 · Optimisation globale structurée: propriétés, équivalences et résolution (École Polytechnique de Montréal 1997, no DOI or arXiv id)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: sole-authored thesis. The chapter results are joint work with P. Hansen, B. Jaumard and G. Savard: GERAD reports G-94-52, G-95-20, G-96-06 and G-96-32, JOTA 93(2) 1997, a 1997 Kluwer chapter, and a forthcoming GERAD report on QCQP branch-and-cut (bibliography [14]–[20], pp. 177–178). · **Position in trajectory**: This is the PhD thesis (Philosophiae Doctor, mathématiques de l'ingénieur), Département de mathématiques et de génie industriel, November 1997 (pp. 1–2). The jury page names B. Jaumard as *directrice de recherche* and G. Savard as *codirecteur de recherche*; J. Gauvin chaired, and P. Marcotte and J.-P. Vial (external) were members (p. 3). This settles the unverified formal-advisor item (⚠️) in SKILL.md's Academic Lineage. Funding came from NSERC (CRSNG), FCAR, ISM and Polytechnique (p. 5). The acknowledgements thank Gilles, Brigitte and Pierre as coauthors (p. 4). In SKILL.md's Research Trajectory this is the start of the 1997–2002 exact global-optimization period. Three threads run forward from it. **Chapter map** (file pages):
- Front matter, pp. 1–18. Title, jury (p. 3), acknowledgements (pp. 4–5), French résumé (pp. 6–7), English abstract (pp. 8–9), table of contents and lists (pp. 10–18).
- Introduction, pp. 19–23. Global optimization relies on problem structure, and communities developed class-specific results in parallel (pp. 19–20). The thesis sets two ways to use a reformulation: solve the reformulated problem, or transfer and adapt the tools of one class to the other (p. 20). A chapter-by-chapter roadmap follows (pp. 21–23).
- Ch. 1 *Classes de problèmes d'optimisation globale*, pp. 24–47. The chapter defines nine structured classes: MIP0-1, BILD, BIL, concave QP, QQP, GLCP, LMM, BLP and MIBLP (§1.1, pp. 25–32). It defines a reformulation by polynomial-time transfer of optimal solutions (Def. 1.2, p. 33), then proves the web of reformulations and inclusions (Fig. 1.1, p. 34; Props 1.3–1.14, pp. 35–46), including a counterexample to a tempting penalized reformulation (Ex. 1.12, p. 43). It teaches how to build a taxonomy of problem classes linked by proven reformulations.
- Ch. 2 *Le problème maxmin*, pp. 48–85. The chapter covers the two interpretations of linear maxmin, bilevel versus disjoint bilinear (§2.1, pp. 48–54). It shows that deciding boundedness of BILD is strongly NP-complete (PROHIBITION, via KERNEL; pp. 54–58), with necessary and sufficient tests through auxiliary bilinear problems (pp. 58–62). It covers local optima under the symmetric maxmin reformulations (§2.3, pp. 62–65) and concavity cuts for both reformulations (§2.4, pp. 65–70). The exact, finite algorithm Al(BILD) branches on complementarity (§2.5, pp. 70–78), and the experiments follow (pp. 79–84). It teaches how to design an exact algorithm from the optimality conditions of two symmetric reformulations.
- Ch. 3 *Algorithmes plongés*, pp. 86–109. The chapter defines an embedded algorithm (Def. 3.1, p. 88). It proves that Beale–Small branch-and-bound for MIP0-1 is embedded in Hansen–Jaumard–Savard's BLP algorithm via the MIP0-1 → BLP reformulation (Thm 3.6, pp. 96–97), with a side-by-side example (pp. 98–100) and matching branching rules (pp. 100–102). It also generalizes Tomlin's penalties to BLP (Prop. 3.8, p. 103), gives the Wen–Yang bound comparison (Prop. 3.9, p. 105), and shows that the converse fails for one reformulation (Ex. 3.10, pp. 106–107). The discussion (pp. 107–109) closes the chapter. It teaches how to compare algorithms across problem classes and port tests between them.
- Ch. 4 *Les jeux bimatriciels*, pp. 110–128. The chapter reviews equilibrium enumeration (§4.1, pp. 113–116). It presents algorithm EEE, which specializes Al(BILD) to enumerate all extreme Nash equilibria (pp. 116–122), with a finiteness theorem that needs no nondegeneracy assumption (Thm 4.1, pp. 120–122). It works through Nash's 2×2 example (pp. 122–125), reports experiments up to 29×29 and 700×5 (pp. 125–127) and ends with a discussion (pp. 127–128). It teaches how to specialize a general algorithm until most of its tests become trivial.
- Ch. 5 *Le problème quadratique*, pp. 129–172. The chapter shows why QCQP is doubly hard (p. 129) and reviews the literature (§5.1, pp. 131–133). It covers the linearization framework and iterated bound tightening (§5.2–5.2.1, pp. 133–136) and four classes of valid inequalities (§5.2.2, pp. 136–146). The best cut of each class is chosen by a minimax-error criterion (§5.2.3, pp. 146–152). The branch-and-cut algorithm finds an ε_r-ε_z-optimal solution in finite time (§5.3.1, pp. 152–156). Eight applications follow: pooling, Colville/P&G, Bartholomew-Biggs, heat exchangers, alkylation, 3- and 5-stage membrane separation, and Graham's octagon (§5.3.2, pp. 157–167). Results are on pp. 167–171 and the discussion on pp. 171–172. It teaches outer approximation with cut selection by an error criterion, applied to engineering instances.
- Conclusion, pp. 173–175. Modeling realism must be balanced against solvability. Heuristic answers of unknown precision degrade good models (p. 173). Reformulations serve as a viewpoint (p. 174), and future directions are listed per chapter (pp. 174–175).
- Bibliography, pp. 176–195 (skimmed).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a field-level diagnosis, not an application. Global-optimization methods are built for precise structures, communities studied specific classes in parallel, often at industry's request, and complexity theory says many share the same difficulty (pp. 19–20). The thesis conjectures that classes that look far apart may share the root of their difficulty, so knowledge should be transferred between them (p. 20). It then states two uses of a reformulation. The standard use is to solve the reformulation and map back the solution, which ignores the original structure. The thesis prefers adapting the solution tools of one class to the other (p. 20). Each chapter then enters through a precise gap:
  - Ch. 2: most BILD methods need X and U bounded, all except Thieu's (p. 53). Verifying that a bounded optimum exists is much harder than verifying feasibility, and must precede the extreme-point algorithm (p. 55).
  - Ch. 3: NP-completeness makes all NP-complete classes equivalent, yet in practice some are much harder, and a reformulation can make a solver far slower. The question is therefore whether one algorithm contains another (pp. 86–87).
  - Ch. 4: existing enumeration methods enumerate all vertices and handle only small games, up to 8×8 in McKelvey–McLennan (pp. 114–116).
  - Ch. 5: QCQP generalizes every other class in the thesis and models many industrial situations (pp. 129, 131). Even finding a feasible point is strongly NP-complete, and the optimum can be irrational with integer data, so a fast exact algorithm is not to be expected (p. 129).
  The conclusion adds the motivation that frames the whole thesis: the models are too complex for existing exact methods, so heuristic solutions of unknown precision degrade them (p. 173; Quote 2).
  - Transferable technique: open a thesis with a map of neighboring problem classes and the proven reformulations between them (Fig. 1.1, p. 34). Each later chapter can then enter as a transfer of a tool from class A to class B instead of starting from scratch.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key idea is that a reformulation is a lens, not only a transformation. Study which properties survive it and move optimality conditions, bounds and tests across (pp. 47, 174). Chapter-level designs:
  - *Ch. 2, Al(BILD).* BILD is equivalent to two symmetric linear maxmin problems, LMM_xy and LMM_uv (Prop. 1.13, p. 45). The algorithm uses the optimality information of both: complementary slackness (Prop. 2.16, p. 71) and monotonicity relations (Prop. 2.17, p. 72). Bounds come from the two first-level relaxations (upper, pp. 72–73) and from Gauss–Seidel alternation of LP_x and LP_u (lower, Prop. 2.19, pp. 74–75). The branching design is motivated by tree balance. Each branching creates one child that fixes a first-level variable (or slack) to zero and one that fixes a second-level one, so the two children carry comparable information and the tree is more likely to be balanced (pp. 70, 72). Phase I decides boundedness by applying the same Phase II engine to auxiliary bilinear problems (pp. 75–77). What is not changed: branching on binary indicators of tight constraints, as in Hansen et al.'s BLP algorithm (pp. 71, 89). What is changed: both symmetric reformulations are used at once.
  - *Ch. 2, concavity cuts.* Tuy's γ-extensions are computed for both reformulations, and each reduces to an LP with n+1 variables (Cor. 2.14–2.15, pp. 68–69). When X is bounded, the cuts from the symmetric reformulation LMM_uv are deeper, because D_u is bounded on all of ℝ^{n_u} (p. 70).
  - *Ch. 3.* No new solver. The contribution is a comparison concept and a ported test: Tomlin's stronger MIP penalties are generalized to BLP (Prop. 3.8, p. 103).
  - *Ch. 4, EEE.* Al(BILD) is specialized to bimatrix games. The maxmin reformulations and monotonicity relations become trivial, so the algorithm keeps only branching on one complementarity pair, x_i = 0 versus A_i·y = α or y_j = 0 versus x′B_j = β, choosing the pair with the largest complementarity product such as x_i(α − A_i·y) (step e, pp. 118–119; Fig. 4.1, p. 120). All children of equilibrium nodes are enumerated, with no backtracking bound, because the goal is to list every extreme equilibrium (pp. 118–122).
  - *Ch. 5, Al(QQP).* Variables v_i ≈ x_i² and w_ij ≈ x_i x_j are introduced (p. 133), with four cut classes: tangent under-estimators of x² (I), paraboloid tangents that generalize I with a depth-maximizing γ (II), piecewise over-estimators of x² with binary δ variables (III), and McCormick-type quadrant estimators of x_i x_j (IV) (pp. 137–146). The design choice is to pick the linearization point that minimizes the maximum (weighted) error, not the midpoint (Props 5.14–5.17, pp. 147–152). Other choices: the branching variable has the largest approximation error, and existing δ variables are reused to keep the relaxation small (p. 155). All cuts are valid on the whole domain (pp. 23, 171). Preprocessing iterates bound tightening until no bound improves by more than the tolerance (pp. 135–136, 153).
  - Transferable technique: when a problem has two symmetric equivalent reformulations, branch so that each child fixes one object on each side (a leader variable, a follower constraint). Then measure the tree balance against a single-reformulation competitor (pp. 70, 82).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The assumptions are stated per chapter and tied to what they buy.
  - Ch. 2: no compactness assumption on the feasible set or on the optimal value, which is the chapter's selling point (p. 85). LMM is defined with X not necessarily the projection of P, a deliberately more general setting (pp. 48–49). The concavity-cut section assumes X is a polytope and that PROHIBITION does not hold (p. 68).
  - Ch. 1: the big-L reformulations need L large enough. In practice L is increased until a rational solution with v = 0 appears, and the text flags that a large L can cause numerical difficulty (p. 37).
  - Ch. 3: the embedding holds for the reformulation without a large constant (p. 107). The competitor Al(BLP) needs bounded y and v, so bounding constraints are added (p. 81).
  - Ch. 4: finiteness needs no nondegeneracy assumption (p. 113). The LPs are solved by simplex so that the solutions are vertices; an interior-point method would not give them (p. 119).
  - Ch. 5: minimization form, X without equalities, x ≥ 0 explicit or implied, and finite positive bounds computable on every variable (p. 130). The target is an ε_r-ε_z-optimal solution (Def. 5.18, pp. 152–153), justified by the problem's difficulty (pp. 152, 172).
  - Transferable technique: when an exact solution is out of reach (the feasibility problem is strongly NP-complete, optima can be irrational), define a two-tolerance notion of approximate optimality (feasibility tolerance ε_r, value tolerance ε_z) and prove finite convergence to it (pp. 129, 152–156).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: Devices, in order of appearance:
  - *Reformulation defined by solution transfer.* A reformulation need not be a polynomial transformation, only allow polynomial-time recovery of an optimum (Def. 1.2, p. 33). This admits exponential facet reformulations (p. 33).
  - *Forcing integrality through a lower level.* The follower's problem makes v = min{u, 1−u}, and the leader imposes v = 0 (Props 1.3 and 1.11, pp. 35, 42). This generalizes to n-level problems with binary variables becoming (n+1)-level linear problems (p. 38).
  - *Counterexamples against tempting shortcuts.* A penalized version of GLCP → BLP fails for every finite L, because the first-level relaxation is unbounded (Ex. 1.12, p. 43). A large-L bounding box cannot replace Phase I(c), since it may cut off a local optimum of BILD that is the global optimum of the restricted problem (p. 84).
  - *NP-completeness by reduction.* PROHIBITION (is there x ∈ X with Y(x) = ∅?) is strongly NP-complete via KERNEL (Thm 2.2, pp. 56–58). Checking finiteness of BILD is therefore strongly NP-complete (Cor. 2.3, p. 58).
  - *Normalization of a cone.* Adding u′1 ≤ 1 turns an unboundedness test into a bounded bilinear problem with a strict-positivity criterion. The text says the constraint is introduced only to bound the domain (Prop. 2.4, pp. 58–59).
  - *Necessity via ray decomposition.* The proof that the auxiliary tests are also necessary writes diverging sequences as vertex plus k·ray and splits on whether ‖u‖ diverges (Thm 2.8, pp. 61–62). A 1-D example shows that the vertex-only tests are insufficient (Ex. 2.6, p. 60).
  - *One-directional optimality transfer plus counterexample plus stronger hypothesis.* Every local optimum of a maxmin reformulation gives one of BILD (Prop. 2.9, p. 63). The converse fails (Ex. 2.10, pp. 63–64) but holds under strict local optimality in u (Prop. 2.11, pp. 64–65). The reformulations therefore do not add local optima (p. 65).
  - *γ-extension through an inner minimization* reduces to one optimization problem (Lemma 2.13, p. 67).
  - *Finiteness by counting.* Each node fixes one more variable, and complementarity enumerates the bases implicitly (Thm 2.20, p. 78; Thm 4.1, pp. 120–122).
  - *Embedding proof by induction on the subproblem index,* matching the two algorithms step by step and showing redundant steps are never triggered (Thm 3.6, pp. 96–97). Supporting lemmas show the relaxations coincide (Lemma 3.2, p. 94) and the penalties coincide (Prop. 3.5, pp. 95–96).
  - *Extreme point ⇔ singleton set of tight constraints* (Thm 4.1, pp. 120–121).
  - *Minimax error equalization.* The best linearization point makes the two error terms equal, and a quadratic root is selected by an intermediate-value argument (Props 5.14–5.17, pp. 147–152).
  - *Finite convergence by an interval-length argument.* Each child makes an interval of length ε_r/u_i acceptably linearized, so only finitely many linearizations per variable are possible (Thm 5.19, pp. 155–156).
  - The author states plainly that the proof gives no information on actual running time (p. 156; Quote 1). There are no rates, and complexity appears only as NP-hardness classification.
  - Transferable technique: for every obvious-looking simplification of your reformulation (penalize instead of constrain, box the domain instead of testing rays), write the smallest counterexample showing why it is invalid, and put it right after the correct result (pp. 43, 84).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Setup.* Ch. 2 and 4: C with CPLEX 2.1 on a SPARC SS20/514MP under Solaris (pp. 79, 125). Ch. 5: C++ with CPLEX 4.0 on the same machine (pp. 167–168).
  - *Ch. 2 test sets.* Generators with a known number of local and global optima (Vicente et al.). Time appears to grow exponentially with the number of global optima and to be insensitive to the number of local optima (pp. 79–80; Table 2.1 garbled). Next, Floudas–Pardalos concave QPs solved through QP → BILD. Al(BILD) confirms that solutions from Sherali–Tuncbilek (ε = 5%) and Phillips–Rosen are exactly optimal. Timing differences are attributed to ST's 5% tolerance, the machines differ (SPARC vs IBM 3090), and the optimal node is on average found before one tenth of the nodes (pp. 80–81). Finally, random instances: 10 per setting, mean and standard deviation, density parameter D with the real density shown in parentheses (p. 81).
  - *Fair comparison.* Al(BLP) runs on the same machine, language and libraries. It is even tuned for the test (no penalties, monotonicity relations only at the root) to improve its performance (pp. 81–82). Where both rows of Table 2.3 are readable (column headers are garbled), Al(BLP) uses about 14–80 times more nodes, e.g. 9.4 vs 385.6 and 110.7 vs 8883.6 (p. 82); the text attributes this to the more balanced tree (p. 82).
  - *Implementation ablation.* Evaluating monotonicity relations only at the root slightly increases nodes but cuts time by about 30% on low-density problems (p. 79).
  - *Scale and phase costs.* Problems reach 200 variables for each of x and u and 100 constraints each (pp. 83, 85). When the value is bounded, Phase I dominates the time, explained by degeneracy of the auxiliary problems (pp. 83–84).
  - *Declined comparison.* Thieu's algorithm, the only other one without compactness, was tested only up to sizes 8 and 10, and a detailed comparison is judged not constructive (p. 84).
  - *Ch. 4.* Winkels' degenerate 6×2 example reveals an extreme equilibrium the author had missed: 12 equilibria in about 0.75 s and 417 nodes (p. 125). Random games draw entries uniformly in [0,10], 10 per size, with mean and standard deviation of time, nodes, number of extreme equilibria and the node of the first equilibrium (pp. 125–126). One readable row: m = 5, n = 700 takes 10060.4 s and 263500 nodes, with 47.8 extreme equilibria on average (p. 126). An outlier is reported (n = 21: 2140 s, 272034 nodes; p. 126). A log-time plot shows asymptotically exponential growth, and per-node time is roughly proportional to n·m (pp. 126–127).
  - *Ch. 5, instance handling.* Instances are reformulated (monotonicity relations, variable scaling to a common magnitude, reindexing) and printed in full with their ε-optimal solutions (pp. 157–167). A typo is flagged in Quesada–Grossmann's alkylation formulation (9800 should be 98000, p. 160).
  - *Two-run protocol.* Each of Ex. 5.20–5.26 is run without information, then with an ε-optimal solution supplied so the algorithm only has to prove it. The stated motivation is fast heuristics without precision guarantees (p. 168). Proving optimality is generally faster than the cold run, especially when preprocessing improves the bounds (p. 169). Table 5.2 reports variables at bounds after preprocessing, new δ variables, added cuts, nodes, and time split into preprocessing, tree and total (p. 169). In the extracted table only one time triple per example survived, so which run it belongs to is unreadable.
  - *Octagon campaign* (pp. 169–171). Geometric analysis first gives a non-optimal starting octagon built from the regular heptagon (p. 169). A loose solve at ε = 0.0005 is repaired to feasibility and brackets the area in [0.7261, 0.7290] (p. 170). Four runs, each adding one bound constraint (x1 ≥ 0.3, x1 ≤ 0.25, x4 ≥ 0.94, x4 ≤ 0.87), show that no solution above 0.7262 exists outside the remaining box. A final tight run inside the box gives area 0.7268 after 110 hours, conditional on Graham's conjectured configuration (pp. 170–171).
  - Transferable technique: to certify a hard numeric optimum, bracket it with a loose run, then exclude regions one bound constraint at a time (each run proving that no better point exists in the excluded region), and only then run at full precision in the remaining box (pp. 170–171).
- **D6 Positioning**: The thesis unifies communities rather than joining one: bilevel (Bard, Vicente, Hansen et al.), bilinear (Konno, Al-Khayyal, Sherali), MIP (Beale–Small, Tomlin), game theory (Nash, Mangasarian, Vorob'ev) and QCQP (Al-Khayyal–Falk, Sherali–Tuncbilek) (pp. 25–46, 50–54, 113–116, 131–133). Horst and Tuy's definition is the anchor (p. 24). The reviews are sorted by what each method needs (compactness) and what it guarantees (ε-optimal, finite or not) (pp. 53–54). Criticism is direct and technical:
  - Vaish and Shetty's algorithm is essentially Konno's, and their convergence claim is called *fallacieusement* asserted. The exact logical error is spelled out: Weierstrass gives an accumulation point, not one that belongs to the sequence (pp. 53–54).
  - Ivanilov–Mukhamediev's strict-increase hypothesis is not discussed by its authors (p. 53).
  - Numerical results are rare in the literature, and Konno's largest problem is small (p. 53).
  - Mukhamediev's maxmin information is shown to be redundant for games (p. 115).
  - Wen–Yang's bound is proved never better than the continuous relaxation (Prop. 3.9, p. 105).
  - Winkels' example missed one equilibrium (p. 125).
  Credit is given too: Prop. 1.5 slightly generalizes Vicente et al. (p. 36), and the text says Beale–Small will not be replaced by the BLP algorithm despite the embedding (p. 108).
  - Transferable technique: when you criticize a published convergence claim, quote the faulty step and name the exact theorem that does not deliver it (Weierstrass: accumulation point ≠ member of the sequence), rather than simply asserting that the proof is wrong (pp. 53–54).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Each chapter opens with a literary epigraph (pp. 19, 24, 48, 86, 110, 129, 173) and ends with a *Discussion* section (pp. 47, 85, 107, 127, 171). Each chapter introduction gives a section roadmap and names the reports or papers where its results appear (pp. 25, 49–50, 87, 113, 131). The core contribution sentence sits in the abstract's third paragraph (p. 8) and in the introduction's framing (p. 20). Worked examples carry the hardest concepts:
  - a 2-point rational set (Ex. 1.1, p. 31);
  - a two-column step-by-step run of both algorithms, with step letters deliberately non-consecutive so that matching steps share letters (pp. 92, 98–100);
  - Nash's 2×2 game, including why a weaker branching rule would not prune node 13 (pp. 122–125);
  - an iterated bound tightening converging to [1,2] (Ex. 5.2, pp. 135–136).
  Hedging is calibrated. Performance results use *il semble* (pp. 79, 82–83, 105, 126). A counterexample for one transformation is said to suggest, but not suffice to conclude, that no embedding exists (p. 107). The random-instance caveat appears in Ch. 4's discussion (p. 127). Omitted proofs are declared (pp. 36, 59–60, 68–69, 71–72). Reader's note: the chapters read as their GERAD reports bound together, and the only cross-chapter synthesis is the introduction and the conclusion.
  - Transferable technique: when you prove that two algorithms are step-equivalent, present them with a shared step-label scheme (skip letters in the simpler one) and give one example in two parallel columns (pp. 92, 98–100).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions come in layers. Theory: a reformulation web (Ch. 1), a new NP-complete problem PROHIBITION with necessary and sufficient boundedness tests (Ch. 2), and the embedded-algorithm concept suggesting a difficulty preorder inside NP (pp. 107–109). Algorithms: Al(BILD), EEE and Al(QQP), each with a finiteness theorem (pp. 78, 120, 155). Computation: larger bimatrix games than before and ε-optimal solutions for eight literature applications (pp. 127, 172). The limits are concrete:
  - time grows exponentially with size or with the number of global optima (pp. 79, 82–83, 126–127);
  - Phase I dominates when the value is bounded (pp. 83–84);
  - the Ch. 4 conclusions come from random games only, and applications or other generators might differ (p. 127);
  - the convergence proof says nothing about running time (p. 156);
  - Al(QQP) returns only approximate solutions and does not exploit subclass structure (p. 172);
  - the octagon answer is conditional on Graham's conjecture and has four decimals (pp. 170–171);
  - the Ch. 3 converse is left open (p. 107).
  Future work is specific: add concavity cuts to Al(BILD) whenever the incumbent improves (p. 174); test whether Ibaraki's GLCP algorithm and cutting planes (Gomory) embed across MIP and BLP (pp. 108–109, 174); explore Nash-equilibrium refinements with EEE (pp. 128, 175); add nonlinear cuts, new cut classes and other selection criteria; and specialize to fractional and polynomial problems (p. 175).
  - Transferable technique: in each chapter's discussion, state the population your experiments sample (e.g., random games only) and name which other populations might behave differently (p. 127).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll): variant ⚠. The same proposer/certifier split appears inside exact branch-and-bound. Gauss–Seidel alternation of LP_x and LP_u supplies incumbents, which the text says are often useful at shallow nodes (Prop. 2.19, pp. 74–75), but finiteness rests only on complementarity branching (Thm 2.20, p. 78). The two-run protocol prices what a heuristic incumbent is worth to the certifier (p. 168), and the octagon uses a geometric warm start (p. 169). This matches the proposer–certifier pattern in card c01 (S015), where the same QCQP solver is used.
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first): variant ⚠. In place of constraint types, the thesis classifies problems into structured classes before choosing the algorithm (Ch. 1, pp. 25–46). One decision works like Method 2: whether the leader may choose an x that empties the follower's feasible set. Forbidding it gives the bilevel interpretation, allowing it gives disjoint bilinear programming with unbounded value (pp. 49–52). A modeling decision about infeasible inner problems changes the problem class.
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder, bounded by counterexamples): evidence ✅ for the counterexample-and-audit habit, already in 1997. The evidence includes a counterexample to a tempting reformulation (p. 43), insufficiency of vertex-only tests (Ex. 2.6, p. 60), the failed converse of local optimality with a stronger hypothesis that restores it (pp. 63–65), and a failed converse embedding stated with calibrated wording (pp. 106–107). The literature audits cover Vaish–Shetty (pp. 53–54), Ivanilov–Mukhamediev (p. 53) and Winkels (p. 125), plus necessary-and-sufficient conditions (Thm 2.8, p. 61). Variant ⚠ in form: the guarantees are finiteness, exactness and ε_r-ε_z-optimality, not a smoothness-dependent stationarity ladder. The admission that the proof says nothing about time (p. 156) anticipates the asymptotic, no-rates limitation of Method 3.
- Method 4 (Turn real blackboxes into public benchmark artifacts): variant ⚠ (early form). Engineering instances (pooling, P&G, heat exchangers, alkylation, membranes) are printed in full in the reformulated form used, with ε-optimal solutions, and a typo in a published formulation is corrected (pp. 157–167, 160). There is no online release and no executable.
- Method 5 (Budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking): variant ⚠. The currency is CPU time and nodes, not evaluations (pp. 79–84, 125–127, 169). The fair-comparison moves are there: same machine, language and libraries, plus tuning of the competitor for the test (pp. 81–82). Cross-machine comparisons are flagged, and the competitor's 5% tolerance is named as the likely cause of the timing gap (pp. 80–81). Means and standard deviations over 10 instances are reported (pp. 81, 125), and a comparison that would not be meaningful is declined (p. 84).
- Method 6 (The solver as research instrument): variant ⚠ (weak). Each chapter has its own research code (C with CPLEX 2.1; C++ with CPLEX 4.0; pp. 79, 125, 167). The Ch. 5 code later becomes the instrument for the pooling paper (card c01, S015), but no long-lived product exists yet.
- new-pattern candidate: **reformulation as a lens (transfer tests, not just solutions)**. Evidence: pp. 20, 47, 174; Tomlin's penalties ported to BLP (p. 103); both symmetric reformulations used inside one algorithm (pp. 70, 85).
- new-pattern candidate: **algorithm embedding via reformulation**. Prove that algorithm A generates the same subproblem sequence as algorithm B on the reformulated instance (Def. 3.1, p. 88; Thm 3.6, pp. 96–97), and read a difficulty preorder from it (pp. 108–109).
- new-pattern candidate: **proposer–certifier protocol** (the same name as in card c01). The cold run versus the run given a known optimum measures the cost of the proof alone (p. 168). This makes S070 the second card with it, after S015.
- new-pattern candidate: **audit the literature instances** (the same name as in card c01). Evidence: Winkels' omitted equilibrium (p. 125) and the Quesada–Grossmann typo (p. 160).
- new-pattern candidate: **decide boundedness first**. Isolate the side question (does a bounded optimum exist?), prove its complexity, and answer it with the same engine on normalized auxiliary problems (pp. 54–62, 75–77).
- new-pattern candidate: **minimax-error parameter choice**. Place each cut or linearization point where two error terms are equal, not at the midpoint (Props 5.14–5.17, pp. 147–152). Card c01 records this as one of the four improvements of the 2000 algorithm.
- new-pattern candidate: **extremal-geometry side line: numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later** (the same name as in card s03, S094). The octagon (pp. 166–171) is the earliest instance: numeric only, 4 decimals, conditional on a conjecture.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2/D6] Build a reformulation map between neighboring classes (Def. 1.2 by solution transfer; Fig. 1.1). For each arrow, ask which optimality conditions and tests can be carried along, not only which solutions (pp. 33–34, 174).
- [D4] Prove an equivalence between two algorithms by induction on the subproblem index, comparing them step by step, and show which steps of the richer algorithm become redundant (steps c and i, p. 97). Then port the stronger test of one to the other (Tomlin penalties, p. 103).
- [D4] Turn the question of whether the optimum is unbounded along a cone into a bounded problem by adding a normalization constraint (1′u ≤ 1) and testing strict positivity (Prop. 2.4, pp. 58–59).
- [D4] Before designing the main algorithm, decide whether its precondition (bounded optimum) is itself hard: define the decision problem, reduce a known NP-complete problem to it (KERNEL → PROHIBITION), and reuse the main engine on auxiliary problems to answer it (pp. 56–58, 75–77).
- [D2] Choose cut or linearization points by equalizing the maximum weighted errors on both sides, e.g. a = (√2−1)x̂ + (2−√2)√v̂ for the tangent to x² (Prop. 5.14, pp. 147–148), instead of bisecting.
- [D2/D5] Branch so each child fixes one object from each of two symmetric reformulations, and show the balance effect as a node-count ratio against a same-machine competitor (pp. 70, 82).
- [D5] Give a competitor every advantage you can (same machine, language and libraries; tuned options), and state so in the comparison paragraph (pp. 81–82).
- [D5] Report a small implementation trade-off as numbers (monotonicity relations only at the root: slightly more nodes, about 30% less time, p. 79).
- [D5] For random instances, report the nominal density parameter and the realized density in parentheses (p. 81). Report mean and standard deviation over 10 instances and flag outliers (pp. 81, 125–126).
- [D5] Run the exact method twice, cold and given a known optimum, to separate search cost from proof cost (p. 168).
- [D5] For a hard certificate, bracket the optimum with a loose tolerance, exclude regions one bound constraint at a time, and only then tighten the tolerance in the remaining box (pp. 170–171).
- [D5/D8] Print each application instance in the exact reformulated form you solved, with tolerances and solution vector, and correct any typo you found in earlier formulations (pp. 157–167, 160).
- [D7] Present two step-equivalent algorithms with aligned, deliberately non-consecutive step letters, and run one example in two parallel columns (pp. 92, 98–100).
