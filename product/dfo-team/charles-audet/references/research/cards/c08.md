### S128 · A summary of benchmarking constrained, multi-objective and surrogate-assisted optimization methods (Optimization Letters 2026, 10.1007/s11590-026-02302-z)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: W. Hare, C. Tribes · **Text read**: the extracted file is the GERAD technical report G-2025-36 (May 2025), titled *Benchmarking constrained, multi-objective and surrogate-assisted derivative-free optimization methods* (p. 1–2), i.e. the preprint of the Opt. Lett. paper; the published version was not available. Pages below are the [[page N]] markers (report page 1 = marker p. 4). Read in full, including the appendix (p. 16). · **Position in trajectory**: methodology consolidation that builds on the Audet–Hare textbook, the group's multiobjective-indicator review and the RunnerPost tool (refs [3], [4], p. 16); reused within a year by S156 (its ref [6]) and S157 (its ref [9]).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an implicit assumption of the classical tools: they assume feasible points cost nothing to find, treat one objective only and ignore surrogates (p. 10). The currency is fixed first: in DFO the effort is the number of calls to the simulation, because each call is expensive (p. 4). The problem is stated as a benchmark setup: a test set P of *problem instances* (a problem paired with an initial point or a seed), a finite algorithm set A, and per-evaluation logs (pp. 4–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep the four classical tools (convergence plots, accuracy, performance and data profiles; §3, pp. 6–10) and change only what is fed into them. (i) Constrained: split the convergence plot at η_a, the first feasible evaluation, plotting the constraint violation h before it and the best feasible f after it (pp. 10–11, Fig. 5); set the baseline f0 to the min or the max of the algorithms' first feasible values (p. 11). (ii) Multi-objective: replace f by the hypervolume of the front, normalized in the ideal–nadir box of the union of all fronts, and define s_acc = (s_a − s0)/(s* − s0) (pp. 12–13). (iii) Surrogate-assisted: replace the evaluation count by an effective count N = Nt + w·Ns with w ∈ [0,1) (p. 15); for dynamic surrogates use CPU time instead (p. 14); for Monte Carlo fidelities count repetitions (p. 15). Unchanged: τ-solving, performance ratios and the (np+1) evaluation groups (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Problem (1) with f: X → R ∪ {∞} and quantitative constraints c(x) ≤ 0 (p. 4); an evaluation returns f, c and whether x ∈ Ω (p. 4). Failures caused by hidden constraints are logged as ∞ or NaN (p. 5). All algorithms are assumed to have been run with comparable stopping criteria (p. 5). The three choices of f* (instance-based, cross-instance, literature) are ordered by value (p. 6). As extracted, the definition of h gives the sum of squared violations when x ∈ Ω and ∞ otherwise (p. 10), which would make h zero wherever it is finite; X is presumably meant (my reading). The text also switches between g and c for the constraints (p. 5, p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis**: No theorems; the work is definitional. Devices: the accuracy value in [0,1] (Def. 2.1, p. 5); the equivalence −log10(1 − f_acc) ≥ d ⇔ f_acc ≥ 1 − 10^−d, which turns accuracy into a count of correct decimals (p. 7); the (np+1) scaling justified by the cost of a simplex gradient (footnote, p. 9); a closed-form sum for the two-objective hypervolume (p. 13). Degenerate cases are handled explicitly: if no algorithm improves f0 the instance is removed and flagged as difficult (p. 6); s0 = 0 is allowed when initial points fall outside the box (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: No runs of real solvers. Every figure uses anonymized data built to show attributes that arise in practice, and all plots come from RunnerPost (p. 4, p. 16). What the figures demonstrate: plotting against time instead of evaluations reverses a verdict (Fig. 1, p. 6); a smaller τ reverses a data-profile ranking after k = 50 (p. 10); the switching phenomenon when Algo 1 is removed from a performance-profile comparison (pp. 13–14); the two f0 choices lead to the same conclusion (p. 11); w = 0 vs w = 1/10 at equal budget (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: Recalls Dolan–Moré, Moré–Wild, Beiranvand–Hare–Lucet (accuracy profiles) and Gould–Scott (switching) without criticizing them (pp. 7–9); borrows h from filter methods (p. 10) and the constraint vocabulary from Le Digabel–Wild (p. 10); takes multiobjective indicators from the group's own review (p. 12). The stance is extension, not replacement.
- **D7 Writing**: Every tool follows the same template: definition, an example figure, where the axes start and stop, and a worked reading of the figure (e.g. p. 7, p. 8). Prescriptions are written as *should* / *need to* rules: staircase plots (p. 6), several values of τ (p. 8), very few algorithms (p. 5). The contribution sentence appears in the abstract and again in the introduction (p. 3, p. 4). Very little hedging.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Methodology plus software (RunnerPost: C++ with a Python interface, MIT licence, p. 16). Limits are mostly left implicit: combining the extensions (e.g. multiobjective with surrogates) is asserted to be easy but not shown (p. 16); the hypervolume becomes hard to compute beyond two objectives or for infinite fronts (p. 13); the choice of w is left to the user (p. 15). No concrete future-work list (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 4–16): this is the benchmarking summary (Opt. Lett. 2026) listed under Method 5's practice. It formalizes the evaluation currency, several τ values, and whole-set profiles. The SKILL.md limitation saying this paper was not read in full can now be lifted (read in full, preprint version).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (p. 5, p. 9): step 3 (competitors from another family) is bounded here: DFO algorithms should not be compared with derivative-based ones, and the number of algorithms should be kept very low because performance profiles switch.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (p. 5, pp. 10–11): hidden-constraint failures logged as ∞/NaN; h built only from quantifiable constraints; infeasible starts get their own two-phase plot.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (p. 16): RunnerPost as the group's shared benchmarking instrument, reused by S156 and S157.
- new-pattern candidate: *common evaluation currency with explicit cost weights* (N = Nt + w·Ns, p. 15); also appears in S155 and S156 of this batch.
- new-pattern candidate: *representativeness over size for test sets* (the road-construction example, pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) If your method calls a cheaper surrogate, draw its profiles against N = Nt + w·Ns and show at least two weights (e.g. w = 0 and w = 1/10) at the same budget (p. 15).
- (D2) For runs that start infeasible, draw a two-part convergence plot: h up to the first feasible point, then the best feasible f (pp. 10–11).
- (D2) Define the baseline f0 identically for all algorithms (min or max of their first feasible values), then check that the conclusion survives the other choice (p. 11).
- (D5) Before declaring a winner from performance profiles, remove the best solver and replot. If the others swap places, you are seeing the switching phenomenon, and data profiles are the safer summary (p. 9, pp. 13–14).
- (D4) Handle degenerate instances explicitly: if no solver improves f0, drop the instance and flag it as difficult instead of dividing by zero (p. 6).
- (D5) Minimum log per evaluation: evaluation number, f (∞/NaN on failure), feasibility flag. Store the evaluation number explicitly if only improvements are logged (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "Of course, having a large test set is preferable to a smaller one, but having a test set that is representative of the ultimate goal is crucial." (p. 4)
- "Moreover, comparing DFO algorithms to algorithms that explicitly use derivatives should be avoided." (p. 5)

### S155 · A partitioned optimization framework for structure-aware problems (Journal of Optimization Theory and Applications 2026, 10.1007/s10957-026-03042-x)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: P.-Y. Bouchet (first author), L. Bourdin · **Text read**: arXiv 2407.05046v6 (28 May 2026), dated October 2025 (p. 1). Read in full; figures and tables read through captions and table rows, because plot residue is garbled in the extraction (pp. 14–25). · **Position in trajectory**: comes out of Bouchet's PhD thesis (Polytechnique Montréal 2023, ch. 7, ref [10]) and an optimal-control report (ref [11]) (p. 3). Its algorithmic guarantee rests on the covering-step paper (Opt. Lett. 2025, ref [3]) (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a class of problems in which fixing a few well-chosen combinations of the variables makes the rest easy (abstract, p. 1). The motivation is an application: optimal control with a discontinuous Mayer cost. A parachute must land on a target of concentric rings with different costs, and once the landing point is fixed the problem becomes smooth (p. 3). Composite greybox problems whose blackbox parts have few inputs (p. 3) and counterfactuals in contextual optimization (p. 3) are further targets. The introduction solves a 2D example, φ(y1,y2) = (y2 − σ(y1))² + ε(y1), before any formalism (§1.1, p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Partition Y into sets Y(x) indexed by x in a finite-dimensional X. Define an oracle γ(x) that returns a global minimizer of φ over Y(x) ∩ Ω, and minimize Φ(x) = φ(γ(x)), with Φ = +∞ when the subproblem is infeasible (Framework 1, p. 5). Solve this reformulation with any DFO algorithm to which a covering step is added (Algorithm 1, p. 7). The experiments use the cDSM: a covering step, a skipped Search, and a Poll with random orthogonal positive bases (Algorithm 2 and Remark 5, p. 8). What changes compared with plain DFO is the dimension the solver sees: dim(X) ≤ 10 while dim(Y) ≈ 100 or is infinite (pp. 19–20). The direct-search machinery and the extreme barrier stay (p. 7, p. 21). Making γ tractable and choosing the partition are declared out of scope (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Y is a Banach space, though only completeness is used (p. 26). φ may be discontinuous and Ω need not be closed (p. 2). Assumption 1: the index map χ is continuous, and X splits into pieces, locally finitely many, on each of which γ is uniformly continuous (p. 6). The authors argue this is weaker than the Lipschitz continuity usual in parametric and bilevel optimization (Remark 2, p. 6). Assumption 2 adds boundedness and ample continuity sets (p. 7). Two assumptions are admitted to fail in practice: that γ returns exact global solutions, and that Algorithm 1 runs forever (p. 7, p. 26). They say they found no generic rules for verifying Assumptions 1–2 (p. 26).
- **D4 Analysis**: A new notion, the *generalized local solution*: a point a* in cl(A) whose neighborhood infimum of the barrier function equals the least accumulation value at a* (Def. 1, p. 6), motivated by two 1D examples without minimizers (Remark 1, p. 6). The proof chain (pp. 9–11): Lemma 2 (γ and χ are reciprocal bijections); Lemma 3 (neighborhoods in X pull back to neighborhoods in Y through the continuity of χ); Lemma 4 (ACC(γ;x) is a finite subset of Y(x), using the locally finite partition, uniform continuity and completeness); Lemma 6 (inf ACC(Φ;x) ≥ min over y in ACC(γ;x) of inf ACC(φ_Ω;y)). The central one-line inequality is φ(y) ≥ Φ(χ(y)) for every feasible y (p. 10). Theorem 2 = Proposition 1 (a restatement of [3, Thm 2]) + Proposition 2 (the assumptions carry over to Φ) + Theorem 1 (p. 11). The guarantee ladder is global → local → generalized local (Thm 1 a–c, p. 6). No rates.
- **D5 Experiments**: Two tiers. §5 has four problems with 2–3 variables, each solved from 8 starting points; tables give the first iteration at which x^k is within ±5E−3, ±5E−6 and ±5E−9 of x* (Tables 1–4, pp. 14–19). The authors report runs that converge to another local minimizer near 9.27 (p. 17), and a case where the sufficient lower-semicontinuity condition fails yet the output is still a local solution (p. 14). §6 lifts the problems to dim(Y) ≈ 100 with dim(X) ≤ 10. The cost model is 1 unit per φ(y) and 1 + τ units per Φ(x), and the plots show the best value against budget for several τ (p. 19). Competitors are NOMAD (defaults) and PRIMA (BOBYQA), run on the original problem with the extreme barrier and box constraints from 6 random starting points; the DFPOm starts from their images under χ (p. 21). Honest reporting: NOMAD sometimes stops early on a poor incumbent (p. 22). One §6.3 run fails, which the authors blame on the naive cDSM having no search step for globalization (p. 23). NOMAD solving the reformulation tolerates τ = 30, against 10 for cDSM (p. 25). The gain is credited to the dimension reduction, not to cDSM (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Placed between parametric optimization and bilevel optimization, differing from bilevel by having one level and no min–max conflict (p. 4). Complementary to high-dimensional DFO: active-subspace and random-direction methods probably win when only a few variables matter, and POf wins when the objective depends on a few complex combinations of all variables (p. 4). Also complementary to the composite greybox works [22, 24] (p. 4). It separates its use of the word partitioned from DIRECT-type partitioning methods (p. 4). The competitors are explicitly not discredited, because they were not designed for this setting (p. 20).
- **D7 Writing**: A fourfold contribution paragraph and a roadmap that maps each goal to a section (p. 3). Every assumption is followed by a Remark arguing that it is mild in practice (Remark 2, p. 6; Remark 3, p. 8). Proofs are deferred to §3.3, and each lemma is preceded by one sentence saying what it will be used for (pp. 9–10). Hedging is dense (likely, we believe; pp. 4–6). The discussion includes a self-audit of how tight the analysis is (p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions are the framework theory, the method, an analytical solution of a discontinuous double-integrator problem, and numerics. In the double integrator, the optimal index is x* = −5, while the naive continuous relaxation that drops the ceiling gives −125/24 ≈ −5.20 (p. 13). Limits are stated concretely. The oracle γ is the most demanding practical requirement (p. 26). If the assumptions fail the method becomes a heuristic, though the thesis experiments showed no loss (p. 26). No popular solver implements a covering step (p. 27). The method pays off only when N_ref·τ < N_ini (p. 27), and dim(X) ≤ 50 is desirable for cDSM (p. 27). The indexing of the partition shapes Φ (p. 27). Future work (pp. 27–28): let γ return approximate local solutions; use a progressive barrier whose infeasibility for x is the infimum over Y(x) of the subproblem infeasibility; learn the partition; apply the framework to counterfactuals and parametric optimization.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (p. 6, p. 26): the guarantee is a ladder (global / local / generalized local), and the authors audit what each rung uses: Thm 1.a needs no assumption, 1.b only continuity of χ, and completeness is needed only for 1.c and Thm 2. The evidence is partial: there are no counterexamples showing necessity, only examples where the sufficient condition fails but the conclusion holds (p. 14, p. 26).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 7–8, p. 27): the step that carries the proof is a covering step placed in front of any core algorithm, not a Poll. The Search is skipped in the experiments (Remark 5, p. 8), and the authors add that the covering step is probably optional in practice (p. 27).
- Heuristic 9 ✅ evidence (pp. 13–14): the grey-box structure (explicit f and φ̃, blackbox σ and ε) is exploited while the outer loop remains direct search.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (p. 21, p. 27): extreme barrier on the reformulation, with an explicit plan to move to a progressive barrier after the thesis runs produced many +∞ evaluations.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 19–25): best-value-vs-cost curves on four constructed problems, not profiles. The inner solve is charged (1 + τ), and the claim is narrowed to POf-suited problems (p. 20), as Workflow D's checkpoint asks.
- new-pattern candidate: *partition-and-oracle reformulation* (pp. 5–6).
- new-pattern candidate: *common evaluation currency with explicit cost weights* (1 + τ per outer evaluation, break-even N_ref·τ < N_ini; p. 19, p. 27).
- new-pattern candidate: *framework theorem with a plug-in instance* (Algorithm 1 accepts any iterative algorithm, and Theorem 1 accepts any solution of the reformulation; pp. 6–7). Shared with S157.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) If fixing a few combinations of variables makes the problem easy, optimize over those combinations only: Φ(x) = φ(γ(x)) with γ an inner solver, so the outer DFO solver sees dim(X) variables (pp. 5–6, pp. 13–14).
- (D4) To carry optimality from a reformulation back to the original problem, prove the pointwise bound φ(y) ≥ Φ(χ(y)) and pull neighborhoods back through the continuity of χ. The global and local cases then take three lines each (p. 10).
- (D4) For discontinuous objectives on non-closed sets, state results for generalized local solutions (neighborhood infimum = least accumulation value) instead of assuming lower semicontinuity (p. 6).
- (D5) Charge the inner solve: count each outer evaluation as 1 + τ units, report the largest τ at which the method still wins (p. 19, p. 25), and give a break-even rule of the form N_ref·τ < N_ini (p. 27).
- (D5) Report local convergence as the first iteration at which the iterate enters ±5E−3, ±5E−6 and ±5E−9 of x*, over several starting points (Tables 1–4, pp. 14–19).
- (D5) When one run of a deliberately simple implementation fails, name the missing ingredient (a search step for globalization) and show that a stronger solver on the same reformulation does better (p. 23, p. 25).
- (D7) Solve the running example informally in the introduction, before the formal framework (§1.1, p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "What makes the DFPOm efficient is the sheer dimension reduction provided by the POf." (p. 20)
- "Yet, the proof of Theorem 1 actually shows that our analysis is not tight." (p. 26)

### S156 · Benchmarking Bilevel Derivative-Free Optimization Algorithms (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2605.30531)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: V. Dijon (a student, going by his university e-mail, p. 1), Y. Diouane · **Text read**: arXiv v1 (28 May 2026); read in full, including all figures and CPU-time reports (pp. 1–19). · **Position in trajectory**: carries S128 over to bilevel problems, citing it for the effort aggregation (ref [6], p. 14). It builds on, and partly criticizes, Diouane's earlier inexact direct-search bilevel work (ref [20], p. 14).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a flaw in current practice: bilevel DFO benchmarks rarely check that the reported solutions are admissible, and they do not count the effort of both levels properly (abstract, p. 1). The mechanism: an inefficient lower-level solver can return a suboptimal y, so that (x, y) looks admissible and has an excellent upper-level value. That solver then appears to win (p. 3). The problem is stated in optimistic bilevel form (1) (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two additions on top of single-level profiles. (a) A *refereeing procedure*: after the run, re-optimize the lower level at points claimed to be admissible, and revoke admissibility if the referee finds an εΩ-feasible y_r with f(x, y_r) < f(x, y) − εobj (Def. 3, p. 11). There are three strategies (pp. 11–12). End-Point checks the last point only, and discards the whole history if that point is revoked. Complete checks every point. Reverse walks the history backwards until it reaches a point that is not revoked. Several referees are combined by intersecting their filtered histories (eq. 3, p. 12). (b) A *scaled effort*, N = λ·N_UL + N_LL (or N_UL + λ⁻¹·N_LL), with λ taken from the CPU-time ratio (pp. 13–14), and data-profile groups of (nx+1)(ny+1) budget units (p. 14). Everything else in the benchmarking toolkit stays unchanged (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Inexact versions of lower-level optimality, feasibility, rationality and admissibility, parameterized by ε = (εobj, εΩ) (Defs. 1–2, p. 5), so that inexact lower-level solvers are not penalized unfairly (p. 5). All constraints are assumed scaled so that one tolerance fits them all (p. 5). A referee can only refute a claim, never certify it (p. 11). The referee's settings (starting point: the algorithm's own start, the challenged y_k, or the nominal y0; budget; method parameters) must be the same for every algorithm (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Analysis**: No theorems. The arguments are: a cost analysis of the three strategies, in which End-Point cost grows with |P|, Complete with history length, and Reverse lies between them, equal to Complete in the worst case and End-Point in the best (p. 12); the claim that a deterministic Complete referee and a deterministic Reverse referee find the same final admissible point, which makes Reverse the right tool for accuracy profiles (p. 12, stated without proof); and a justification of the (nx+1)(ny+1) groups by two properties (p. 14). The groups reduce to the classical np+1 when ny = 0, and they equal the minimal cost of one unsuccessful bilevel poll with minimal positive bases.
- **D5 Experiments**: 67 instances from BOLIB, ported to Julia as BOLIB.jl (p. 15, Fig. 1). Budgets are N_UL = 300 and N_LL = 100·ny; εobj = 1e−9 and εΩ = 0; a two-phase extreme barrier handles infeasible starts; the referee starts from the instance's y0 with the same lower-level budget and tolerance as the algorithms; τ ∈ {1e−1, 1e−2, 1e−4} (p. 15). Solvers are anonymized as Algo1–3 except in the internal-referee test (p. 15). Test 1 uses λ = 60: counting only upper-level evaluations makes Algo1 look as good as Algo2, and counting only lower-level evaluations widens the gap (p. 16). Test 2 uses an external referee. Without refereeing the ranking is wrong. End-Point says Algo1 dominates Algo3, while Reverse and Complete find them comparable. Refereeing times are 2.0 min, 1.9 h and 3.6 h (pp. 16–17). Test 3 uses internal referees: the upper level is a MADS poll, and the lower level is CS, MADS without search, or MADS with quadratic search, all from NOMAD. Without a referee the ranking is scrambled. With any referee the expected order (CS < MADS No Search < MADS Quad Search) is recovered. Refereeing times are 1.2 min, 20.9 min and 2.1 h (pp. 17–18).
- **D6 Positioning**: A systematic review of 20 bilevel DFO methods, tabulated by upper-level scheme, lower-level exactness, lower-level solver and type of benchmarking (tables / convergence plots / profiles) (Table 1, p. 10). Criticisms are specific and technical. One method's accuracy value is not defined mathematically and admissibility is never discussed (p. 8). Diouane et al.'s groups count upper-level evaluations only, so they do not represent the total effort; this is the coauthor's own prior work (p. 14). Cesaroni et al.'s nx·ny+1 grouping collapses to 1 when ny = 0 (p. 14). Works that sum both levels assume the two cost the same (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing**: Contributions as four bullets (pp. 3–4). The motivation is told as a short failure story about an inefficient lower-level solver that claims excellent solutions (p. 3). Readers are told to read the figures from left to right (p. 18), and each test ends with an *In summary* paragraph (p. 17, p. 19). Two internal slips. §4.4 finds End-Point the best choice in its example, while the conclusion recommends Reverse (p. 19). The text says the groups count a minimal number of iterations where evaluations are meant (p. 14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A methodology (referee, scaled effort, grouping) plus open-source software, BOLIB.jl (p. 4). Limits are acknowledged. The referee can cost more than the optimization itself (p. 12). End-Point is myopic (p. 11). Starting the referee at the challenged point may revoke almost everything, because DFO runs usually exhaust their budget (p. 13). Refereeing adds a substantial computational cost (p. 19). All test problems are analytic BOLIB problems (pp. 14–15). There is no future-work section (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 13–19): the evaluation currency is made explicit for two levels, data profiles are drawn at several τ with dimension-normalized groups, and the CPU cost of the benchmarking itself is reported.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (p. 15): competitors are anonymized (Algo1–3), so step 3's naming of competitors and their settings is not practiced. This suits a methodology paper and matches S128's anonymized data (S128 p. 4).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (p. 11): the rule of never declaring a result wrong without an explicit counterexample moves from theorems to benchmark logs. Revocation needs a witness y_r, and the referee never certifies.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (p. 4, p. 15): the MATLAB BOLIB library becomes the open-source BOLIB.jl.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (p. 17): NOMAD's CS, MADS and MADS-with-quadratic-search give a controlled ladder of lower-level solvers.
- new-pattern candidate: *refute-don't-certify referee* (pp. 11–12).
- new-pattern candidate: *validate a benchmarking method by recovering a known algorithm hierarchy* (pp. 17–19).
- new-pattern candidate: *common evaluation currency with explicit cost weights* (pp. 13–14; shared with S128 and S155).
- new-pattern candidate: *degenerate-case consistency of new metrics* ((nx+1)(ny+1) reduces to np+1 when ny = 0; p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Before profiling any method that reports solutions of an inner problem (bilevel, robust, equilibrium), re-solve the inner problem at the reported points with an independent referee, and discard points the referee beats by more than εobj (p. 11).
- (D2) Use the Reverse referee, walking the history backwards until the first non-revoked point, to reach the Complete referee's conclusions at about half its cost (p. 12, p. 17).
- (D5) Validate a new benchmarking protocol on a nested family of algorithms whose ranking is known in advance (CS < MADS without search < MADS with quadratic search). The protocol passes if it recovers that ranking (pp. 17–19).
- (D4) When you generalize a normalization such as np+1, pick one that reduces to the classical unit in the degenerate case (ny = 0) and has an algorithmic meaning (the cost of one failed poll) (p. 14).
- (D5) Plot the same runs under three effort currencies (upper level only, scaled, lower level only) side by side, to show how the currency changes the verdict (Fig. 1, pp. 15–16).
- (D6) Build the literature-review table so that its columns are exactly the gaps your paper fills (lower-level exactness; tables, convergence plots or profiles) (Table 1, p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "A referee does not certify the admissibility of a point (x, y)." (p. 11)
- "The Reverse Referee is our recommended approach, as it leads to conclusions similar to those of the Complete Referee while requiring a least computational cost." (p. 19)

### S157 · Surrogate-based categorical neighborhoods for mixed-variable blackbox optimization (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2603.27839)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: E. Hallé-Hannan (first and corresponding author, p. 1), Y. Diouane, S. Le Digabel, C. Tribes · **Text read**: arXiv v1 (29 Mar 2026); read in full, including the figures (pp. 1–26). · **Position in trajectory**: the latest step in the Hallé-Hannan line: mixed-variable framework (Oper. Res. Forum 2023, ref [7]), distance for meta variables (Neurocomputing 2025, ref [16]), Cat-Suite (ref [17]) and CatMADS (ref [6]). Here the CatMADS neighborhoods are made surrogate-based and constraint-aware.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap in neighborhood-based methods. MV-MADS needs user-defined neighborhoods, and its theory assumes they lie in the feasible set (p. 4). CatMADS builds neighborhoods from the objective only, which fails when the constraints behave very differently across categories (p. 4). BO lacks local mechanisms, convergence guarantees and principled stopping criteria, and Gower-type metaheuristic distances are not informed by the problem (p. 4). Figure 1 (three colored planes, one per category) shows an objective-based neighborhood choosing a highly infeasible category (pp. 4–5). The contribution is scoped narrowly as extra structural information, not a new optimizer (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Fit mixed-variable GPs (one-hot or matrix-based categorical kernels, tuned by marginal likelihood; pp. 8–11) to the objective and to each constraint. Derive two quantities: (i) a kernel-induced categorical distance d_f(u,v) = κ(u,u) + κ(v,v) − 2κ(u,v) = ‖ϕ(u) − ϕ(v)‖² (eq. 11, p. 12); (ii) a constraint pseudo-distance d_g = ‖ĝ⁺(u) − ĝ⁺(v)‖_p, where ĝ⁺ sets to zero every constraint predicted feasible after a relaxation by λσ̂ and normalizes the others (eqs. 12–13, p. 13). Candidate categories are ordered by a primary and a secondary ranking that swap according to the incumbent's feasibility: the primary is d_g if the incumbent is feasible and d_f otherwise (eq. 14, p. 14). The ordering puts the Pareto-nondominated components first, then the ties at zero primary distance sorted by the secondary ranking, then the rest by the primary ranking (Def. 1, p. 15; Fig. 4). The neighborhood is the m lowest-ordered components (Def. 2, p. 16). Def. 3 extends it to mixed domains by holding the quantitative part fixed (pp. 18–19). The neighborhoods are plugged into CatMADS's categorical poll, and a BO search step reuses the GPs (p. 22). The CatMADS framework, the progressive barrier and the mesh are unchanged (pp. 19–21).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The constraints that define Ω are relaxable and quantifiable. Unrelaxable constraints define the domain (f = +∞ outside it), and hidden constraints also give f = +∞ (p. 3). The categorical set is finite, dimensions and bounds are fixed, and meta variables are excluded (p. 3). The GP formulas are the noiseless ones (p. 10). The theory is inherited, because the method stays an instance of the CatMADS framework (p. 21). The paper does not say whether neighborhoods are recomputed when the GPs are updated during the BO search (p. 22); the discussion says only that the number of neighbors is fixed (p. 26).
- **D4 Analysis**: No new theorem. The convergence argument is membership in an existing framework (p. 21). Small proofs: d_f is a metric through the Moore–Aronszajn feature map (pp. 11–12). The prediction-based |f̂(u) − f̂(v)| and d_g are pseudo-distances: symmetry and the triangle inequality hold, and the identity of indiscernibles fails by design (pp. 12–14). The ordering relation is shown to be only a partial order, because of ties (p. 15).
- **D5 Experiments**: (a) A case study on the purpose-built mechanical-part problem (3 categorical and 2 continuous variables, 24 categorical components; p. 7). LHS with up to 100 000 points per component suggests only about six components can be feasible (pp. 7–8). Starting from a single feasible point, each strategy proposes six neighbors: the Gower and objective-based neighborhoods find no feasible one, and the surrogate-based neighborhood finds four, including the component of the best LHS point (pp. 16–17, Table 1). One inconsistency: §4.4 samples three points for each of 32 components (96 points, p. 16), while §2 counts 24 components (p. 7). (b) Optimization with a 200-evaluation budget, where every solver gets the same 40-point DoE containing no feasible point. The convergence plots show h and then the best feasible f, with feasibility declared at h ≤ 1e−8. Repeated over several seeds, Pymoo usually reaches feasibility first but its objective value stays high in about 75% of runs, and Optuna rarely finds a feasible point (pp. 22–23). (c) Cat-Suite: 30 unconstrained and 30 constrained problems, each with 3 seeds, 180 instances in all. The budget is 250n with a 20% DoE, f0 follows S128's rules (ref [9]), τ ∈ {1e−2, 1e−3, 1e−5}, and data profiles use (np+1) groups (pp. 23–24). CatMADSGP is best. At τ = 1e−2 it solves about 65% of the unconstrained problems, against 60% for CatMADS, 45% for Pymoo and 25% for Optuna (p. 25). Competitors are Pymoo's mixed-variable GA, Optuna's CMA-ES extension to categorical variables, and CatMADS (p. 22). No BO solver is included, although BO is the main alternative discussed (p. 4). CatMADSGP differs from CatMADS in two components, the neighborhoods and a BO search step (p. 22), and no ablation isolates their effects. The text calls Fig. 10a performance profiles, but its axis is groups of np+1 evaluations, i.e. data profiles (p. 25).
- **D6 Positioning**: Defined against CatMADS (objective-only neighborhoods built from a basic IDW interpolation) and MV-MADS (user-defined neighborhoods) (p. 6). Metaheuristic operators such as CatCMA and mixed-variable NSGA-II are treated as playing the role of neighborhoods (p. 6). Categorical kernels are borrowed from BO: one-hot, matrix-based and latent-space encodings (p. 6, pp. 9–10). Prior methods are criticized by restating their focus (on the optimizer rather than neighborhood design), not by attacking them (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing**: The research gap is stated in one sentence (p. 5), followed by four numbered objectives that double as the contributions (p. 5). A toy engineering problem (supplier, material, shape) runs through §2, §3, §4.4 and §6.1 (pp. 7–23). A three-color Hamming-distance mini-example shows why a problem-informed distance is finer (p. 12). Hedging is moderate: the improvement is said to be expected rather than proven (p. 21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: An algorithmic component, a proof-of-concept solver (CatMADSGP) and public scripts and data on GitHub (bbopt/surrogate_based_neighborhoods, p. 26). Acknowledged costs: GP construction (likelihood maximization) is expensive (p. 12), and the BO search and GP updates stop after 500 evaluations or 33% of the budget (p. 22). Future work: adapt the number of neighbors during the run, and define neighborhoods with meta variables (p. 26). The missing BO baseline and the missing ablation are not mentioned (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (p. 21, p. 22): the data-driven idea lives in the *Poll's* categorical neighborhood, not only in the Search. The proof survives because the CatMADS framework accepts any neighborhood. The BO search step that reuses the GPs is the classic Method 1 move (p. 22).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (p. 3, pp. 13–15, p. 20): outputs are classified (relaxable-quantifiable, unrelaxable, hidden); the neighborhood ranking switches with the incumbent's feasibility; the progressive barrier keeps a feasible and an infeasible incumbent.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To compare categories, turn a fitted kernel into a distance, d(u,v) = κ(u,u) + κ(v,v) − 2κ(u,v). If you have only predictions, fall back to |f̂(u) − f̂(v)| (pp. 12–13).
- (D2) Build the constraint similarity so that it is zero whenever both candidates are predicted feasible (after an uncertainty relaxation λσ̂). Feasibility margins then cannot distort the ranking (p. 13).
- (D2) Merge two similarity criteria by Pareto dominance first and lexicographic tie-breaking second, and let the primary criterion switch with the incumbent's feasibility (pp. 14–15).
- (D5) Before running any optimizer, show how hard the test problem is: give per-category LHS feasibility rates at increasing sample sizes (Fig. 2, pp. 7–8).
- (D5) Give every solver the same initial DoE, even one without a feasible point, and plot h until feasibility and the best feasible f afterwards (pp. 22–23).
- (D8) When a new component is plugged into an existing convergent framework, say explicitly that the theory is unchanged and why (p. 21).
