# Paper cards · batch c03 (core)

Batch intent: See how Audet works in five different genres from 2012 to 2024, each a different way of extending or defending the MADS program: a field survey (how he maps direct search and states its open needs), a stochastic extension (how he imports a proof architecture from the probabilistic trust-region school and rehosts it on the mesh/frame), a solver redesign paper (the solver as research instrument), a short letter born of an industrial pipeline (structure exploitation with the direct-search backbone kept), and a counterexample note against a peer's theorem (auditing published guarantees). The main questions: does Method 1 (free Search, rigid Poll) hold as a design rule across genres; how does Method 3 (guarantee ladder bounded by counterexamples) look when the guarantee becomes probabilistic; and do Methods 5–6 (benchmarking, solver as instrument) match what these papers actually do?
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all; core batch). Extra weight on D2 (what is changed and what is kept), D4 (proof architecture and borrowed devices) and D6 (how peers and his own earlier work are treated).

### S023 · A survey on direct search methods for blackbox optimization and their applications (Mathematics without boundaries: Surveys in interdisciplinary research 2014, 10.1007/978-1-4939-1124-0_2)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: Written in October 2012 as GERAD Cahier G-2012-53 (p. 1), with support from NSERC grant 239436 and AFOSR FA9550-12-1-0198 (p. 5). It sits between the 2003–2009 theory-building period and the 2010–2016 applications phase of SKILL.md's Research Trajectory. It condenses the GPS → MADS → progressive-barrier line and catalogues applications. It is the survey that later papers in this batch cite as the reference for direct search (S053 ref. [5], p. 29; S021 ref. [7], p. 18). **Read**: all of §§1–7 (file pp. 5–19); the reference list (pp. 19–24) was skimmed to count self-citations. File pages 2, 4 and 6 are blank, and file page 7 is report page 1. The Springer chapter version (pp. 31–56 per S021 ref. [7]) was not read. Figure 1 (p. 10) is garbled in extraction, so only its caption and the explanatory text were read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is defined by its pathologies, each tied to a cited case and a number: one simulation can take up to 2000 computational hours [116, 112], 60% of simulation calls return an error in [37], and outputs vary for the same input in [29] (p. 7). The abstract says the blackbox's internal structure cannot be exploited because it is unknown, hidden, unreliable or nonexistent, while bounds, linear constraints or a supplied surrogate may sometimes still be used (p. 5). Scope is fixed by contrast with model-based DFO: model-based methods do not know the derivatives but assume they exist, and they target blackboxes that are not as ill-conditioned. The chapter covers directional methods that make no assumptions on f or Ω (p. 7). Problem form: min_{x∈Ω} f(x) (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: open a survey or an introduction with three blackbox pathologies (cost, failure, nondeterminism), each backed by one cited real case and one number.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design** (for a survey: the organizing idea): The field is told as a genealogy in which each generation removes a named limitation of the previous one, and each limitation comes with a failing example.
  - Coordinate search (2n poll points, step halved on failure, p. 8) → GPS, with three generalizations: poll directions from positive bases, a step size allowed to grow on success, and a finite Search on the mesh (pp. 8–9).
  - GPS → MADS, because of two GPS limitations: the direction set is fixed and finite (coordinate search converges to a nonstationary point on a modified Dennis–Woods function, the max of two strictly convex quadratics), and general constraints are not handled (p. 9). MADS splits the step into mesh and poll size parameters so that normalized poll directions become dense (pp. 9–10). A counting argument makes this concrete: with poll-to-mesh ratio λ, the 2D poll set can be chosen from (2λ+1)² − 1 mesh points (p. 10).
  - Sufficient-decrease methods are presented as the mesh-free alternative, where a minimal decrease plays the role of the mesh (p. 11).
  - The Search step is explained as a practical device. Users tend to alter the code to exploit their knowledge of the problem, and changes to the poll set could destroy the theory, so a separate Search on the mesh is added (p. 9).
  - Structure the blackbox may still expose: integer variables via an integer mesh, stopping when the mesh size drops below one; categorical variables via user-defined neighbourhoods (p. 11); bounds and linear constraints via tangent cones (p. 12); general constraints via extreme barrier, filter, progressive barrier, or a hybrid that moves a constraint from PB to EB once an incumbent satisfies it (p. 12); minimax (p. 12); multiobjective problems and trade-off studies by cache inspection or biobjective runs (p. 13).
  - Tools for costly blackboxes: static surrogates (for starting points, for subproblems such as extended polls or a VNS descent, and for ordering trial points under opportunistic termination), dynamic quadratic/kriging/RBF models, and parallelism (pp. 13–14). A surrogate need not be accurate. In [29] the surrogate value is in seconds and the true value in hours, a poor approximation that is still very useful because the two share similarities (pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable technique: in a survey, present each method as a pair: the limitation of its predecessor, shown by a concrete failure, and the modification that removes it.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The only user requirement is a starting point where the simulation runs, i.e. f(x0) < ∞. The starting point need not be feasible (p. 10). Termination is by evaluation budget, wall-clock time or a mesh-size threshold (p. 10). The constraint taxonomy is stated with reasons (p. 12): unrelaxable constraints, which must hold when the simulation cannot run or cannot be trusted if they are violated (e.g. logarithms or square roots of negative values); hidden constraints (the simulation fails inexplicably); and relaxable constraints, whose amount of violation is available. The theory assumptions are graded: none (zeroth order), local Lipschitz continuity, regularity, strict differentiability (p. 15), and the hypertangent cone for constrained problems (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: state the minimal input as a point where the simulation succeeds rather than a feasible point, and keep feasibility as a separate issue.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: There are no proofs, but §5 lays out the ladder in words.
  - No surveyed method guarantees a global minimizer. The chapter cites the paper titled *global optimization requires global information* and adds that a blackbox provides no information at all (p. 14).
  - Coordinate search and GPS can stall at a saddle (f = x1x2 from the origin), but unlike Newton's method they cannot converge to a strict local maximizer, and this extends to MADS (p. 14).
  - If ∇f is Lipschitz, the gradient at an unsuccessful iterate is bounded by a constant times the mesh size, which justifies stopping on a small mesh size (p. 15).
  - The Audet–Dennis GPS analysis defines refined subsequences and refined points. The zeroth-order result needs no assumption. Next come nonnegative Clarke derivatives along refining directions if f is Lipschitz near the limit, then regularity and strict differentiability (p. 15). MADS strengthens this to all directions, i.e. 0 ∈ ∂f (p. 15). Under EB, f°(x̂; d) ≥ 0 on the hypertangent cone. Rockafellar's upper subderivative [141] extends the ladder to non-Lipschitz f (p. 15). Under PB, infeasible refined points satisfy h°(x̂; d) ≥ 0 on the hypertangent cone of X, i.e. a local minimum of the constraint violation, which happens in particular when no feasible point exists (p. 16).
  - The rationality requirement on the step-size factor was later shown to be necessary for the analysis, citing Audet's 2004 tightness paper [14] (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: state what the method guarantees at infeasible limit points (stationarity of the violation h), not only at feasible ones.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: None (survey). The only experimental content is the benchmarking stance in the Discussion (p. 19; see D8 and Quote 2).
- **D6 Positioning**: The survey is organized around Audet's lineage: 30 of its 143 references have Audet as an author (pp. 19–24). Predecessors are credited as origin points: Fermi and Metropolis's coordinate search in 1952, Nelder–Mead and Hooke–Jeeves (p. 7), Torczon's GPS framework and her liminf result, called the first cornerstone of the analysis (pp. 8–9), and Lewis–Torczon positive bases (p. 8). Earlier methods are criticized only through others' counterexamples: McKinnon's Nelder–Mead failure on a strictly convex 2D problem (p. 8) and Kolda–Lewis–Torczon's coordinate-search failure (p. 9). Model-based DFO is placed in its own niche by the assumption it needs, not criticized (p. 7). Sufficient-decrease, frame and GSS methods get a neutral paragraph (p. 11). Vicente & Custódio (2012) [141] is cited approvingly for enriching the hierarchy (p. 15), the same paper whose Theorem 4.1 is refuted in S138. Applications fall into six non-exhaustive groups, and the reader is pointed to the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente book for more (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: place rival method families by the assumption each requires (derivatives exist vs. nothing assumed), not by performance claims.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The voice is pedagogical and plain. Theorems appear as verbal statements with a single displayed formula, the Clarke derivative (p. 15). The shape of the theory, a hierarchy, is announced in the roadmap paragraph before §5 (p. 7; Quote 1). One deliberate surprise sentence: a method using first and second derivatives (Newton) gives weaker guarantees than one using only function values (p. 14). Applications are short case summaries with quantitative outcomes, e.g. 70% and 89% trailing-edge noise reduction (p. 16). Hedging is sparse: the closing wish that more real test problems will be shared starts with *Hopefully* (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: announce the structure of your theory (e.g., stronger hypotheses → stronger guarantees) in the roadmap, so the theory section reads as a ladder, not a list.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a taxonomy, a summary of the convergence hierarchy, and an applications catalogue (abstract, p. 5; §7, p. 19). The stated limits concern the field, not the chapter: most real blackboxes are proprietary or internal-only, so there are few real problems to share for benchmarking, and testing on smooth CUTEr or Hock–Schittkowski problems, or perturbed versions of them, is not ideal (p. 19; Quote 2). Global optimization is declared out of reach (p. 14). Reader's observation from a full read of §§2–6: noisy or nondeterministic outputs are named as a pathology (p. 7) but get no algorithmic section. The only related item is variable-precision evaluation [125] (p. 13). That gap was later filled by Robust-MADS (2018) and StoMADS (S053).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll): evidence ✅ (stated). The Search step exists so users can add problem knowledge without breaking the Poll structure that carries the theory (p. 9). Surrogates, VNS and model ordering all enter through Search/Poll ordering (pp. 13–14).
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first): evidence ✅ (stated). Unrelaxable, hidden and relaxable constraints each get a reason and a mechanism (EB, failure, filter/PB/hybrid), and x0 need not be feasible (pp. 10, 12).
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder, bounded by counterexamples): evidence ✅ (stated). The hierarchy is announced (p. 7) and laid out (pp. 14–16), and a hypothesis is shown necessary via [14] (p. 9). Variant ⚠: this chapter builds no counterexamples and relies on others' (pp. 8–9).
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → public benchmark artifacts): evidence ✅ (stated, strongest source so far). The 2012 Discussion names the missing public real blackboxes and the inadequacy of CUTEr/HS for benchmarking (p. 19). This is the need that STYRENE, SOLAR and the bbopt repos later answer.
- Method 5 (Budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking): evidence ✅ for the premise that realistic blackboxes must be in the test set (p. 19). No experiments.
- Method 6 (Solver as research instrument): evidence ✅. NOMAD is described as integrating the features surveyed in the paper (p. 16).
- new-pattern candidate: **surrogates judged by shared trends, not accuracy**. A surrogate in different units (seconds vs hours) is still useful for ordering and screening (pp. 13–14).
- new-pattern candidate: **genealogy exposition**. Each method generation is introduced by a named, example-backed limitation of its predecessor (pp. 8–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Pathology paragraph: one real case and one number each for cost, failure rate and nondeterminism (p. 7).
- [D2] Explain why decoupling mesh size from poll size enriches the directions by counting candidate poll points: (2λ+1)² − 1 in 2D (p. 10).
- [D3] Require only f(x0) < ∞, not feasibility, as the user input (p. 10).
- [D4] Cite the smooth-case bound (gradient ≤ constant × mesh size at unsuccessful iterates) as the theoretical justification of a mesh-size stopping rule (p. 15).
- [D4] Characterize infeasible limit points as stationary for the violation function h (p. 16).
- [D2] Use a cheap surrogate for ordering even when its values are on a different scale from the truth (pp. 13–14).

**Quotes**:
- "The convergence analysis is hierarchical in the sense that the stronger the hypotheses on the objective and feasible region, the stronger the resulting theoretical guarantees." (p. 7)
- "Comparing direct search methods for blackbox optimization on smooth problems from the CUTEr [77] or from the Hock and Schittkowski [86] collection (or on some perturbed variants) is not ideal, as these problems do not possess the same kind of difficulties than blackbox problems without derivatives from real applications." (p. 19)

### S053 · Stochastic mesh adaptive direct search for blackbox optimization using probabilistic estimates (Computational Optimization and Applications 2021, 10.1007/s10589-020-00249-0; arXiv 1911.01012)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K.J. Dzahini, M. Kokkolaras, S. Le Digabel · **Position in trajectory**: The version read is arXiv v1 of 4 Nov 2019, titled *StoMADS: Stochastic blackbox optimization using probabilistic estimates* (p. 1). The published COAP version was not read and may differ, especially in its experiments. Funding is the NSERC CRD RDCPJ 490744-15 and InnovÉÉ grant with Hydro-Québec and Rio Tinto (p. 28), the same grant that funds NOMAD 4 (S021, p. 18). The paper belongs to the noise line of SKILL.md's 2017–2022 consolidation period and follows Robust-MADS (2018) and Bouchet's 2019 MSc (both discussed on p. 3). Dzahini is listed as a downstream student in SKILL.md's Academic Lineage. **Read**: all 31 pages. Figures 4–9 (profiles, pp. 25–27) are images, so only their captions and the text's reading of them (pp. 23–24) were read. In the proof of Theorem 4 (p. 18), some ε symbols are lost in extraction.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min_x f(x) = E_Θ[f_Θ(x)], where f_Θ is the noisy blackbox and the noise distribution is unknown. The analysis assumes f is locally Lipschitz and bounded below (p. 2). The motivation is statistical machine learning (p. 2). The entry is a double gap.
  - Inside the MADS family: Robust-MADS (kernel smoothing) has only zero-order results and no tests with random noise, and it is a deterministic algorithm, so its convergence is not stochastic (p. 3). Bouchet's MSc variant assumes Gaussian noise and needs many evaluations to reach good solutions (p. 3).
  - In the stochastic-DFO family that has probabilistic-estimate theory (Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg [23], Paquette–Scheinberg [38], Wang–Yuan [40]), descent directions come from models or gradients (p. 3).
  - Claimed novelty: no model or gradient information is needed (p. 3; Quote 1), and it is, to the authors' knowledge, the first stochastic MADS variant with full convergence support via martingale theory (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable technique: define the gap as the intersection of two literatures: my family lacks property X, and the family that has X relies on Y, which my setting forbids.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *What is kept*: the MADS skeleton, i.e. Search and Poll, the mesh M^k and frame F^k, δ_m = min{δ_p, δ_p²}, and OrthoMADS-type Householder poll directions (pp. 4, 7, 17).
  - *What changes*: the acceptance test compares estimates, not values. Prop. 1 shows that if f_s − f_0 ≤ −γ ε_f δ_p² with ε_f-accurate estimates and γ > 2, then f really decreased, and symmetrically for an increase (p. 5). This gives three iteration types: success, certain failure (f_s − f_0 ≥ γ ε_f δ_p²), and uncertain failure (the difference falls inside the uncertainty interval) (Def. 2, p. 5).
  - The frame size parameter is updated differently for each type: after success δ_p ← min{τ⁻² δ_p, τ^{−ẑ}}; after certain failure δ_p ← τ² δ_p; after uncertain failure δ_p ← τ δ_p, deliberately a less aggressive shrink (pp. 5–7, Algorithm 1). δ_p is capped by τ^{−ẑ} so the random frame size is integrable (p. 5).
  - Remark 1: the frame size plays two roles. It sets the mesh resolution and, through the accuracy requirement ε_f δ_p², it controls the variance, so no separate control parameter is needed, unlike the line search of [38] (p. 9).
  - Estimates are sample means with p_k ≥ V / (ε_f² δ_p⁴ (1 − √β)) samples under unbiased, bounded-variance noise (p. 10). To save evaluations, samples accumulate at the current incumbent across iterations, using update formulas (3) after success and (4) after failure (pp. 10–11).
  - Transferable technique: when porting a deterministic method to noisy evaluations, replace the binary success test with a three-way test (success / certain failure / uncertain failure) and give the uncertain case a gentler step-size reduction.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Estimates are β-probabilistically ε_f-accurate conditioned on the past (Def. 3, p. 8). They satisfy a κ_F-variance condition E[|F − f|² | past] ≤ κ_F² Δ_p⁴ (Assumption 2, p. 9). f is bounded below (Assumption 3, p. 12). f is locally Lipschitz and all iterates lie in a compact set (Assumption 1, p. 9). The authors say the compactness assumption was taken to make the analysis simpler, because stochastic iterates can leave the initial level set (p. 16). The Clarke result additionally fixes D to the 2n coordinate directions, δ_p⁰ = 1 and τ = 1/2, and generates poll directions with the Householder construction of Algorithm 2 (p. 17). Constants: γ > 2, ν from inequality (6), and β ∈ (1/2, 1) from inequality (7) (p. 12). The test noise is biased by the constant Σ E[Θ_i²], which the authors note leaves the minimizer unchanged (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: if your test noise breaks an unbiasedness assumption, check whether the bias is constant (minimizer unchanged) and say so explicitly.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The analysis is a probabilistic version of the MADS ladder, built with devices borrowed from [23, 34, 38] (p. 12).
  1. Lemma 1: conditional Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the expected estimate error on bad-estimate events by (1 − β)^{1/2} κ_F Δ_p² (p. 10). The proof is described as slightly modified from [38] to stress integrability (p. 10).
  2. Lemma 2: a successful iteration with accurate estimates decreases f by at least (γ − 2) ε_f δ_p² (pp. 11–12).
  3. Theorem 1: potential Φ_k = ν(f(X^k) − f_min) + (1 − ν)(Δ_p^k)². The proof shows E[Φ_{k+1} − Φ_k | past] ≤ −η (Δ_p^k)² with a 2 × 3 case split: good/bad estimates × success/certain failure/uncertain failure. ν is chosen so that the f-decrease dominates the Δ-growth on success (6); the uncertain failure gives the smallest decrease (17). β is chosen so that the good-case decrease beats the bad-case increase bounded via Lemma 1 (7). Conclusion: Σ Δ_m^k < ∞ almost surely (pp. 12–15).
  4. Corollary 1 gives Δ_m → 0 a.s. for the whole sequence. Remark 2 notes this is stronger than MADS's liminf result and attributes it to the sufficient-decrease test, as in the directional direct-search analysis of Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente (pp. 15–16).
  5. Theorem 2: under compactness, an a.s. convergent refining subsequence exists. Its iterates are not necessarily mesh local optimizers (p. 16; p. 28).
  6. Theorem 4: liminf Ψ_k ≤ 0 a.s. with Ψ_k = (f(x^k) − f(x^k + δ_m d))/δ_p, by contradiction. A log-scale step variable R_k = −½ log_τ(δ_p/λ) is shown to be a submartingale and compared with the ±1 random walk W_k = Σ(2·1_{J_i} − 1), whose limsup is +∞ a.s. by a martingale convergence theorem (Theorem 3) (pp. 17–19).
  7. Theorem 5: at refined points, f° ≥ 0 along refining directions a.s. (p. 20). The key is that δ_p‖d‖∞ ≥ 1 while δ_m‖d‖∞ → 0 (p. 17). The proof differs from MADS because uncertain failures do not certify f(x + s) ≥ f(x) (p. 20).
  - No rate or complexity bound is given. Reader's note: I did not verify the step r_k − r_{k0} = k − k0 ≥ w_k − w_{k0} (p. 19). ε symbols are missing from the extracted text of that proof.
  - Transferable technique: build the potential ν·(optimality gap) + (1 − ν)·(step size)², choose ν so every good-estimate outcome decreases it, then choose the accuracy probability β so the good-case decrease beats the bad-case expected increase, which is bounded by Cauchy–Schwarz and the variance condition.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Problems: 66 unconstrained instances from the 22 Moré–Wild CUTEst sum-of-squares functions with several starting points, n = 2–12 (p. 21).
  - Noise: each residual f_i is perturbed additively by an independent uniform Θ_i on [−σ|f(x0) − f*|, σ|f(x0) − f*|], with σ = 1%, 3%, 5% (pp. 21, 23). The Rosenbrock case is worked out as an example (p. 23).
  - Budget and measures: 1000(n + 1) noisy evaluations; data profiles (evaluations/(n + 1)) and performance profiles at tolerances τ = 10⁻¹ and 10⁻³ (p. 22).
  - Competitors, all from NOMAD 3.9.1: Robust-MADS (NOMAD-robust), MADS without Search (NOMAD-basic), and the default (OrthoMADS n + 1, anisotropic mesh, quadratic models) (Table 1, p. 21). Search is disabled for StoMADS, NOMAD-robust and NOMAD-basic (p. 21).
  - Five StoMADS variants use n_k = 1…5 new evaluations per point, which does not meet the theoretical sample size (p. 22), and γε_f = 0.17 was chosen arbitrarily (p. 22).
  - Reported outcomes: StoMADS beats NOMAD-robust and both deterministic variants. More noise means fewer problems solved. n_k = 1–2 are good at low budgets, and n_k = 4 seems preferable for larger budgets (pp. 23–24).
  - Not reported in the version read: the number of replications or seeds, implementation details, and any competitor from another family (e.g. the stochastic trust-region methods it cites) (pp. 21–24).
  - Transferable technique: build noisy test sets by perturbing each residual of a sum-of-squares with noise scaled by |f(x0) − f*|, so a given σ means a comparable relative noise level on every problem.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper is an import and rehosting: StoMADS is analyzed with elements from MADS [7], Bandeira–Scheinberg–Vicente [16], Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg [23] and Paquette–Scheinberg [38] (p. 2). Seven of its 40 references have Scheinberg as a co-author (pp. 29–31). Borrowing is attributed per result: Def. 1 is adapted from [23] (p. 4), the conditioning-on-the-past formalism comes from [23, 38] (p. 8), and Lemma 1 is slightly modified from [38] (p. 9). Criticism of the group's own earlier work is direct: Robust-MADS lacks stochastic tests and does not show how improvement of the smoothed function implies decrease of the true one (pp. 3, 28), and Bouchet's method is computationally expensive (p. 3). Stochastic methods are classified with Angün–Kleijnen's white-box vs blackbox split (p. 2), and stochastic Nelder–Mead methods are separated out (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: when you borrow a proof architecture, say per definition and lemma what was taken and what was modified.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The novelty claim appears twice with the same content, in the introduction (pp. 3–4) and the Discussion (p. 28). Random variables are uppercase and their realizations lowercase, a convention stated once (p. 7). Remarks 1–3 after key results explain what they mean for the algorithm: variance control, strength of the whole-sequence result, estimates becoming representative (pp. 9, 16). Hedging concentrates in the experiments (sample reuse seems very useful, p. 11; the variants seemed to work well enough, p. 22; noise levels arbitrarily chosen, p. 23), while theory statements are unhedged. The theory–practice gap on sample sizes is admitted in the experiment section (p. 22) and again in the conclusion (p. 28; Quote 2).
  - Transferable technique: when the experiments do not meet the theory's prescription, say so where the setup is described and again in the conclusion.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions come in layers (p. 28): an algorithm (three-way test on probabilistic estimates), theory (a.s. summability of mesh sizes, refining subsequences not necessarily of mesh local optimizers, a.s. Clarke stationarity), and a computational study. The limits are specific: unconstrained problems only; sample sizes in practice below the theory (pp. 22, 28); compactness of the iterates assumed for convenience (p. 16); no rates (whole paper). Future work: stochastically noisy constrained and chance-constrained blackbox optimization (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll): evidence ✅. The Search/Poll/mesh/frame framework is kept, and the change is confined to how success is decided (pp. 4–7). Variant ⚠: all experiments run with the Search disabled (p. 21), so the free-Search half is not exercised.
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder): evidence ✅ in probabilistic form: zero order (Σ Δ_m < ∞ a.s.) → refining subsequence → a.s. Clarke stationarity (pp. 11–20). Variant ⚠: no counterexamples test the necessity of the hypotheses, and compactness of the iterates is assumed for simplicity (p. 16).
- Method 5 (Budget-aware benchmarking): variant ⚠. Equal evaluation budgets and data/performance profiles at two tolerances match the method (p. 22). But all competitors come from NOMAD, all problems are analytic with artificial noise, and replications are not reported (pp. 21–24). Contradiction ✗ with a SKILL.md detail: the Method 5 practice line cites StoMADS on the YATSOp/STARS problems, but the version read tests only the 66 Moré–Wild problems (pp. 21–27). The published COAP version was not read, so this may be a version difference.
- Method 6 (Solver as research instrument): variant ⚠. Baselines are NOMAD 3.9.1 configurations (p. 21), but StoMADS itself was not yet in NOMAD (cf. S021, p. 18).
- SKILL.md Roundtable (vs Scheinberg lens): evidence ✅. The guarantees are almost-sure and asymptotic, with no expected-iteration bound (pp. 11–20), and the probabilistic-estimate conditions are Scheinberg-school imports (pp. 2, 8–10).
- new-pattern candidate: **import and rehost a proof architecture**. Take the supermartingale analysis of probabilistic trust-region/line-search methods and rehost it on the mesh/frame by replacing model decrease with an estimate-based sufficient-decrease test (pp. 2, 12).
- new-pattern candidate: **step size doubles as accuracy controller**. Accuracy is required relative to δ_p², so shrinking the frame automatically drives the estimate variance to zero (Remark 1, p. 9; Remark 3, p. 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Three-way acceptance test with type-specific step updates (τ⁻² / τ² / τ) (pp. 5–7).
- [D2] Cap the step size (τ^{−ẑ}) so that the random step size is integrable and conditional expectations are well defined (pp. 5, 9).
- [D2] Accumulate samples at the incumbent across iterations (formulas (3)–(4)) to raise accuracy at no extra evaluation cost (pp. 10–11).
- [D4] Potential Φ = ν(f − f_min) + (1 − ν)Δ² with explicit inequalities (6) for ν and (7) for β (pp. 12–15).
- [D4] Prove liminf-type stationarity by contradiction: a log-step submartingale dominated from below by a ±1 random walk that must reach +∞ a.s. (pp. 18–19).
- [D5] Residual-wise noise scaled by |f(x0) − f*| at several σ levels (pp. 21, 23).

**Quotes**:
- "The main novelty of the present work is that no model or gradient information is needed to find descent directions, compared to prior works, in particular [23, 38] and [40]." (p. 3)
- "MADS is not appropriate for stochastic blackbox optimization, even though StoMADS estimates accuracy do not meet the prescription that has been derived theoretically." (p. 28)

### S021 · Algorithm 1027: NOMAD Version 4: Nonlinear Optimization with the MADS Algorithm (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 2022, 10.1145/3544489; arXiv 2104.11627)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Le Digabel, V. Rochon Montplaisir, C. Tribes · **Position in trajectory**: The version read is arXiv v2 of 4 May 2021 (p. 1). The TOMS version was not read. NOMAD started in 2001, and version 3 dates from 2008 (p. 2). Funding: NSERC CRD RDCPJ 490744-15 with an InnovÉÉ grant (Hydro-Québec, Rio Tinto) and NSERC Alliance 544900-19 with Huawei-Canada (p. 18). This is one of SKILL.md's Signature Works (Method 6, Method 5) and the consolidation-period milestone. **Read**: all 22 pages. The references (pp. 18–22) were skimmed. The component and thread diagrams (Figs. 1–8, pp. 6–13) are partly garbled in extraction and were read with their captions and surrounding text. Plot axes in Figs. 9–12 are only partly readable.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is software debt, not a new algorithm. NOMAD 3 grew through minor releases. Recent algorithmic work required changes in many portions of the code, and the complex interactions between algorithms and sub-algorithms had not been anticipated, so the team decided on a complete redesign (p. 2). A second driver: the NOMAD 3 parallel strategies could not use clusters with thousands of cores (p. 2). Twenty years of development are sorted retrospectively into three avenues: algorithmic improvement (constraints, scaling, surrogates), sub- and super-algorithms (e.g. VNS/NM/models inside MADS; BiMADS, RobustMADS, PSDMADS around it), and performance and parallelism (p. 2). The requirements follow from these: maintainability through reusable components, efficient use of many cores, easy tuning and comparison of algorithms, and optimization performance maintained between versions (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: before a redesign paper, classify your past contributions into a few avenues and derive the requirements of the new design from them.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Algorithmic components*: every algorithm is a component executing the generic tasks Start/Run/End, arranged in a depth-first hierarchy of nested components and specialized tasks, with optional tasks in brackets (p. 5). Examples are the LH component (Fig. 1, p. 6) and the Mads component, which consists of Initialization plus Iterations of Update, several Searches and a Poll (Fig. 2, p. 7).
  - *The theory's contract becomes an interface*: any algorithm may run inside the Search as long as it produces finitely many points, projects them on the current mesh, and respects the evaluation budget (p. 6).
  - *Recursion*: QuadraticModelSearch optimizes the model with a nested Mads instance whose own quadratic-model search is disabled to avoid infinite recursion (p. 7).
  - *Super-algorithms*: biobjective solving via a series of single-objective runs and PSDMADS reuse the Mads component (p. 8).
  - *Evaluation queue*: a priority queue implemented as a sorted vector, with cache lookup and opportunism (skip the rest of the queue after a success). Ordering can follow the last successful direction, the generation order, lexicographic order or random order (pp. 8–9).
  - *Threads*: the main thread runs all algorithmic tasks, while secondary threads only evaluate points; OpenMP manages them (p. 9).
  - *MegaSearchPoll*: all Search and Poll points are generated before any evaluation, so more cores are busy at once (pp. 9–10).
  - *PSDMADS rebuilt*: workers solve random subspaces of dimension 2–4, and a pollster in the full space evaluates a single poll point. The pollster rarely improves the incumbent but is required for convergence. Mesh sizes are bounded by a master mesh whose update is now delayed until enough variables have been explored (p. 11).
  - *Hot and warm restarts*, added at a key user's request (p. 13).
  - *Unchanged*: MADS itself (Algorithm 2.1, p. 4) and the coordinate-direction mesh (p. 4). Of NOMAD 3's poll types, only OrthoMADS 2n is ported so far (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: turn the theorem's requirements on the free step (finite, projected on the mesh, within budget) into the plug-in interface, so every plug-in automatically preserves the guarantee.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The architecture assumes that one blackbox evaluation costs far more than all other algorithmic tasks (p. 8). f may take the value ∞ for failed evaluations (p. 3). The problem form is min f(x) s.t. c(x) ≤ 0, x ∈ X (p. 3). Sequential MADS is recommended for n ≤ 50 (p. 11). The mesh-size adjustment parameter τ is rational, and δ = min{Δ, Δ²} (p. 4). Reader's note: Algorithm 2.1 takes x0 ∈ Ω (p. 4), while §3 says x0 is not required to be feasible (p. 5), a difference between the EB and PB presentations.
  - Transferable technique: state the cost model your architecture relies on (evaluation cost ≫ overhead) as an explicit design assumption.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: There is no new theorem. Correctness is argued in two ways: (i) the redesign inherits MADS's hierarchical convergence analysis (p. 2) by keeping the theory-bearing parts intact (mesh projection of Search points, p. 6; the pollster in PSDMADS, p. 11); (ii) empirical parity with NOMAD 3 serves as the test that the algorithms were coded correctly (p. 14; Quote 2).
  - Transferable technique: in a software paper, name the component that carries the convergence guarantee and show that the redesign did not touch it.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Tool: the in-house Runner (p. 14). Measure: data profiles with the x-axis in groups of n + 1 evaluations (p. 14).
  - Equalization: NOMAD 4 uses its defaults except that the direction type is set to ORTHO 2N and points are ordered by the last successful direction, because NOMAD 3's defaults (ORTHO N+1 QUAD, model-based ordering) were not yet implemented in NOMAD 4 (p. 14). Both versions are thus restricted to their common feature set.
  - Smooth unconstrained set: 53 Moré–Wild problems, n = 2–12, budget 400(n + 1), 10 seeds, τ = 10⁻² and 10⁻⁴. Performance is comparable with no clear dominance (p. 14; Fig. 9, p. 15).
  - Constrained set: 18 analytic problems (Table 1: n = 2–20, m = 1–15, 13 with bounds, sources from Hock–Schittkowski and Lukšan–Vlček to MDO and SPRING), budget 1000(n + 1), 10 seeds. Performance is similar (pp. 14–16).
  - PSDMADS: SRosenbr50/250, 30 runs each, average with min/max bars. For a comparable task distribution, NOMAD 3 uses MPI with 6 processes and NOMAD 4 uses 4 OpenMP main threads. NOMAD 4's worst run is significantly better than NOMAD 3's best (pp. 14–16). Reader's note: the setup text says the 2 variables per worker are chosen from 60 available (p. 15), which is inconsistent with n = 50 and n = 250 (p. 14).
  - Wall-clock: Solar 7 (about 5 s per evaluation, 7 variables of which 1 integer, 6 constraints, bounded) on an 8-core machine: 1 core vs 8 cores vs 8 cores + MegaSearchPoll, 10 seeds. Data profiles against real time and time-vs-evaluations plots show up to 3.3× speed-up (pp. 16–17).
  - No other solver is compared, although the paper itself says new developments must be compared with other blackbox optimizers (p. 3).

### S123 · Hierarchically constrained blackbox optimization (Operations Research Letters 50(5):446–451, 2022, 10.1016/j.orl.2022.06.006; arXiv 2111.13953)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Alarie (Hydro-Québec and IREQ), P. Jacquot (EDF), S. Le Digabel (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Funded by a MITACS Elevate grant with Hydro-Québec (p. 1) and motivated by Hydro-Québec TransÉnergie's PRIAD maintenance project (p. 2). SKILL.md already cites it for Method 2, Heuristic 9 and the tension between blackbox doctrine and structure exploitation. The version read is arXiv v1 of 30 Nov 2021 (10 pages). The ORL version was not read. Reader's link, inferred from the name only: SKILL.md's Latest section lists Micro-PRIAD v1.0 (Aug 2026), a stochastic power-utility maintenance benchmark, which may be this project's benchmark descendant. **Read**: all 10 pages. Figures 1–2 (data profiles, pp. 8–9) are images, so their captions and the text's reading of them were read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is industrial. In PRIAD, each evaluation of a maintenance-frequency vector runs five chained blackboxes: workload feasibility, equipment failure probabilities, scenario generation, power-flow simulations, and risk/cost assessment. Outputs feed the next inputs, and cost grows from seconds (first two) to days (fourth). The model was still under development (p. 2). The problem is min f(x) s.t. c_j(x) ≤ 0, where each c_j and f can be evaluated independently or sequentially, with c_j = +∞ when an evaluation cannot finish (p. 1), and a decision can be taken after each constraint (p. 2). The goal is to minimize total computational effort (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: turn an operational property of the simulator (a sequential pipeline with rising cost per stage) into the defining assumption of a new problem class.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Two two-phase procedures, each a feasibility phase followed by an optimization phase, built on MADS with the extreme barrier (p. 2).
  - *Int*: the feasibility phase minimizes h = Σ max(c_j, 0)² with EB and stops evaluating a trial point t as soon as a partial sum s_ℓ(t) reaches h(x^k). This is valid because the partial sums are monotone in ℓ, so t can only replace the incumbent if every partial sum stays below h(x^k). The optimization phase stops at the first violated constraint (pp. 3–4, Algorithm 2.1).
  - *Hier*: solve in sequence (P_j): min c_j s.t. c_1, …, c_{j−1} ≤ 0, each with EB and stopped at its first feasible point, which seeds the next problem. The optimization phase is the same as Int's (pp. 4–5, Algorithm 2.2).
  - Why EB and not PB: the progressive barrier needs all constraint values at every trial point (p. 3). The MADS algorithm itself is unchanged (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when a decision depends on a sum of nonnegative terms computed one at a time, stop as soon as the partial sum makes the decision certain.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: h uses squared violations, from filter methods (p. 2). X is R^n, N^n or a bounded subset (p. 1). The equivalence result needs no dynamic surrogate models and the same parameters and random seeds (p. 4). The constraint ordering is a user input, by cost or by estimated probability of violation (pp. 6, 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: Proposition 2.1: MADS with EB and Int produce the same sequence of iterates from the same starting point when no dynamic surrogate models are used (p. 4). Proof: the same incumbent, parameters and seeds give the same list of trial points. In each phase, a two-case analysis (the trial point improves or it does not) shows both methods make the same accept/reject decision, so the interruption removes only information that could not change a decision (p. 4). The paper names why models break the result: models are built from evaluated values, and Int evaluates fewer of them (p. 4). For Hier, the text states only that it produces different trial points from EB and Int. No equivalence or convergence result is given for it (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: prove that a cost-saving variant is decision-equivalent to the baseline, so the baseline's convergence theory transfers for free, and name the exact feature (here dynamic models) that breaks the equivalence.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Test problem: tension/compression spring design (TCSD), 3 bounded variables and 4 nonlinear constraints, in closed form. The paper states plainly that it is not a blackbox and serves as an illustration (p. 5; Quote 2). Feasible points are hard to find and there are many local optima (p. 6). The best known f is 0.0126652 (p. 6).
  - Cost model: multiplications and divisions per function (c1 1, c2 4, c3 8, c4 14, f 3), with a budget of 10,000 such operations (p. 6). There are 40 instances from random infeasible starting points drawn uniformly in the bounds (p. 6).
  - Baselines: NOMAD with EB and with PB (p. 5).
  - Measures: average cost to the first feasible point, number of calls per function, and average and best final f (Tables 3 and 5, pp. 7, 9), plus data profiles at a 5% gap against cost (pp. 7–9).
  - Two constraint orderings: by increasing cost, and by empirical infeasibility estimated from 5,000 Latin-hypercube points (c3 2%, c1 34%, c2 99%, c4 99% feasible, p. 8).
  - Results: Hier reaches a first feasible point about 60% faster than PB (p. 7). Int dominates most of the profile. EB is slightly ahead near a cost of 7,000, which the authors attribute to NOMAD's default quadratic models, outside Prop. 2.1's hypotheses (p. 7). A division by zero in c4 ends some EB and PB evaluations before f is computed (p. 7). With the infeasibility ordering, Int's cost to feasibility drops by about 8% and Hier's rises by 3.5% (p. 8). At a cost of 4,000, Int and Hier have solved about 80% of instances vs about 40% for EB and PB (p. 9).
  - From the tables: Hier has the worst average final f under both orderings (0.0133600 and 0.0133596 vs 0.0129580–0.0131401) (pp. 7, 9). The text calls final values comparable for the second ordering (p. 8). Reader's note: the EB and PB rows differ between the two series because the random starting points were redrawn (p. 8), so the orderings are not compared on identical instances.
  - Transferable technique: when the real blackbox is not ready, emulate evaluation cost on a closed-form problem with a proxy cost unit (operation counts) and a budget in that unit, and say plainly that it is an illustration.
- **D6 Positioning**: The positioning is minimal. The paper has 12 references: 5 group works (MADS, PB, the textbook, granular MADS, the NOMAD 3 TOMS paper), the Fletcher–Leyffer filter, the TCSD sources, Moré–Wild, Latin hypercube sampling, and two industrial sources (p. 10). No prior work on partial, interrupted or lazy evaluation of constraints is cited (p. 10). Industrial knowledge is used as a design argument: Rio Tinto engineers know that keeping flood risk low at Kemano is much harder than ensuring continuous smelter operation, which motivates ordering by infeasibility (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A letter format. The industrial pipeline is described before the method (p. 2). There is one proposition with a short case proof (p. 4) and an honest framing of the test problem (p. 5). The one experimental anomaly against the proposition is explained where it appears (p. 7). Hedging is uneven: results suggest effects (pp. 7, 9), but the discussion asserts that industrial problems will undoubtedly benefit, without real-blackbox evidence (p. 9; Quote 1).
  - Transferable technique: when a plot seems to contradict your proposition, name the violated hypothesis right there in the text.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are two interruption procedures, one equivalence proposition, and an illustration (abstract, p. 1; p. 9). The limits are specific: one closed-form test problem, a static constraint order, and the objective always evaluated last (pp. 5, 9). Future work: dynamic ordering of constraints, inserting the objective into the sequence, and above all application to real blackboxes (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first): variant ⚠ (extension). Constraints are characterized not only by relaxability but by evaluation cost, position in the pipeline and likelihood of violation (pp. 2, 6, 8). ### S138 · Counterexample and an additional revealing poll step for a result of "analysis of direct searches for discontinuous functions" (Mathematical Programming 208(1):411–424, 2024, 10.1007/s10107-023-02042-3; arXiv 2211.09947)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: P.-Y. Bouchet (GERAD), L. Bourdin (XLIM, University of Limoges) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The version read is arXiv v2 of 18 Dec 2022, titled *Erratum, counterexample and an additional revealing poll step for a result of "Analysis of direct searches for discontinuous functions"* (p. 1). The published title drops the word *Erratum*, and the published version was not read. Funding: NSERC Discovery Grant 2020–04448 (p. 1). The target is Vicente & Custódio, Math. Program. 133 (2012) [5], the same paper the 2012 survey cites approvingly as enriching the convergence hierarchy (S023, p. 15). The repair reuses the Revealing Poll of DiscoMADS (Audet, Batailly, Kojtych, SIAM J. Optim. 2022) [2] (pp. 6–7). Bouchet is the MSc student whose variable-precision work S053 cites (S053, p. 3). SKILL.md already cites this paper for Method 3 and Heuristic 4. **Read**: all 10 pages. Figure 1 (p. 3) is garbled in extraction, so its described construction (pp. 3–4) was read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an audit of a published theorem. The main results of [5] (Theorems 3.1–3.2: if a refining subsequence has f(x_k) → f(x*), the Rockafellar generalized derivative is nonnegative along refining tangent directions) are acknowledged as important extensions to discontinuous functions (p. 2). The target is the end-of-paper result [5, Theorem 4.1] and [5, Corollary 4.1]. These try to give sufficient conditions for f(x_k) → f(x*) when f is piecewise Lipschitz on a finite partition near x* whose pieces satisfy an exterior cone property (pp. 2–3). The claim is that one statement of Theorem 4.1 is incorrect and that this also invalidates the corollary (p. 2; Quote 1). The note reproduces the target's assumptions (2.1 and 4.1), theorem and corollary in full before attacking them (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: when auditing a theorem, restate its assumptions and conclusions in full in your note, so readers can check the counterexample against them without the original.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper produces two things, a counterexample and a repair.
  - *Counterexample (§2)*: f is the convex quadratic (x + 1)² − 2 for x ≤ 0 (so f(0) = −1). For x > 0 it is built from scaled copies 2^ℓ p(x/2^ℓ) of a quartic p on [1, 2] with p(1) = 1, p(2) = 2 and a single interior local minimizer at 5/4, so f has local minima at (5/4)·2^ℓ for every integer ℓ (pp. 3–4). The dDSM instance: x0 = 5/4, α0 = 1/4, D_k = {−1, 1}, α halved on failure and kept on success. The Search step is empty at even iterations and proposes the single point x_k − 5α_k at odd iterations, which is exactly the next local minimizer to the left (p. 4). By construction, even iterations fail and odd iterations succeed, x_k → 0 and α_k → 0, and no trial point ever enters x ≤ 0 (pp. 3–5).
  - *Repair (§4)*: a Revealing Poll step is inserted before the Poll. At every iteration it evaluates at least one point drawn uniformly in a ball of fixed radius R around x_k, and accepts it under the same forcing-function sufficient-decrease rule (Algorithm 1, p. 7). Because the Revealing Poll can be viewed as a specific Search step, the algorithm inherits all properties of [5, Algorithm 2.1] (p. 7). Reader's note: the prose says the step evaluates a random mesh point (p. 7), while Algorithm 1 draws uniformly from the ball with no mesh (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: design a fix as an extra step that the old proof can treat as a Search step, so the old results survive and only the new result needs a new proof.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The counterexample satisfies [5, Assumption 2.1] (bounded level set, f bounded below) and [5, Assumption 4.1], with the partition B1 = ]−2, 0], B2 = ]0, 2[: nonempty interiors, exterior cone property, and f Lipschitz on each piece and continuously extendable to its boundary (Prop. 1, p. 4). f is lower semicontinuous at x* = 0 (pp. 2, 5). The refining directions {−1, 1} are dense in the unit sphere because in R the unit sphere is exactly {−1, 1} (Prop. 2, p. 5). The repair's Assumption 1 replaces the exterior cone property by cl(B_i) = cl(int B_i), i.e. no branch has a meagre part that does not adhere to its interior (p. 8). The forcing function ρ is continuous and nondecreasing with ρ(t)/t → 0 (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: when a theorem relies on directions being dense in the unit sphere, test it first in dimension one, where the hypothesis holds with a finite set.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**:
  - *Verification*: Lemma 1 proves the iterate and step-size pattern by induction over q (pp. 4–5). The theorem's claim (a) holds, while claims (b) and (c) fail: lim f(x_k) = 0 ≠ −1 = f(x*), and no trial point lies in B1 (p. 5).
  - *Proof diagnosis (§3)*: the original proof is summarized in six numbered paragraphs, plus a seventh for the corollary. Steps 1–4 are certified as valid. The error is in step 5, which takes strict decrease of the extension f̃ along d at x* to imply a negative Clarke derivative. The single-variable function x ↦ −|x| refutes this: its Clarke derivative at 0 equals 1 in both directions (p. 6). The erroneous passage is quoted, and steps 6–7 are shown to depend on it (p. 6).
  - *Repair proof*: Lemma 2, adapted from DiscoMADS Lemma 4.12, shows that the revealing-poll points are a.s. dense in B_R(x*) and the refining directions a.s. dense in the sphere. The argument lower-bounds the probability of hitting any small ball by a volume ratio that stays positive, then uses independence across iterations (p. 8).
  - *Theorem 1*: under [5, Assumption 2.1] and Assumption 1, with f lower semicontinuous at x*: in the interior case, f(x_k) → f(x*) and Clarke stationarity holds on tangent refining directions. In the boundary case, a subsequence in cl(B*) has f(x_k) → f(x*) and trial points a.s. dense in B ∩ B_R(x*), for any number n_B of pieces, not only n_B = 2 (pp. 8–9). The limit is proved by contradiction with the continuity of f restricted to B*, using density (p. 9). Density of refining directions also keeps [5, Theorems 3.1–3.2] usable (p. 9).
