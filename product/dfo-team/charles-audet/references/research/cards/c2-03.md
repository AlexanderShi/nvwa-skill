# Batch c2-03 · paper cards (core)

**Batch intent**: See how Audet, in the NOMAD 3 period (2007–2009 preprints), turns the MADS framework into robust practice along three lines, and how he later frames the field publicly. The lines are the poll directions (OrthoMADS derandomises LTMADS, S006), the constraint treatment (the progressive barrier for relaxable constraints and infeasible starts, S007) and multi-lab comparison on a shared real problem (the groundwater community problems, S022, where Audet is one of 15 authors). The 2016 special-issue editorial (S034) shows the public framing. The questions: do Method 1 (free Search, rigid Poll), Method 2 (constraint semantics), Method 3 (guarantee ladder bounded by counterexamples) and Method 5 (budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking) hold in these source papers? And which experiment protocols did the group use before data profiles became its standard?
**Focus dimensions**: D1–D8 (all; core batch). Emphasis on D2 (what is kept and what is changed relative to MADS/LTMADS), D4 (proof devices, counterexample construction) and D5 (comparison protocols).
**Material roles**: S022 core · S006 core · S007 core · S034 core (a 2-page editorial, so D4 and D5 do not apply). Version caveats: all three research papers are preprints, not the journal versions. S022 is "Preprint submitted to Elsevier 14 January 2008" (p. 1). S006 is dated 28th February 2008 (p. 1). S007 is dated June 26, 2007 and carries the preprint title "A MADS algorithm with a progressive barrier for derivative-free nonlinear programming" (p. 1). All page references use the `[[page N]]` markers of the text files. For S006 and S007, file page N = printed preprint page N−1. For S022 and S034, file page = printed page.

### S022 · Comparison of derivative-free optimization methods for groundwater supply and hydraulic capture community problems (Advances in Water Resources 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.advwatres.2008.01.010)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K.R. Fowler (corresponding author, Clarkson), J.P. Reese, C.E. Kees, J.E. Dennis Jr., C.T. Kelley, C.T. Miller, C. Audet, A.J. Booker, G. Couture, R.W. Darwin, M.W. Farthing, D.E. Finkel, J.M. Gablonsky, G. Gray, T.G. Kolda (15 authors; Audet is the 7th; GERAD affiliation shared with Couture; pp. 1–2) · **Read level**: full, pp. 1–29 plus Appendix A (p. 35). The reference list (pp. 30–34) was skimmed. Figures 1–4 are extracted only as axis ticks and legends (pp. 18–19, 23–24), so all results come from Tables 3–8 and the prose. The paper does not say which author ran which code. NOMAD is cited as "The NOMAD project" (Couture, Audet, Dennis, Abramson, 2002; p. 31), which suggests the GERAD authors supplied it (inference). · **Position in research trajectory**: the 2003–2009 theory-building period, when NOMAD was still a GPS-based C++ code: "The version of NOMAD used in these tests is C++ optimization software based on the generalized pattern search (GPS) algorithm" (p. 35). The paper is a multi-lab effort (NCSU, Clarkson, UNC, ERDC, Boeing, Sandia, GERAD, MIT LL; p. 1) around the water-resources "community problems" of Mayer, Kelley and Miller [58].

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The entry is an application community's shared benchmark, not an algorithmic gap. The paper names two impediments. First, most work tests few methods on an idealized example that may not share the real problems' difficulty. Second, many methods "have yet to be compared" or even "considered by the water resources community" (p. 3). Mayer et al.'s community problems (CPs) were meant to catalyze exactly this. Two areas have not yet succeeded: introducing new method classes "by experts in mathematical optimization" and comparing "significant sets of methods for the same problem" (p. 3). The problem features are named up front: nonsmooth, nonlinear, discontinuous, nonconvex, with many local minima (p. 4). The water community's default answer is GAs and simulated annealing (p. 4). Three numbered objectives are stated: detail the methods, evaluate solution quality and effort against problem specification and initial conditions, and "provide guidance for selecting an initial iterate" (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: to enter an application field, adopt its own published benchmark and state numbered objectives, one of which is practical guidance for its users (here starting points), not only a ranking.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: No new algorithm. The design effort goes into the comparison and the problem formulation.
  - *Portfolio.* Six codes chosen "with the goal of testing algorithms each with different strengths" (p. 10): APPS (Sandia), Boeing Design Explorer (DE, the only commercial one), DIRECT-L (NCSU), IFFCO (NCSU), NOMAD with n+1 and 2n directions, and NSGA-II as the GA (pp. 11, 13).
  - *Unifying lens.* The stencil-based codes are described through a "search-poll paradigm". A search step "allows a great deal of freedom in seeking a better point with the understanding that if this fails to produce improvement, then the algorithm will fall back on a local poll" (p. 11). The codes are then placed in it: DE's search optimizes Kriging surrogates and polls on 2n coordinate directions when a filter shows no progress (pp. 12, 15). IFFCO polls first to estimate a gradient and uses a quasi-Newton quadratic in the search (p. 12). APPS reduces step sizes per direction asynchronously (p. 12). DIRECT and the GA are presented under "Unstructured Searches" (§3.2, pp. 12–13).
  - *Formulation devices.* An inactive-well threshold |Q_i| < 10⁻⁶ removes a well from the simulation and the cost, which makes the objective discontinuous but lets a method drop a well's capital cost (p. 9). Real-valued well locations are rounded to MODFLOW grid nodes, "which creates a step function" (p. 10). A mixed-integer GA variant adds an integer variable for which well is off (p. 21). Plume capture uses head-difference ("gradient control") constraints at M = 5 points (pp. 8, 22).
  - *Constraint handling* (§3.3, pp. 13–14). Bounds are built into every algorithm. Linear and nonlinear constraints are handled by rejection (a barrier) in APPS, DE, IFFCO and NOMAD, and by a penalty in the GA and DIRECT. Violations of the linear net-pumping constraint are declared evaluation failures, so MODFLOW is not called (p. 14).
  - *Termination.* Stencil-based codes stop when the stencil size equals the simulator's spatial grid resolution (p. 16). Budgets are checked only after an iteration, so runs can overshoot (p. 15).
  - What is not changed: every code runs at its defaults ("we avoided detailed tuning", p. 17). NOMAD is run without surrogates (p. 15) and without its categorical-variable capability, "for comparison sake" (p. 28).
  - Transferable technique: describe heterogeneous competitors through one common skeleton (here search/poll: what each code does in the search step, in the poll step, and when it shrinks), so readers see exactly where the codes differ.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: A homogeneous unconfined aquifer on a 1000 × 1000 × 30 m domain, with K = 5.01×10⁻⁵ m/s, a 5-year horizon and the boundary conditions (3)–(8) (pp. 5–6). The cost model (9) has capital and operating terms with the Table 1 coefficients (pp. 6–7), and the constraints (10)–(15) have the Table 2 data (pp. 7–9). Grids are 50 × 50 × 10 for water supply (p. 17) and 100 × 100 for hydraulic capture (p. 22). Several choices are the authors' own interpretation, and the paper says so. Wells must be at least 200 m from the Dirichlet boundaries, "Although not specified in [58, 59]" (p. 8). The plume boundary is the 5×10⁻⁵ kg/m³ contour, a choice [58] leaves to the reader (p. 8). The conclusion lists the inactive-well threshold, grid rounding, MODFLOW and the discretization as "specific to this work" (p. 29). No simulator failures occurred (p. 14). No convergence assumptions are stated, since theory is deferred to the references (p. 10). Reader's notes: the head-difference bound d = 10⁻⁴ has units m in Table 2 (p. 9) but "m/s" in the text (p. 22). Table 4 lists an initial y(2) = 7800 m, outside the [0, 800] bound of (13) (p. 19), probably a typo for 780.
  - Transferable technique: when a benchmark is under-specified, list every interpretation you made where you make it, and repeat the list in the conclusion as specific to your study (pp. 8, 29).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: No theorems. The analytic content is limited to four items.
  - A feasibility claim: "It is easy to prove by examination of the objective function and constraints that a feasible solution to the water supply CP requires a minimum of five wells", which forces Q_i = −0.0064 m³/s for N_w = 5 (p. 17). No proof is given.
  - A landscape argument for failure (p. 21). From a six-well start that meets the demand, eliminating a well requires five rates to rise while one goes to zero. That raises the objective, and the rise "cannot be characterized as high frequency, low amplitude noise", so sampling methods do not find the region. The starting-point rule is derived from this mechanism (see D5).
  - The status of theory for the barrier approach, code by code (p. 14). Rejection of infeasible points is justified for linear constraints for NOMAD, DE, DIRECT and APPS, and "almost surely true for IFFCO". For nonlinear constraints, only DIRECT has a proof among the tested codes. Penalty methods need a "sufficiently large" constant for ℓ1 aggregation and one going to infinity for ℓ2 (p. 14).
  - Rigor is justified by diagnosis, not by performance (Quote 1, p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: when a local method fails from many starts, explain the failure by a landscape mechanism (a required move that raises the objective above the noise level), then design starting points that remove the need for that move (p. 21).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Metrics.* Best cost found and number of evaluations to termination (Tables 3 and 6), plus "cost profile as a function of the number of function evaluations" (p. 17; Figs. 2 and 4, log-scale x-axis). Run times are not compared, because only APPS ran in parallel (p. 17). All codes use defaults (p. 17).
  - *Five-well supply (10 location variables).* Starting from $127,421, NOMAD(2N) reaches $124,386 in 539 evaluations, NOMAD(N+1) $124,389 in 346, the GA $124,386 in 925, APPS $124,427 in 117, IFFCO $125,129 in 165, DIRECT-L $125,085 in 648 and DE $125,598 in 510 (Table 3, p. 18). The paper qualifies the <1% spread itself: the fixed cost is ~80% of the total (Quote 2, p. 19). IFFCO stagnated, and one restart fixes it. DIRECT and DE stopped early. APPS gave the lowest cost per evaluation up to its own termination. Codes seeded with the feasible start were more efficient (p. 20).
  - *Six-well supply.* A suite of starting points was generated by running DIRECT on a feasibility reformulation (50,000 evaluations, ~5,000 feasible points) and clustering the results with AGNES (p. 21). From most of the 135 starts, no seeded method found a five-well solution ("we tried 135 cases that failed", p. 21). With the rule to "specify at least five of the wells with the maximum possible rate", APPS and IFFCO succeeded and NOMAD succeeded in some cases, typically within 200 calls (p. 21).
  - *Hydraulic capture (4 candidate wells).* IFFCO reaches $23,421 in 385 evaluations, the mixed-integer GA $24,870 in 930 and APPS $25,018 in 111. NOMAD(N+1) reaches $50,574 in 94, NOMAD(2N) $50,797 in 168, DIRECT-L $49,549 in 592, the real-coded GA $54,973 in 930 and DE $68,238 in 665, all from $80,211 (Table 6, p. 24). The failures are diagnosed. NOMAD "terminated prematurely based on a small stencil size", and a larger initial stencil or a start with all wells at maximum rate is suggested (p. 26). DIRECT ran out of budget (p. 26). DE could not model the narrow inactive-well region (p. 26).
  - *Robustness.* From 65 clustered starts, APPS and IFFCO converged to local minima with all four wells on (p. 26). From two hand-designed starts they reached near-optimal one-well designs, IFFCO after one or two restarts (pp. 26–27; Table 8, p. 27). The GA failed on 9 of 10 random seeds. With a larger population, 10 seeds gave near-optimal designs, at 10⁴ evaluations (p. 27).
  - *Gradient-based control.* FDNIPS (OPT++, finite-difference gradients) failed in every option combination on the easiest (five-well) problem (pp. 27–28).
  - Reader's note: codes are compared at their own stopping points (94 to 930 evaluations in Table 6, p. 24), not at a common budget. The evaluation-based profiles (Figs. 2, 4) are the only budget-matched view, and they are not readable in the extraction. Deterministic codes run once per start, and only the GA's seed sensitivity is probed (p. 27).
  - Transferable technique: build a pool of diverse feasible starting points by running a global sampler on the feasibility problem and clustering its feasible hits (p. 21). Then report, per solver, from how many starts it succeeds.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper introduces "sampling methods" (the mathematical-optimization community's deterministic direct searches) to a field dominated by GAs and simulated annealing (p. 4). It contrasts them on two points: "there is no randomness in the method, and there are rigorous convergence results", with a GA included "for completeness" (pp. 1, 4). Each code is credited to its home institution and version, with a caveat that current downloads "could well be more general, robust, and efficient" (p. 11). Earlier CP work [32, 33, 39, 43, 44, 57] is cited as the baseline (p. 3). The one-well results "are comparable to those found in the literature" (p. 24). Surrogates "should not be discarded" despite DE's failure (p. 26). Hybrid local/global approaches already in the groundwater literature [70, 71] are endorsed (p. 28). The reliability argument for determinism is explicit (p. 25).
  - Transferable technique: when benchmarking other groups' codes, name developer, version and settings, and concede that newer versions may do better (p. 11). This disarms the fairness objection before a reviewer raises it.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A multi-author applied paper written for a water-resources audience. Terms are defined for that audience: "black-box formulation" as decoupling the physics solver from the optimizer (p. 9), and a surrogate as distinct from an approximation, because popular surrogates "can not be guaranteed to be close to the function they replace" (p. 15). Caveats sit next to results: the 1% spread is called "a bit misleading" (p. 19), and "care is needed in interpreting these results" (p. 19). The closing framing is modest: "This work is a preliminary attempt at that larger effort" (p. 29). Hedging is moderate ("appears", "almost surely", "could well"; pp. 11, 14, 20). Typos remain (optimzation, reliablity, threshhold; pp. 20, 25, 28). Audet's individual voice cannot be separated among 15 authors.
  - Transferable technique: when a headline difference is small, decompose the metric (fixed vs operating cost) in the same paragraph, so the reader sees how much of the metric the methods could actually affect (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is applied and methodological. It compares several DFO families on the same CPs, something the paper says the CPs had not yet achieved (p. 3). It also gives starting-point guidance and a download site with problem details and links to simulators and optimizers, http://www4.ncsu.edu/~ctk/community.html (p. 29). Limits are concrete. Global solutions are unknown (pp. 3, 16), there is no tuning (p. 17), NOMAD stopped early on capture (p. 26), fixed costs are hard for surrogates and thresholds (p. 28), and the problem interpretation is specific to this study (p. 29). Future directions: hybrid stencil-plus-global methods, methods that choose the number of wells while keeping the fast local convergence of stencil methods, and handling fixed costs (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: six codes from several families (pattern search, surrogate/Kriging, DIRECT, implicit filtering, GPS-based NOMAD, GA) on the same realistic problems, measured in function evaluations (pp. 11, 17). A gradient-based baseline is tried and fails (p. 28). ⚠ Variant: there is no common budget (termination points differ, Table 6, p. 24), no data or performance profiles (plain cost profiles, p. 17), and one run per deterministic code.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the search–poll paradigm is used as a lens to describe *other groups'* codes (pp. 11–12), not only MADS.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: constraints are classified by mathematical form (bounds, linear, nonlinear; p. 13), not by relaxability. The failure mechanism is reused to short-circuit cheap linear constraints before the simulator is called (p. 14).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: a real simulator-based problem with a public download page (p. 29). ⚠ Variant: the benchmark was created by the water community (Mayer et al. [58]), not by Audet's group.
- Research Taste 6 / Heuristic 8 ✅ evidence: repeatability of deterministic methods (p. 25).
- Warning sign 5 ✅ evidence: DFO is justified by the failure of a finite-difference interior-point code (pp. 27–28).
- Method 6 ✅ weak evidence: NOMAD (GPS-based C++, with a MATLAB version) is the group's contribution to a multi-lab study (pp. 11, 35).
- new-pattern candidate: **starting-point engineering as a reported result**. Starting points are generated (DIRECT feasibility runs plus clustering), counted (135 and 65 starts) and turned into a design rule (pp. 21, 26–27).
- new-pattern candidate: **cheap-constraint short-circuit**. Cheap constraints are checked before the expensive simulation, and violations are returned as failures (p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Feasible-start pool: run a global sampler on the feasibility problem, then cluster (AGNES) to pick representative starts; report success counts per solver (p. 21).
- Mechanism-derived starting rule: start where removing a costly component (a well) needs only one variable to move (p. 21).
- Skip the simulator when a cheap explicit constraint fails, and return an evaluation failure (p. 14).
- Tie the stopping tolerance to the simulator's resolution (stencil size equals grid spacing; p. 16).
- Probe the stochastic competitor's seed sensitivity (10 seeds) and population size before drawing conclusions (p. 27).
- Put a metric decomposition next to any claim that methods differ by less than 1% (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "Such analysis can assist in evaluation, tuning, and debugging by eliminating possible failure modes." (p. 14)
- "If viewed in terms of the total cost, the optimal solutions differed by less than 1%. This is a bit misleading because the fixed cost represented about 80% of the total cost." (p. 19)

### S006 · OrthoMADS: a deterministic MADS instance with orthogonal directions (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2009, DOI 10.1137/080716980)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M.A. Abramson (AFIT), J.E. Dennis Jr. (Rice), S. Le Digabel (GERAD) (p. 1) · **Read level**: full, pp. 1–23, including the references. Figure 1 is partly garbled but decodable from its caption (p. 4). Figure 3 is extracted as axis ticks and scattered symbols (p. 21). The value columns of Tables 8–9 are scrambled, so constrained and application results are taken from Table 10 and the text (pp. 19–20). · **Position in research trajectory**: the preprint is dated 28 February 2008. Its funding footnote lists FCAR, NSERC, LANL, AFOSR, the Boeing Company and ExxonMobil Upstream Research (p. 1). It replaces LTMADS, the first MADS instance [4], and relies on the MADS erratum [2] for the instance conditions (p. 22). Its tests draw on the progressive-barrier and PSD-MADS technical reports [5, 6] and the MDO/STY applications from the MADS-VNS paper [1] (pp. 15, 22). NOMAD is not mentioned anywhere in the text.

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The entry is two drawbacks of the authors' own LTMADS (p. 2). The polling directions are random, so convergence to Clarke stationary points holds only "with probability one". And it "has been observed [10]" (Custódio & Vicente) that LTMADS can produce "undesirably large angles" between poll directions. The acknowledgements credit Custódio and Vicente's remarks on the cones of directions missed in single LTMADS iterations, together with the authors' own similar observations. Booker is credited with suggesting quasi-Monte Carlo methods, which led to Halton sequences (p. 22). The problem statement is the MADS one: min f over Ω, with f : Ω → ℝ ∪ {∞} a blackbox and Ω possibly defined by "black-box nonlinear constraint, or even Boolean, functions", treated by the extreme barrier (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: turn an outside group's criticism of your own method into the paper's opening problem, credit them, and test on their problem sets (see D6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Replace LTMADS's random poll construction by a deterministic one, in three steps (pp. 3–4, Fig. 1).
  1. Take the t-th Halton vector u_t, started at t = n+1 to remove the linear correlation of early terms (p. 5).
  2. Scale and round it: q_t(α) = round(α(2u_t−e)/‖2u_t−e‖), with α chosen by bisection so that ‖q‖ is as close as possible to 2^{|ℓ|/2} without exceeding it (Problem (3), p. 6).
  3. Apply a scaled Householder transform, H = ‖q‖²(I − 2vvᵀ) = ‖q‖²I − 2qqᵀ, which yields an integer orthogonal basis (Prop. 2.5, pp. 8–9). The poll set is D_k = [H −H], i.e. 2n directions (p. 12).

  Index bookkeeping: whenever the poll size is the smallest so far, t_k = ℓ_k + n + 1. Otherwise a new Halton index is used. This way the failed iterations at the finest poll sizes run through the tail of the Halton sequence in order (p. 11).
  - What is *not* changed: "The LTMADS and ORTHOMADS algorithms are identical except for the construction of the set Pk and the POLL directions Dk" (p. 10). That covers the same mesh D = [I −I], τ = 4, w⁻ = −1 and w⁺ = 1 (p. 10). The Search stays optional: "This paper focuses on the POLL step" (p. 3). Allowing a negative ℓ_k is flagged as "not specific to ORTHOMADS" (p. 10).
  - Why this design: integer components keep poll points on the mesh (p. 3). Orthogonality minimizes the missed cones (p. 1). Determinism makes results reproducible (p. 3). The minimal n+1 positive basis is rejected "in order to keep orthogonal directions" (p. 12).
  - Transferable technique: to derandomize a randomized algorithm component, substitute a low-discrepancy sequence whose density is a known theorem, then make every subsequent transformation (scale-and-round, orthogonalize) provably density-preserving.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: No new assumptions. All MADS hypotheses are inherited from [4], and constraints use the extreme barrier (p. 2). The discussion claims the result comes "without probabilistic arguments, and without additional parameters" (p. 22). The only obligations are the four MADS-instance properties (p. 14). Reader's notes:
  - Lemma 2.2's proof picks α so that the largest-magnitude component of α(2u_t−e)/‖2u_t−e‖ is exactly ±1/2. The claim ‖q‖ = 1 therefore needs rounding of halves away from zero, a convention the paper does not state (p. 6).
  - The text says ℓ_0 is "set to 1" with Δ^m_0 = Δ^p_0 = 1 (p. 10). But (5) gives Δ^p = 1/2 for ℓ = 1, and Figure 2 initializes ℓ_0 ← 0 (p. 11).
  - Figure 2 sets t_k ← max{t_j} at non-smallest iterations (p. 11). The text ("keep increasing tk so that a new Halton direction is used", p. 11) and Table 3 (k = 3 uses 8, k = 7 uses 9; p. 12) use the maximum plus one.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The main result is Theorem 2.8, "ORTHOMADS is a valid MADS instance" (p. 14). Its proof checks the four axioms of a MADS instance from [2, 4], so all MADS convergence results are inherited deterministically.
  1. Poll directions are nonnegative integer combinations of D: by construction.
  2. Poll points lie within Δ^p_k of the poll center: since ‖D_k e_i‖ = ‖q‖² ≤ 2^{|ℓ|}, the bound ‖Δ^m_k D_k e_i‖ ≤ Δ^m_k 2^{|ℓ_k|} = Δ^p_k holds (pp. 12, 14).
  3. Limits of the normalized D_k are positive spanning sets: shown as in the LTMADS proof, using det(normalized H) = −1 (p. 14).
  4. Normalized directions over failed iterations are dense: a chain of results (below).

  The density chain for axiom 4:
  - The Halton sequence is dense in [0,1]ⁿ (Prop. 2.1). The proof is only sketched: Diophantine equations plus the Chinese Remainder Theorem, with a reference to [11] (p. 5).
  - α_{t,ℓ} ≥ 2^{|ℓ|/2}/√n − 1/2 (Lemma 2.3, pp. 6–7), and rounding perturbs the direction by less than ε/2 once α > 2√n/ε + √n/2 (Prop. 2.4, p. 8). Together these give density of the normalized q_{t,ℓ} with ℓ = t−n−1 (Prop. 2.7, pp. 12–14).
  - The Householder map preserves density (Prop. 2.6, pp. 9–10). The device: for a target unit vector w, choose d = (e_i − w)/√(2(1−w_i)), so that e_i − 2d d_i = w exactly, then bound the perturbation by 2‖δ_i(d+δ) + d_iδ‖ (pp. 9–10).
  - The set U of failed iterations at the smallest poll size so far uses the consecutive pairs (n+i, i−1). Δ^m_k → 0 along U comes from [4] (pp. 11, 14). No rates or complexity results.
  - Reader's note: Table 3's column ‖D_k e_i‖ mostly equals 2^{|ℓ_k|} (e.g. 4 at (t,ℓ) = (7,2)), whereas Table 2 gives ‖q_{7,2}‖² = 2, and row k = 8 shows 5. I could not reconcile these (pp. 7, 12).
  - Transferable technique: *prove membership, inherit theory*. Analyze a new variant by verifying the defining axioms of an already-analyzed framework, not by redoing the convergence proof (Theorem 2.8, p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: to show a map keeps a dense set of directions dense, construct the exact preimage of an arbitrary target (here the reflection vector mapping e_i to w), then bound the perturbation (Prop. 2.6, pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Problems.* 45 problems (p. 15): 21 smooth unconstrained CUTEr problems (the set of [10]), 13 nonsmooth problems from Lukšan–Vlček (the set of [9]), 9 constrained problems from [5, 6, 14], and 2 real applications (MDO, STY) from [1].
  - *Competitors.* GPS, run as GPS-EB and as GPS-FILTER on constrained problems because EB theory for GPS covers only linear constraints (p. 15), and LTMADS.
  - *Protocol.* LTMADS is run 30 times per problem. GPS and ORTHOMADS each get a score s: the number of LTMADS runs whose final f they match or beat, within 1% relative precision. Scores below 10 are bad, 10–19 acceptable, 20 or more good (p. 15). All methods use 2n maximal bases, the opportunistic poll and an "optimist" Search along the last successful direction, and no other Search (p. 15). Runs stop when Δ^p_k < 10⁻¹² or after 1000n evaluations (p. 15).
  - *Results.* Average score 22.3 for ORTHOMADS versus 13.5 (GPS-FILTER) and 14.2 (GPS-EB) (Table 10, p. 20). ORTHOMADS matched the best of the 30 LTMADS runs on 25 of 45 problems and scored ≥ 20 on 32. It was acceptable on 4 and bad on 9 (p. 20). Perfect scores on 17 of 21 smooth problems, good scores on 7 of 13 nonsmooth problems, 4 good and 4 bad among the constrained problems, and perfect scores on both applications (p. 15). Failures are visible, e.g. VARDIM n = 20 scored 0 (p. 17) and WONG2 scored 0 (p. 18).
  - *Direction diagnostic.* Figure 3 plots the normalized poll directions on Rosenbrock (n = 2 with 2000 evaluations, n = 3 with 3000). With LTMADS, half the n = 2 directions are ±e₁ or ±e₂, and at least two thirds of the n = 3 directions have a zero coordinate (p. 21).
  - Reader's note: the score uses only the final f value. Many runs stop at the 1000n cap (e.g. 10000 and 20000 evaluations in Tables 5–6, pp. 16–17), and no evaluation-based profile is shown. The competitors are all from the same family.
  - Transferable technique: to compare a deterministic method with a randomized predecessor, run the predecessor R times (here 30). Score the deterministic run by how many of the R runs it matches or beats within a relative tolerance, and bin the scores into bad/acceptable/good (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents itself as a correction of the authors' own first instance, LTMADS ("drawbacks that we wish to correct", p. 2). It builds on MADS [4] and the erratum [2] for the instance conditions (p. 14), contrasts with GPS's finite set of directions (p. 3), and uses Coope–Price [7] for the definition of limits of direction sets (p. 14). The critics' own test sets are reused: Custódio–Vicente [10] for smooth problems and Custódio–Dennis–Vicente [9] for nonsmooth ones (p. 15). Halton [11], Householder [12] and Matoušek [15] are cited purely as tools (pp. 4–5, 8). No other DFO family is cited or compared (reference list, pp. 22–23).
  - Transferable technique: test your fix on the exact problem sets of the paper whose criticism motivated it, so it is judged on the critic's ground (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract gives the two advantages (determinism and orthogonality) in sentence 2, inherited convergence in sentence 3 and "quite good" numerics last (p. 1). The contribution sentence is a plain purpose statement: "The purpose of this paper is to introduce a new variant of MADS" (p. 2). ### S007 · A progressive barrier for derivative-free nonlinear programming (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2009, DOI 10.1137/070692662)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J.E. Dennis Jr. (Rice) (p. 1) · **Read level**: full, pp. 1–34, including the references. Figures 1–3 (the (h, f) plane) and 5 are partly garbled but readable with their captions (pp. 7, 10–11, 19). The curves in Figures 6–11 are not in the extraction (pp. 26–32). · **Position in research trajectory**: the preprint is dated June 26, 2007 and titled "A MADS algorithm with a progressive barrier for derivative-free nonlinear programming". It is funded by FCAR, NSERC, LANL, AFOSR, the Boeing Company and ExxonMobil (p. 1). It combines the authors' GPS-filter method (SIAM J. Optim. 2004, [5]) with MADS-EB ([6], 2006) (p. 2). It is cited as GERAD report G-2007-37 in S006 (p. 22 of S006). The directions used are LTMADS's, whose density holds with probability 1 (p. 12). S006 closes that gap.

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The entry is the authors' own two methods. GPS-filter [5] has convergence results "limited by the GPS restriction to a fixed finite set of directions" and known pathological cases, yet "has solved some difficult industrial problems" (p. 2). MADS-EB [6] has strong results but rejects all infeasible points (p. 2). The paper then asks why not simply abandon the filter, and answers with four user-driven reasons (pp. 3–4):
  1. MADS-EB needs a feasible start, and the aircraft planform problem [2] had none (p. 3).
  2. GPS-filter users valued the sensitivity information about constraints, which EB does not provide (p. 3).
  3. Industrial codes fail, return Boolean feasibility, or are undefined outside X (pp. 3–4).
  4. Allowing violations may save evaluations when the domain is disjoint or the boundary erratic (p. 4).

  The problem is min f over Ω = {x ∈ X : c_j(x) ≤ 0}, with f, c_j : X → ℝ ∪ {∞}. "closed" constraints (defining X) must always hold, and "open" constraints need hold only at the solution (p. 2).
  - Correction for SKILL.md: its Signature Work Anatomy row *Why then* says NOMAD 3 (2008) needed a default treatment for relaxable constraints, and marks this as inference. The paper does not state this. Its stated motivations are the four user situations above (pp. 3–4). NOMAD appears only as the host of the STYRENE code [12] (p. 31).
  - Transferable technique: pose *why not just keep the existing method?* explicitly in the introduction and answer it with a numbered list of concrete user situations (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Aggregate the open-constraint violations into h(x) = Σ_j max(c_j(x), 0)², with h = ∞ outside X (p. 5). Impose a barrier h^max_k that never increases (p. 5).
  - *Two incumbent sets.* F_k holds the best feasible points. I_k holds the best-f points among the undominated infeasible points with 0 < h ≤ h^max_k, where dominance is filter-style (Defs. 2.1–2.3, p. 6). The Poll is done around one or both incumbents (Def. 2.6, p. 9).
  - *Iteration types* (pp. 9–12). Dominating: a point dominates an incumbent. Improving: not dominating, but some infeasible point has 0 < h < h^I_k. Unsuccessful: neither. The mesh is updated by type (eq. 5, p. 10–11), and the barrier becomes h^max_{k+1} = max{h(y) : h(y) < h^I_k} after an improving iteration and h^I_k otherwise (eq. 6, p. 12).
  - *Frame-center trigger ρ.* If f^F_k − ρ > f^I_k, the infeasible incumbent becomes the primary poll center (Def. 2.7, p. 13), an idea credited to Paul Frank of Boeing (footnote 2, p. 14).
  - What is changed: infeasible points in X \ Ω are allowed; "We do not use a filter, but we do use the notion of dominance fundamental to filters" (p. 3). What is kept: the mesh, frames, the free Search ("any number of trial points… as long as these points belong to the mesh", p. 8) and LTMADS directions (p. 12).
  - *Nesting.* With a feasible point in V₀ and h^max_0 = 0, the algorithm "reduces to MADS-EB" (p. 12).
  - Options: a user may treat a constraint as open until it is satisfied, then move it to the closed set (p. 3), and a soft-constraint threshold h^min can be set (remark, p. 12). Squared violations are preferred because they pass on any smoothness of the constraints to h (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: when relaxing a hard rule (reject every infeasible point), introduce a monotone threshold whose extreme value recovers the old rule. The old method becomes a special case, and the new parameter has an obvious safe default.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - A1: some x₀ ∈ V₀ lies in X with f(x₀) < ∞ and h(x₀) < h^max_0.
  - A2: all trial points lie in a bounded set, which the paper calls easy to ensure by making bounds closed constraints (pp. 5, 14).
  - A3: an external constraint qualification. For every hypertangent v to Ω at x̂, h°(x; v) < 0 for all infeasible x ∈ X near x̂ (pp. 5, 21).
  - Further conditions in the theorems: local Lipschitz continuity of f or h near the limit, a nonempty hypertangent cone, and a dense set of refining directions (pp. 16–18, 24).

  The Clarke derivative is Jahn's generalization for f undefined outside X (p. 15). A3 is not assumed up front. It is introduced after a counterexample (pp. 18–21), related to smooth constraint qualifications (Thm. 3.10, pp. 21–22), and footnoted as "sufficient, but not necessary" (p. 24). Reader's notes:
  - The hierarchy says density "is ensured by the LTMADS way of defining the polling directions" (p. 24). It omits the "with probability 1" qualifier the paper itself states on p. 12, the gap S006 closes.
  - Lemma 3.11's proof asserts that "Assumption A3 ensures that h is Lipschitz continuous on I" (p. 22). A3 only constrains the sign of h°, so Lipschitz continuity is implicitly presupposed by the use of the Clarke derivative.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The analysis is split into three cases (pp. 15–16).
  - *Feasible refining subsequence* (Theorem 3.3, pp. 16–17). The standard MADS argument: t_k = Δ^m_k‖d_k‖, y_k = x_k + t_k(d_k/‖d_k‖ − v), a Lipschitz bound, and the limsup split of Lemma 3.2. The nonnegative quotient comes from x_k being a feasible *minimal* frame center (an unsuccessful iteration). Corollary 3.4 extends this to dense directions, using the fact that the closure of the hypertangent cone is the Clarke tangent cone (p. 17).
  - *Infeasible refining subsequence, results on h* (Theorem 3.5, p. 18). A mirror proof with (h, X) in place of (f, Ω), after dispatching the trivial case h(x̂) = 0.
  - *Counterexample, Example 3.7* (pp. 19–21). A 1-D problem with f(x) = x and a constraint c that is differentiable with a Lipschitz derivative. c is built from a quintic p (p(1) = 8, p(4/3) = 4, p(2) = 16, zero slopes there) rescaled over dyadic intervals, so that 4×2^{−ℓ} is the unique minimizer of c on [3×2^{−ℓ}, 5×2^{−ℓ}] (p. 19). The MADS instance is "tailored to produce a bad limit point": its Search, at iterations 3ℓ+2 only, proposes t_ℓ = x_k − 2^{ℓ+5}Δ^m_k. Prop. 3.8 proves by induction that the frame centers are 4×2^{−ℓ}, all infeasible, converging to 0. There every hypertangent direction is a descent direction (pp. 20–21). A3 fails because h′(7×2^{−ℓ}; −1) > 0 (p. 21).
  - *Relating A3 to smooth constraint qualifications* (Theorem 3.10, pp. 21–22). It uses h°(x; v) = 2vᵀC′(x)ᵀW(x)C(x), with W diagonal 0/1 (eq. 10, p. 22). The resulting condition is weaker than ∇c_i ≠ 0 on the active set, which is weaker than the SQP full-rank assumption (p. 21).
  - *Lemma 3.11* (pp. 22–23): h strictly decreases along directions near v, by contradiction through Clarke's mean-value theorem (Thm. 2.3.7 of [10]).
  - *Theorem 3.12* (p. 23). At unsuccessful iterations, a poll point with lower h cannot be infeasible, since it would make the iteration improving or dominating. It must therefore be feasible, which yields infinitely many feasible points and a feasible refining subsequence. The worked illustration, min (x+π)² subject to x ≤ 0 from x₀ = 1, shows that the conclusion applies to the feasible limit −π, not to the infeasible limit 0 (p. 23).
  - *Hierarchy* (§3.5, pp. 24–25). Outcomes (i)–(x): unbounded iterates; a refined point exists; Clarke stationarity for h over X; Clarke stationarity for f over Ω; Clarke KKT under strict differentiability; contingent stationarity under regularity; contingent KKT. The proofs of (v)–(x) are omitted as "practically identical" to [6] (p. 25). No rates.
  - Transferable technique: **adversarial Search counterexample**. Since the theory must hold for *every* Search, choose a legal but adversarial Search step to drive a valid instance of your own algorithm to a bad limit. That shows which extra hypothesis is needed (pp. 19–21).
  - Transferable technique: when a new constraint qualification is needed, prove a theorem placing it relative to the standard smooth qualifications, so readers can see it is weaker (Theorem 3.10, p. 21).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Methods* (p. 25). GPS-EB, GPS-filter, LTMADS-EB, and LTMADS-PB with one secondary poll direction (−b(ℓ)) or two (±b(ℓ)). Default parameters throughout. LTMADS results are "the average of five distinct calls with different random seeds".
  - *Protocol* (p. 25). Every problem is run from a feasible and from an infeasible start. EB runs from an infeasible start are two-phase: minimize h until h = 0, then minimize f, with both phases' evaluations counted. Plots show the incumbent feasible f against the number of evaluations. The budget is 600n evaluations (pp. 26, 29).
  - *Convex problem: min Σx_i subject to Σx_i² ≤ 3n,* n = 5, 10, 20, 50 (p. 26). All LTMADS runs reach the global minimizer, and GPS stalls on the boundary (p. 27).   - *Nonconvex problem: min x_n over Σ(x_i−1)² ≤ n² ≤ Σ(x_i+1)²* (pp. 28–29). GPS fails because its directions are fixed (p. 29). Table 1 (p. 31; cited as Table 4.2 on p. 29): at n = 50, EB reaches feasibility in 1004.0 evaluations with f = −22.2, PB in 2401.9 with f = −46.0.   - *STYRENE* (p. 31). 8 variables, "4 closed yes-no constraints and 7 open constraints", 1–3 s per call, and it "still may fail to return a value". The C++ code is "freely available [12] and can be used by designers of other derivative-free methods". Explicit feasible and infeasible starting points are listed.   - Transferable technique: sweep the new parameter over orders of magnitude on the hardest instance, then state a default rule in one sentence (p. 28).
