**What changed from 2017 to 2026.** The 2017 structure is the one the reprinted first-edition preface describes (pp. 15–17). The comparison is the reader's.
- *Benchmarking moved from the back of the book into its foundations.* In 2017, comparing optimization methods was an appendix, described as a very brief overview of good practices plus one large project (pp. 16–17). In 2026 it is Chapter 4 of Part 1, after only the naive algorithms of Chapter 3 and before the heuristic, direct-search and model-based parts (pp. 15, 18–19). Three chapters that introduce a problem class also gain a benchmarking section: constrained (§10.6), surrogate-based (§15.5) and biobjective (§16.4) (p. 9).
- *Constraints moved into the direct-search core.* In 2017, *Variables and Constraints* was Ch. 12, in Part 5 (Extensions and Refinements; p. 16). In 2026 it is Ch. 10, the last chapter of Part 3 (Direct Search Methods; p. 20). The 2026 preface does not mention this move.
- *Model quality was separated from model construction.* The single 2017 model-building chapter (Ch. 9, p. 16) became two: Ch. 11 on model quality, in the language of order-N accuracy, and Ch. 12 on generalised simplex gradients and Hessians built with pseudo-inverses. The authors say that as a result Chapters 13 and 14 are simpler and more flexible (p. 10).
- *Other changes the authors list* (pp. 9–10): Chapters 15 and 16 roughly doubled in size. A new appendix of real BBO test problems comes with software and feeds the end-of-chapter exercises. Each chapter has at least 20 exercises, with answers to every fourth. Intensification and diversification are woven through the book. Notation was updated (ζ now denotes a random integer). New and coloured figures were added.
- *Stale cross-references.* The reprinted 2017 preface keeps its 2017 chapter numbers (GA = Ch. 4, MADS = Ch. 8, surrogates = Ch. 13, biobjective = Ch. 14, comparing methods = appendix; pp. 15–17). Dennis's foreword still puts the McKinnon example in Chapter 5 (p. 8); it is now §6.4 (p. 19). A reader who follows the prefaces' chapter numbers into the 2026 book will land one or two chapters early.

**The authors' own syllabi** (a 13-week course with three hours per week; p. 11). A direct-search course covers chapters 1–2–3–4, 6, 7–8–9–10, 11–12 and 15–16. A model-based course covers 1–2–3–4, 5–6, 7–8–10 and 11–12–13–14. A shorter practical course covers 1–(2)–3, then the appendix problems, then 5–(6)–(7)–8–9–(11)–13, and ends with 4; brackets mean minimal coverage. Chapter 4 is in all three. The practical course puts the real problems before the algorithms and benchmarking last. A comprehensive course can take up to six or seven months (p. 10).

- **D1 Problem & entry** (the authors' voice unless marked):
  - *2017.* The book states one goal and one belief (p. 13). The goal is Quote 1: a clear grasp of the foundations, in order to push derivative-free and blackbox optimization into the mainstream of nonlinear optimization. The belief is that these foundations are now mature enough to teach at senior undergraduate level. The authors call the field one of the most rapidly expanding in nonlinear optimization research and one of the most important for future real-world applications (p. 13).
  - *Two audiences* (pp. 13–14). Newcomers get the foundations, not the state of the art, which the authors leave to research papers. They also get an elementary introduction to the parts of nonsmooth analysis used to design and analyse the methods, which the authors say is not covered often enough outside the nonsmooth community, and rigorous convergence theory (p. 13). Practitioners are those whose problems gradient-based methods cannot approach. The authors say many of them fell back on ad hoc methods, with a plethora of papers publishing incremental improvements to solution quality. Against this they offer methods with proven convergence, mathematically supported stopping criteria and a practical track record, which are still easy to use (p. 14).
  - *2026.* The entry is teaching effectiveness: significantly expanded exercises, updated notation and a modular course design, for classroom use or self-study (p. 9).
  - *Dennis's voice* (foreword, written for 2017). BBO matters because companies will not pay to rewrite legacy simulation codes for adjoints or automatic differentiation. He reports that some engineers distrust gradient-based optima as too dependent on the starting point, and adds that he would still use derivatives if he had them (p. 6). One of his problems cost three weeks on four SGI processors per function value (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: state the goal and the belief that makes it feasible as two separate sentences, and name each audience together with what it gets and what it is not given (the state of the art is left to papers) (pp. 13–14).
- **D2 Core idea (book design)**:
  - *Architecture*: see the map above. Heuristics are set apart in their own part, which can be skipped. The authors say GA and Nelder–Mead do not meet their definition of a DFO method, and still call them popular and effective (p. 15). Direct search and model-based methods are the two major categories. Direct search acts on function values without approximating derivatives and can therefore be effective in BBO even when the objective and constraints are nonsmooth (p. 15). Model-based methods are effective when the objective is expected to be smooth, and numerical analysis shows that gradient-based algorithms still work with approximated gradients (p. 16).
  - *Pedagogy*: algorithms are presented not as they were discovered but as hindsight makes the authors wish they had been (Quote 2, p. 14). The naive algorithms of Chapter 3 are called generally ineffective, yet they are the building blocks for many other methods and their analyses (p. 15). In 2017, the MADS chapter advances from the GPS chapter and its convergence analysis relies on the material of Section 6.5 (p. 16), probably the constrained optimality conditions (inference: 2017 §6.5 matched to 2026 §7.5 by title, p. 19).
  - *Modularity*: each chapter is about three hours of lecture (p. 17). Parts are designed to be skipped without loss (pp. 15–16). A preparation tree and three sample syllabi help instructors build shorter courses (pp. 10–11).
  - *One real blackbox across the algorithm chapters*: RHEOLOGY experiments appear in §8.5 (GPS), §9.6 (MADS), §13.5 (model-based descent), §14.5 (trust region) and §15.4 (surrogates). Their titles run "Numerical", "Further", "Additional", "More" and "Final" experiments (pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable technique: teach (or write up) a family of methods in reconstructed order. Give the naive ancestor with its convergence proof first, then each successor as a modification of it, and keep the real history in separate end-of-part remarks (pp. 14–15, 17).
- **D3 Assumptions**: The expected background is multivariate calculus, linear algebra and proof techniques (contraposition, induction, contradiction), usually from the first two years of a quantitative degree (pp. 14–15). The front matter refers to "our definition" of a DFO method (p. 15) but does not give it; it is probably in §1.2 (inference). *Dennis's voice*: a theorem is a proof of correctness on the class of problems that satisfies its hypotheses. The hypotheses need not be checked before the algorithm is used: we do not test a matrix for positive definiteness before attempting a Cholesky factorization, and if the factorization fails, then we know (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof content** (section titles only; no proof is visible):
  - Seven algorithm chapters have a convergence section by title: coordinate search (§3.5), GA (§5.6), Nelder–Mead (§6.3), GPS (§8.4), MADS (§9.4), model-based descent (§13.4, after §13.3 *The MBD Algorithm Has Successful Iterations*) and the model-based trust region (§14.4) (pp. 18–21). Three chapters that present an algorithm show none in their titles: Ch. 10 (progressive barrier, §10.5), Ch. 15 (surrogate management framework, §15.3) and Ch. 16 (§16.3 *Biobjective Optimization Algorithm*) (pp. 20–21).
  - The nonsmooth toolkit is taught as a chapter: positive bases, descent directions and optimality conditions (§7.1–7.5), then dense sets of polling directions for MADS (§9.5) (pp. 19–20).
  - A counterexample gets its own section: the McKinnon example (§6.4, p. 19). In Dennis's voice, it shows that pure Nelder–Mead can fail analytically, where everyone knew it could fail numerically; a restart with a fresh simplex remains the practical remedy (p. 8).
  - Model accuracy is taught in the language of order-N accuracy (§11.2), and gradients and Hessians are approximated by generalised simplex gradients and Hessians built with pseudo-inverses (§12.3–12.5). The authors call the result mathematically elegant and easy to implement (p. 10).
  - No title mentions rates, worst-case complexity or probabilistic analysis (pp. 18–22).
  - Transferable technique: put the counterexample that bounds a popular method in its own section, next to that method's convergence study (§6.3–6.4, p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments & test material**:
  - Appendix A holds five real BBO problems with software by Le Digabel. They are used throughout the book, especially in the end-of-chapter exercises (pp. 9, 12, 21). C. Tribes supplied numerical logs for many exercises (p. 12). One of the five, RHEOLOGY, runs through five algorithm chapters (pp. 20–21).
  - In 2017, computer exercises were tagged with a box and harder ones with a plus (p. 17), and C. Poissant checked that every exercise was doable (p. 14). In 2026, answers are given to every fourth exercise (p. 9).
  - Class testing: the winter 2017 classes in Kelowna and Montréal (p. 14) and the 2025 DFO class of Polytechnique Montréal (p. 12).
  - The front matter reports no numerical result.
  - *Dennis's voice*: reliability matters as well as the number of expensive calls. One industrial group adopted MADS because it never took more than 15 minutes on a recurring problem. Their own interior-point code sometimes took milliseconds but sometimes failed, and a failure was expensive (p. 7). In one project, the objective routine returned 2.6 whenever it failed, a value well inside the meaningful range, which cost the team an hour of puzzlement. In another, hidden constraints were violated at about two-thirds of the trial points (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: ship the real test problems with the teaching material, and run one of them through the GPS, MADS, model-based and surrogate chapters (five chapters) so that students compare methods on the same blackbox (pp. 9, 20–21).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The book positions itself against research papers (the state of the art belongs there, p. 13), against ad hoc practice (p. 14), and as a bridge to the nonsmooth-analysis community (p. 13).
  - The 2026 preface closes by honouring Powell and Dennis as pioneers of the field. It quotes Powell's 1994 report of Dennis's description of direct search: finding the deepest point of a muddy lake with a boat and a plumb line when every sounding has a price, with widely spaced measurements to smooth out high-frequency variations (p. 12).
  - Contributors named: Le Digabel (helped design the 2017 table of contents; wrote the 2026 appendix and its software repository), Tribes (numerical examples in 2017, exercise logs in 2026), A. Custódio (targeted feedback on the biobjective chapter), Y. Lucet, C. Poissant, C. Planiden and V. Rochon Montplaisir (pp. 12, 14). Hare's side of the origin story is a UBC sabbatical year spent largely at GERAD (p. 14).
  - *Dennis's voice*: the authors avoided the trap of an encyclopedic volume (p. 7). Hare, who came to the area later, has a fresher eye for what novices find hard (p. 7). The book presents both the theory and the practical details of its algorithms, where most books do one or the other (p. 8). He calls Audet a major research contributor with some truly amazing applications, his favourite being the placement of Québec snowfall sensors, with many nonnumerical constraints (p. 7). Dennis was Audet's postdoctoral mentor and co-author (SKILL.md, Academic Lineage), so the foreword is not independent.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The prefaces are short, first-person plural, and organised as bulleted lists of named changes, each tied to a chapter (pp. 9–10).
  - The authors hedge their own history: the Remarks are only high-level and by no means a complete historical overview (p. 17).
  - Humour: the families are thanked for understanding the importance of properly optimized octagonal whiskey glasses (p. 14). This may be a nod to the small-octagon side line (inference).
  - Carelessness in reprinted and new text: the stale chapter numbers above; "Backgound" in the preparation tree (p. 11); "supporting out idiosyncrasies" (p. 12).
  - Transferable technique: in a new edition or a revised paper, list the changes as named bullets, each giving the chapter it touches and the reason for it (pp. 9–10).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: teaching consolidation. Benchmarking is promoted into the foundations, real test problems come with software, the model-based part is reorganised, and exercises are expanded (pp. 9–10).
  - *Limits the authors state*: the book is not the state of the art (p. 13); its methods are not the only possible approaches to real-world problems (p. 14); the Remarks are high-level (p. 17).
  - *Limits of this card* (reader): the front matter shows only titles, and nothing here verifies what any chapter says. The 16 end-of-chapter projects announced in the publisher's abstract (D005) do not appear as sections. The front matter mentions only that exercises and projects can be selected for students (p. 11), and the 2017 appendix contained one large project (p. 17). The author's page counts (16+1 chapters, about 425 pages; D005) agree with the table of contents (16 chapters plus one appendix; the index starts at book p. 423; p. 22).

### S077 · Nonlinear programming by mesh adaptive direct searches (report version, 2005, 10.21236/ada444692; published in SIAG/Optimization Views-and-News 17(1):2–11, 2006, according to the GERAD page cited on card `a2-01.md`, not stated in the text)
**Role**: core · **Read**: full, all 17 pages including the 47-entry reference list (pp. 13–17). · **Coauthors**: M.A. Abramson (Air Force Institute of Technology), J.E. Dennis Jr. (Rice University) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: The text read is a 17-page report version; p. 1 carries no report number or venue. Card `a2-01.md` identifies the open PDF as Rice CAAM TR05-13, and works.json lists S097 as a duplicate. The paper was written for a multi-issue special feature on derivative-free optimization: it calls itself one of several papers devoted to DFO (p. 2) and refers to "these special issues" (pp. 8, 12). It sits between MADS, still cited as to appear in SIAM J. Optim. ([13], p. 14), and the progressive barrier (2009): constraints here are still treated by the filter (p. 3). Abramson's 2002 Rice thesis was co-chaired by Audet (S213). The funding footnote lists FCAR and NSERC grants for Audet, LANL/DOE and LBNL/DOE for Dennis, and AFOSR, Boeing and ExxonMobil for both (p. 1). This matches the AFOSR/Boeing/ExxonMobil period of the trajectory table [D016]. The abstract says MADS is implemented in the direct search part of the MathWorks MATLAB GADS toolbox (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - *Scope by exclusion*: for the authors, derivative-free optimization excludes methods that use standard finite-difference derivatives inside a Newton or SQP framework. Those methods are called well established and valuable, to be used in place of really derivative-free methods whenever one can; the authors' target problems are simply not amenable to them (p. 2).
  - *Not a survey*: the paper introduces the ideas behind MADS and summarizes the authors' own work (pp. 1–2).
  - *Where the topic came from*: from users (Quote 2, p. 2). The authors add that the area also gave them all the theoretical challenge they could wish for. They argue that DFO will and should form closer ties between computational optimization and nonsmooth analysis, and that nonsmooth analysis should be in any curriculum meant to train nonlinear optimization researchers (p. 2).
  - *Problem*: minimize f over Ω = {x ∈ X : c_j(x) ≤ 0, j ∈ J}, with f and the c_j allowed to take the value +∞. No differentiability is assumed to run the algorithms. The strength of the optimality result at a limit point depends on the local smoothness there and on the tangent cone to Ω (p. 2).
  - *Three kinds of constraint behaviour* (pp. 2–3). X is the set where f and C can be evaluated at all. The user only supplies a routine that says whether x is in X; the authors call these "yes/no" or oracular constraints. Every trial point and the initial point must satisfy them. C(x) ≤ 0 needs to hold only at the solution, because users often want to know how much optimality a slight relaxation would buy; these *open constraints* are treated by the authors' modification of the filter method. Finally, even for x in Ω the evaluation may fail, often after as long as a successful run; this is modelled as f(x) = +∞, as in MDO problems that link legacy PDE solvers at run time (pp. 2–3).
  - *Pathologies*: an expensive simulation computes ancillary variables, so evaluations must be used sparingly. The outputs are likely to have few correct digits, so derivatives probably do not exist everywhere, and difference quotients would not give derivative approximations fit for derivative-based algorithms (p. 3).
  - *The "global optimizer" request*: no algorithm can guarantee it for these problems. The authors think their local methods, with some attention to globality, give equally useful solutions because users also want robustness: a robust optimizer lies in a broad valley, which is easier to find than a narrow, deeper basin (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: before choosing an algorithm, ask of each constraint who can check it and when. A yes/no membership routine that must pass before the simulation is run, a quantifiable violation that must hold only at the solution, and a run that fails and is recorded as +∞ are three different objects (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Meshes*: MADS generates iterates on a tower of underlying meshes and adapts their fineness; the full mesh is never generated (p. 4). The mesh can be dropped, as in the Price–Coope frame methods, but then a sufficient-decrease condition replaces simple decrease, and sufficient decrease is less simple without derivatives than a Goldstein–Armijo backtrack. The authors suspect that using a mesh or not is a matter of taste rather than effectiveness, and admit they have no experience without the mesh on real problems (p. 4).
  - *Origin*: MADS was discovered as a direct result of weaknesses of GPS on nonsmooth problems, exposed when the authors analysed GPS with nonsmooth analysis (pp. 4, 7).
  - *Search and Poll*: each iteration has two steps. If the Search finds no unfiltered point, the Poll runs; if the Poll fails too, the mesh is refined (p. 4). The Search is crucial in practice because it is flexible, and hard for the theory for the same reason (Quote 1, p. 4). Any hope of reaching a deeper basin than the starting one is concentrated in the Search (p. 4). The Poll is more rigidly defined; as the basis of the convergence analysis, it is where most research has been concentrated (pp. 4–5). That last sentence and the Quote 1 sentence are the two sentences the NOMAD user guide carries in its MADS introduction [S019 pp. 16–17], the source of the second half of the SKILL.md epigraph. The guide also reuses this section's description of meshes (S019 p. 16 vs S077 p. 4).
  - *Lineage of the Poll* (p. 5): Lewis and Torczon's observation that the Poll should use directions whose nonnegative combinations span the space (called simple but crucial); Coope and Price's frames, which drop the requirement that poll points be mesh neighbours; and MADS, proposed as a way to implement frames so that the directions used in infinitely many polls are dense in the tangent cone at the limit point.
  - *A menu of Search strategies, each with a verdict* (pp. 5–6):
    - An empty Search is reasonable when a local minimizer in the starting basin is enough.
    - Repeating the last successful poll direction seems reasonable, but others found it of limited value at best.
    - Random search had some success at the first iteration and wasted evaluations afterwards.
    - The best Search strategies in the authors' experience use surrogates: simplified-physics simulations or surfaces fitted to a space-filling design. The word *surrogate* is chosen over *approximation* so as not to imply any requirement on how well it approximates (p. 5).
    - Boeing's Design Explorer fits DACE models to orthogonal-array data, runs a global Newton SQP on the surrogate problem, checks the candidates with the true functions and recalibrates (pp. 5–6).
    - Marsden runs an evolutionary algorithm on DACE surrogates as the Search, and checks membership in X with a cheaper turbulence model before running the accurate simulation (p. 6).
    - Audet and Orban tune a trust-region method with MADS, using the same method on easy problems as the surrogate (p. 6).
  - *The Poll* (pp. 6–7): after a success, the mesh size is kept or increased. When both steps fail, the incumbent is a mesh local optimizer and the mesh size decreases (p. 6). GPS restricts poll directions to a finite set, and known examples [9] show GPS failing for that reason; MADS lets the union of normalized poll directions become dense in the unit sphere (p. 6). With a full gradient, all but one direction are ascent directions and can be skipped, so MADS becomes an approximate steepest descent with one evaluation per iteration; partial derivatives also cut poll evaluations without losing the convergence properties [6] (pp. 6–7).
  - *Evaluation order*: because MADS is opportunistic, the order of poll points matters. Dynamic polling (the last successful direction first) was useful on a chemical-engineering parameter-fit problem; ordering by surrogate value would be a wise choice when a surrogate exists; Custódio and Vicente order by the angle with the negative simplex gradient. The authors warn that none of these is guaranteed to reduce computing time in every case (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: keep an explicit menu of candidate free-step strategies, and for each one record when it pays and when it stops paying (random search: the first iteration only) (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The algorithms need no differentiability (p. 2). The bottom rung of the analysis assumes bounded iterates, which holds when the initial level set is bounded (p. 9). The top rung assumes strict differentiability and a nonempty, full-dimensional tangent cone (p. 9). The results assume that the directions used infinitely often are dense in the hypertangent cone; the authors achieve this by choosing positive spanning directions at random, so most results hold with probability one (p. 10). The justification for stating results conditionally: the functions come from blackbox codes and their smoothness cannot be measured (pp. 8–9). Two admissions: a constraint-violation function still assigns relative weights to the constraints even though a filter needs fewer parameters than a penalty (p. 3); outputs can be noisy, with repeated evaluations at the same x giving slightly different values (p. 8), but no noise model is given.
  - Transferable technique: when the smoothness of a blackbox cannot be measured, state results conditionally on local smoothness at the limit point, one rung per smoothness level, instead of as a global assumption (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques** (described, not proved):
  - A hierarchy by smoothness (pp. 9–10). Bottom: bounded iterates give a limit x̂ of mesh local optimizers on meshes that become infinitely fine. Middle: for locally Lipschitz f, the Clarke generalized directional derivative satisfies f°(x̂; d) ≥ 0 for d in the hypertangent cone, the Clarke tangent cone or the contingent cone, depending on smoothness. Top: with strict differentiability and a full-dimensional tangent cone, f′(x̂; d) ≥ 0 on the tangent cone, i.e. no feasible strict descent direction, and ∇f(x̂) = 0 when unconstrained. Second order [5]: if f is twice strictly differentiable near x̂, a second-order Clarke-KKT necessary condition holds through a generalized Hessian; with further assumptions a sufficient condition holds, so MADS converges to a local minimizer.
  - *Tool import*: the authors say they turned to the nonsmooth community and found in Clarke's calculus exactly the tool they needed, because directional derivatives are undefined for nonsmooth functions (p. 9).
  - *The mesh as proof device*: keeping the mesh allows simple decrease instead of sufficient decrease (p. 4) (cf. catalog P2).
  - *Density by randomization*, hence probability-one results (p. 10).
  - *Counterexamples*: only cited. The GPS examples of [9] (Audet's tightness paper) show why fixed directions fall short (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. Practice claims rest on citations: excellent computational results [15, 16, 41] (p. 5); a very successful surrogate management framework [10] (p. 6); dynamic polling useful on the parameter-fit problem [13, 33] (p. 7); the thermal insulation problem solved with mixed-variable pattern search [36, 3] (p. 11). Evaluation costs range from minutes to weeks (p. 8). In one of the authors' projects [18], evaluations failed roughly twice in every three calls (p. 8). The multidisciplinary analysis behind these failures is a large nonlinear system solved by successive substitution or nonlinear Gauss–Seidel, which depends on the block order and is apt to fail (p. 8). Reader note: the claims that the authors were the first to solve such problems (p. 8) and that MADS gives excellent computational results (p. 5) are not supported by data inside this paper.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Predecessors are credited point by point: Lewis–Torczon (positive spanning), Coope–Price (frames, mesh local optimizer), Price–Coope (mesh-free frames, treated neutrally), Fletcher–Leyffer (filter), Kelley (the term hidden constraints), Custódio–Vicente (simplex gradients, with the caveat that their convergence needs local proximity to the limit point), Booker et al. (surrogate management framework), and Marsden (pp. 3–8).
  - Toward the other special-issue papers: the authors second everything the other authors will say about practical importance and use their space for points others may not make (p. 8).
  - Toward competing families: finite-difference methods are respected but out of scope (p. 2). Heuristic searches use too many evaluations for these expensive functions (p. 8). The weighted-sum approach to multiple objectives is not recommended [29] (p. 12).
  - Self-citation: 23 of the 47 references include at least one of the three authors (pp. 13–17).
  - Reference error (reader): entry [25] (Cramer, Dennis, Frank, Lewis, Shubin) carries the title, journal and pages of entry [22] (Coope and Price) (pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable technique: credit the predecessor's simple observation explicitly as crucial, then say in one sentence what your step adds to it (spanning sets → frames → dense directions) (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Conversational first-person plural throughout; the conclusion invites researchers into the field (p. 12).
  - Hedges are specific and say what experience is missing: the mesh as a matter of taste, with no experience without it on real problems (p. 4); others' doubts about repeating successful directions (p. 5); ordering strategies not always faster (p. 7); filter ideas not easily adapted to multiple objectives (p. 12). One claim is unhedged: that the authors were the first to solve such problems (p. 8).
  - Structure: the problem and its properties; what MADS is; why study these methods (practice, theory); what is still needed; conclusions. Section 4 is an itemized agenda drawn from users' requests (pp. 10–12) (catalog W15).
  - Theorems are described in words; the only displayed formulas are the problem and the top-rung conditions (pp. 2, 9).
  - Terminology is chosen on purpose: surrogate rather than approximation (p. 5), SEARCH–POLL methods (p. 4), open constraints, and yes/no or oracular constraints (pp. 2–3) (catalog W9).
  - Anecdotes carry the arguments: a user who asks for ten design variables and, after success, for a hundred is "a sure sign of progress" in the authors' words (p. 10); an oil field with 25.3 wells (p. 10); a client whose bound should be "as small as possible" (pp. 11–12).
  - Transferable technique: when you hedge, name the missing experience (no runs without the mesh on real problems) rather than hedging in general (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: exposition and an agenda. Software pointers: NOMADm, the NOMAD project, APPSPACK, SID-PSM, DACE and the MATLAB GADS toolbox (p. 12; refs [1, 24, 27, 38, 40, 42]).
  - *Stated limits*: no algorithm can guarantee a global optimum on these problems (p. 3); the mesh-free alternative is untried by the authors on real problems (p. 4); ordering strategies do not always save time (p. 7); like all direct search methods, the number of evaluations grows much faster than n (p. 12); adapting the filter to multiple objectives is not straightforward (p. 12); mixed variable programming is a very new field with open algorithmic and structural questions (p. 11).
  - *Future directions, framed as issues users raise after a first success* (p. 10):
    - *Categorical variables*: mixed variable programming. There is no general notion of local optimality, so the user must provide a set-valued neighbourhood function, and local optimality is defined with respect to it. In the thermal insulation example, neighbours are formed by swapping an insulator's material, adding an insulator with its heat intercept, or deleting one. The Poll step is augmented with the discrete neighbours. Convergence exists for GPS with bound, linear and (with a filter) nonlinear constraints (pp. 10–11).
    - *Multiple objectives*: real problems almost always have them. A bound constraint whose value the client wants as small as possible is really another objective, and clients want to know how much the objective would change if a constraint were relaxed or tightened. So the client needs a notion of the Pareto surface, not a weighted sum (pp. 11–12).
    - *More variables*: hundreds of variables where parallelism alone is not enough; the goal is direct search methods that slow the growth of evaluations with n (p. 12).
  - Reader note: SKILL.md's trajectory table lists PSD-MADS and BiMADS in 2003–2009, after this agenda (inference that the agenda was carried out; the later papers are not checked here).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split constraints by who can check them and when: a yes/no membership routine run before the simulation, a quantifiable violation needed only at the solution, and failed runs recorded as +∞; set the aggregate violation to +∞ outside the evaluable set (pp. 2–3).
- Put global aspirations in the free step, keep the Poll local, and record for each free-step strategy when it pays (pp. 4–6).
- Use a cheaper model to reject points that cannot be evaluated before paying for the accurate simulation (p. 6).
- Reorder poll points (last success first, by surrogate value, or by simplex-gradient angle) without touching the proof, and check whether it actually saves time (p. 7).
- If some derivatives are available, use them to skip poll directions that are ascent directions (pp. 6–7).
- When a client gives a bound, ask for its value. If the answer is "as small as possible", treat it as an objective and offer a trade-off curve (pp. 11–12).
- End an expository paper with the issues users raised after the first success, as the research agenda (pp. 10–12).
