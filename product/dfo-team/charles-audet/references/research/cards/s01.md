# Paper cards · batch s01 · Charles Audet · 2012–2016, NOMAD 3.x seen from the user side and the developer side (supplement)

**Batch intent**: The six entries share the NOMAD 3.x period. Two are applications where Audet is one author among domain specialists and NOMAD is a tool: hydro-generator fleet maintenance (S204) and bioequivalence trial design (S069). One is a modelling layer that wraps NOMAD for tuning algorithm parameters (OPAL, S059). One is an algorithm paper that changes the MADS mesh itself and then becomes a NOMAD default (dynamic scaling / XMesh, S061). One is the NOMAD 3.7.2 user guide (S202). The last is a recreational combinatorics paper with no blackbox at all (S152). Three questions:
- When Audet coauthors an application, does the paper follow the group's own manual? That is, are constraint semantics chosen (Method 2), is a feasible start used, and are budgets reported (Method 5)?
- How does a practical fix inside the Poll or mesh (XMesh) pay for itself in theory (Method 3) and reach the solver's defaults (Method 6)?
- What does his style look like when the output is a pure bound, with no smoothness ladder?

**Focus dimensions**: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line), D8. These are supplement short cards. The emphasis is on D2 (where the idea sits relative to the MADS backbone) and D5 (currency, baselines, failures reported).
**Material roles**: S204 supplement · S059 supplement · S152 supplement · S202 supplement (a duplicate of core S019, see its card) · S061 supplement · S069 supplement.

### S204 · Long-Term Fleet Maintenance of Hydroelectric Generators Using Proportional Hazard Model and Nonlinear Programming (Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2012–11, 2012; no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: H. Aoudjit (first author), M.-S. Ouali, all three at Polytechnique Montréal (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: This is a report of March 2012 (p. 1). Audet is third author on an industrial-engineering reliability study. The optimization side cites MADS (2006), the progressive barrier (2009), BiMADS (2008) and Le Digabel's NOMAD TOMS paper (p. 8, p. 15, p. 20). No funding statement or industrial partner is named in the text (pp. 1–20). **Read**: all 20 pages. The figures are captions only (pp. 7–13, 18), and some formulas lost their overbars in extraction (pp. 11–14).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Aging hydroelectric fleets are reaching end of life together, so a standard age-replacement policy cannot be applied unit by unit. One plant "could contain as many as 38 HGUs installed roughly the same year", and resource shortages follow (p. 7). The entry is applied: "a mathematical programming framework appears to be a suitable tool" (p. 7). The data are:
  - hourly production for 6 years from 3 plants (p. 9);
  - forced outages for 90 HGUs, where 23 of 119 components cause 80% of unavailability. Each unit is therefore reduced to three components: alternator, turbine and excitation system (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The paper chains statistics into NLP into solvers:
  - Kaplan–Meier estimates show increasing failure rates that vary by plant, which motivates a Cox proportional-hazards model with load as the only covariate (eqs. 1–6, pp. 9–11).
  - A +50% load scenario is obtained by the hazard ratio (eq. 7, p. 11).
  - Renewal–reward cost rates per component and per unit are computed (eqs. 8–12, pp. 12–13). The fleet objective is a separable sum over units with one decision variable τ_j per unit, so n = 90 (eqs. 15–16, p. 14).
  - The constraints are overhaul start, duration, amortization, crews and resources, bulk units, maximum capacity outage and disbursement smoothing (pp. 14–15).
  - Solvers are NOMAD (MADS) and Lindo API (SLP + GRG) (p. 15). Constraints are checked on an n×t 0/1 withdrawal matrix, and "−1 is returned whenever a constraint is met" (p. 15).
  - Separability is exploited on the evaluation side only: after a move, only the changed terms are recomputed (p. 14).
  - The operative design, visible only in §5, is a **solver relay**. Lindo finds a feasible plan, and NOMAD is restarted from it to polish (pp. 17–19).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A unit's components are all replaced together under the age-replacement policy, "for sake of simplicity" (p. 7, p. 16). The base hazard is constant and load is the only covariate, again "For the sake of simplicity" (p. 11). Reliability follows a Gompertz–Makeham law (p. 16). Costs are approximate ("Lack of precise maintenance costs records", p. 16); failure cost is 2× preventive for alternators and 1.5× for the other components (p. 16). The case constraints are 38-week withdrawals, amortization ≥ [50, 50, 30] years, ≤ 700 MW out per year, ≤ 3 crews, ≤ 2 cranes per plant and ≤ 250 M$ per year (p. 16). The paper does not say whether NOMAD treated these constraints with EB or PB (pp. 15–19).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no convergence or optimality analysis. The analytic content is the classical renewal–reward cost rate and the optimal-age condition taken from Barlow et al. (eqs. 8–11, p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: 90 HGUs, 6 plants, 50 years, one run per setting, no seeds or profiles, on a single workstation (p. 16):
  - Problem I, from the infeasible 50-year plan (crews 19 vs ≤ 3, outage 1001 vs ≤ 700 MW; Table 1, p. 17): NOMAD finds no feasible point in 10⁴ evaluations (3.53 min), nor in a second, larger run whose count lost its exponent in extraction ("5105", 9.23 min) (p. 17). Lindo returns "status 5", feasible but not optimal (p. 17). NOMAD restarted from Lindo's plan lowers J from 7.709 to 7.566 k$/h with 50,000 evaluations in 33 min, most of the gain within the first 20,000 (p. 17).
  - Problem II, started from Problem I's best plan (p. 17): the solvers are launched "with no luck for NOMAD after 10000 evaluations" (p. 18). The text does not say whether "no luck" means no feasible point or no improvement. Lindo is reported at 17 min in the text (p. 18) but 6.17 in Table 2 (p. 19), and the NOMAD relay lowers J from 9.799 to 9.562 (p. 19).
  - Inconsistencies: "a series of 3 problems" is announced (p. 16), but only two are reported (pp. 17–19). The +50% scenario (p. 11, p. 17) is later described as "doubling the load" (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an application framework plus two reliability indices (AURI and AFRI) for reading plans (p. 16, p. 19). Under +50% load, withdrawals move about 10 years earlier: 66 → 55 years in Table 3 (p. 19). The authors state that the method is "limited only by the ability of nonlinear solvers" (p. 20). Future work: the whole fleet of 300+ units, and more general policies and cost structures (p. 16, p. 20). NOMAD's failure to reach feasibility is reported but not analysed or remedied (pp. 17–18).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (by absence): the constraints are quantifiable (violation amounts appear in Table 1, p. 17), and x0 is infeasible (p. 16). Yet the paper never states an EB/PB choice, and it tries no LH search or PB switch when NOMAD stalls (pp. 17–18). The group's own manual lists the remedies *Difficult constraint → Try PB instead of EB* and *No initial point → Add a LH search* (S202/S019 p. 74).
- Taste warning sign 5 ✅ evidence, from the application side: n = 90 variables (p. 14) is well above the ~20 variables for which the manual calls NOMAD efficient (S202/S019 p. 97). In Problem I, a derivative-based SLP/GRG code reached feasibility where NOMAD did not (p. 17). The paper does not draw this conclusion itself.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: evaluation counts and wall-clock time are reported (pp. 17–19), but there are single runs, no profiles, and the two solvers are chained rather than compared.
- new-pattern candidate: *feasibility-by-relay warm start*. Another solver supplies a feasible point, and direct search polishes from it (pp. 17–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) If a direct-search run cannot find feasibility from an infeasible start, hand it a feasible point from any other solver (even a local SLP/GRG code), then report the direct-search gain separately together with its evaluation cost (pp. 17–19).
- (D8) Report decision-level indicators next to the objective (here average reliability at withdrawal, AURI, and of the remaining fleet, AFRI), so that plans with nearly equal cost can still be told apart (pp. 16–17, 19).

**Quotes**:
- "This is an ideal context to the so-called direct search algorithms which uses only functions evaluations to find the best solution." (p. 15)
- "This gain is however obtained at the cost of 50000 function evaluations in 33 minutes (Figure 9)." (p. 17)

### S059 · Optimization of algorithms with OPAL (Mathematical Programming Computation 2014, 10.1007/s12532-014-0067-x; text read: Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2012–08, March 2012)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: K.-C. Dang, D. Orban (p. 4) · **Position in trajectory**: The paper places itself in its own line of work (p. 8):
- Audet & Orban (2006): 4 trust-region parameters, 55 CUTEr problems, a surrogate built from a subset of the problems, 25% less CPU time;
- Audet et al. (2010a): first OPAL version, used to tune DFO;
- Audet et al. (2011): parallelism;
- this paper: extensions, implementation and interaction with other tools.

Dang's 2012 PhD thesis supplies the IPOPT case (p. 17, p. 18). Funding: NSERC Discovery 239436-05, AFOSR FA9550-09-1-0160 and ExxonMobil EM02562 (Audet), and NSERC 299010-04 (Orban) (p. 6). **Read**: all of pp. 1–19, references skimmed. This is the 2012 report; the 2014 published version was not read.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: "Parameter tuning has widespread applications because it addresses a widespread problem" (p. 8). The gap: other autotuning frameworks use heuristics, coordinate search, Nelder–Mead or random search, whereas OPAL uses a method "supported by a strong convergence theory" (p. 8). The entry is the choice of method plus the modelling burden. The target algorithm is treated non-intrusively as a blackbox and "does not require access to its source code" (p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *Formulation* (1): minimize ψ(p) subject to p ∈ P and ϕ(p) ∈ M. Atomic measures μ_i (iterations, CPU, memory) are separated from composite measures ψ and ϕ built from them (pp. 9–10).
  - *Gating*: whether p ∈ P "can be verified without launching the target algorithm". Points outside P are discarded before the expensive run (p. 10).
  - *OPAL layers* (pp. 13–15): a declaration file (algorithm, typed parameters with bounds, measures), a run file (read the parameters, run, write the measures) and an optimize file. The problem **structure** (the form of objective and constraints) is kept separate from the **data** (algorithm, problem list), "This separation allows the solution of closely-related problems with minimal change" (p. 14).
  - *Surrogates* are static: a subset of the test problems, looser stopping tolerances or a coarser Runge–Kutta discretization (p. 15). They are used either as a separate problem whose optimum seeds (1), or handed to NOMAD, which uses them to order points, perform local descents and identify candidates (p. 15).
  - *Categorical parameters* come with a user-given neighbour list (Listing 5, p. 16), and MADS polls them in a second set of trial points (p. 17).
  - *Parallelism* is available at three levels (p. 17).
  - *Clustering*: self-organizing-map clustering picks representative test problems as a surrogate (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Parameter types are real, integer, binary and categorical (p. 9). The target may stall, raise exceptions or fail, and "Failures are handled in a very natural manner" (p. 9). CPU-time objectives are "not a function", because reruns differ and "The gradient does not exist" (p. 10). Open domains such as h ∈ (0, +∞) are handled by the barrier (p. 13). EB is written ψ̂ = ψ if feasible, +∞ otherwise; relaxable constraints use PB or a filter, and hidden constraints are tolerated (p. 18). No convergence assumptions are stated beyond the summary of MADS.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no new theory. The paper summarizes the MADS ladder (p. 11):
  - limits of mesh local optimizers;
  - Clarke f° ≥ 0 along hypertangent directions if ψ is Lipschitz;
  - ∇ψ = 0, or contingent KKT, if ψ is strictly differentiable;
  - the claim that MADS "cannot stagnate at a strict local maximizer or at a saddle point", credited to the second-order and discontinuous extensions.

  Integer parameters are handled with an integer Δ, and the run stops when a unit step fails (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: There is no systematic benchmark and no competing tuner. The finite-difference toy (p. 13) and the ATLAS MFlops cloud (Fig. 1, pp. 11–12) are illustrations. In the IPOPT case, 6 real parameters over 730 CUTEr problems minimize total function and derivative evaluations under a no-failure constraint. The objective drops from 207,866 to 198,615 in 27h55m with up to 10 concurrent evaluations. The SOM surrogate with 41 problems takes 4h17m and lands "close to" the full solution, with no number given (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is at the software and modelling level: a Python layer with NOMAD as default solver (pp. 12–14, 17). It is described as "non intrusive" and suited to legacy or closed-source code (p. 18). Future work: robust parameter values and automatic identification of the most influential parameters (p. 18). Two small slips in the report:
  - Listing 4 encodes the no-failure constraint as (None, get_error, 0), i.e. ϕ ≤ 0, but the text says "ϕ(p) ≥ 0" (pp. 15–16);
  - Listing 4 line 15 filters an undefined `problems` rather than `cuter_unc` (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence: the algorithm is treated as a blackbox and tuned by MADS; the paper traces the 2006 → 2010 → 2011 → 2012 line (p. 8, p. 10).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence:
  - domain checks come before launch (p. 10);
  - failure is modelled as part of the problem (p. 9);
  - EB/PB/filter/hidden constraints are distinguished (p. 18);
  - *all problems solved* is an explicit constraint rather than a penalty in ψ (pp. 15–17).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: surrogates enter only as a seed-producing side problem or as ordering and candidate generation inside the solver (p. 15).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: OPAL is a front end whose value comes from NOMAD's features (variable types, groups, periodic variables, surrogates, constraint types) (pp. 17–18).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the convergence ladder is used as a **selling argument** against heuristic tuners (pp. 8, 11), but the paper proves nothing and runs no controlled comparison against the tuners it criticizes (pp. 8–18).
- new-pattern candidate: *Representativeness over size for test sets* (already raised in c08/S128). Here it is operational: SOM clustering reduces 730 problems to 41 representatives, used as a static surrogate (p. 17).
- new-pattern candidate: *Cheap-first evaluation gating* (already raised in c02/S019, c07): check P before the expensive run (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When the blackbox is *run an algorithm over a test set*, build a static surrogate by clustering the test set and keeping one representative per cluster. Report the wall-clock of the full and surrogate problems, and how close their solutions are (p. 17).
- (D2) Put *the tuned algorithm must still solve every problem* as a constraint (number of failures ≤ 0), not as a penalty inside the objective (pp. 15–17).
- (D2) In a modelling tool, separate problem *structure* (objective and constraint form) from *data* (target, instance list), so that variants such as another test set or a dropped constraint need one-line changes (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "By contrast, Opal uses a solid optimization method supported by a strong convergence theory, yielding solutions that are local minimizers in a meaningful sense." (p. 8)
- "It is important to stress that our framework does not assume correctness of the target algorithm, or even that it execute at all." (p. 9)

### S152 · Ordering 15 marbles with a three-way scale (The Mathematical Gazette 2014, 10.1017/s0025557200001340; text read: Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2013–05, January 2013)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (solo). Jean Guérin is thanked for proof-reading (p. 5). · **Position in trajectory**: This is a recreational side line with no DFO content. It is the seed of the 2025 working paper S177, which generalizes it to all n (see the c07 card of S177, where this paper is ref. [1]). **Read**: all 14 pages. The small graphs survive only as stray node labels (pp. 6–13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A scale with three trays ranks three marbles: heaviest, middle and lightest. What is the least number of weighings that always sorts 15 marbles? The paper claims at most 20 (abstract, p. 5). The entry is a twist on classical two-pan puzzles: "a similar but different type of question" (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Vocabulary: a *chain* is an ordered set; a *v-chain* is two chains sharing the heaviest marble (Def. 2.1, p. 6).
  - Workhorse: Lemma 2.2 sorts two chains plus a v-chain, p marbles in total, in p − 2 weighings (p. 6).
  - Design idea for small n: "end up with all the marbles being compared to a central one", then sort each side (p. 7). This gives 4 marbles in 3 weighings, 5 in 4, 6 in 5, 7 in 6, 8 in 8 and 9 in 9 (pp. 7–9). The 7-marble strategy is credited in a footnote to a puzzle website (p. 7).
  - The 15-marble bound is lowered in public steps: 25 (p. 7), 23 (p. 7), 22 (p. 8), then 20 via Lemmas 3.1–3.5 and Theorem 3.6 (pp. 9–13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Weights are distinct, one weighing returns the full order of three marbles, and the count is worst case (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - *Upper bounds*: induction on p for Lemma 2.2 (p. 6). Every other result is a case analysis on how many marbles fall on each side of a pivot, with "without any loss of generality" symmetry to halve the cases (pp. 7–13).
  - *Lower bound*: counting. Each weighing has six outcomes, so ⌈log₆(p!)⌉ weighings are needed. For p = 15, log₆(15!) > 15.57, which gives 16 (Prop. 4.1, p. 14).
  - Reference-label slips: "Lemma 2.6" and "Lemma 2.8" are invoked where Propositions 2.6 and 2.8 are meant (pp. 11–12). Prop. 2.7 states 8 weighings for 8 marbles (p. 8) but is later used with 7 weighings for 8 marbles that contain a known chain of length three (p. 11). The p. 9 remark on known chains justifies this, but the statement itself is not updated.
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The result is an upper bound of 20 against a counting lower bound of 16, with the gap left explicitly open (p. 14). The only future direction is to close that gap (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: instead of a smoothness ladder bounded by counterexamples, the claim is bracketed between a constructive upper bound and a counting lower bound. The unresolved gap is stated plainly (p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate: *exposition by successive bound improvement*. The paper shows a sequence of strategies (25 → 23 → 22 → 20), each unlocked by one new lemma, so the reader sees which lemma buys which weighing (pp. 7–8, 13). It is related to the *puzzle-to-family generalization* candidate raised for S177 (c07), of which this paper is the origin.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Before hunting for an optimal strategy, write the counting bound for the oracle: ⌈log_k(#outcomes)⌉, with k the number of answers per query. Then report your construction against it and state the gap (p. 14).
- (D7) Present the upper bound as a ladder of improving strategies. Each step names the new lemma that makes it possible (pp. 7–8, 13).

**Quotes**:
- "We do not know the answer to these questions." (p. 14)
- "Bridging the gap between these two bounds would give the optimal strategy." (p. 14)

### S061 · Dynamic scaling in the mesh adaptive direct search algorithm for blackbox optimization (Optimization and Engineering 2016, 10.1007/s11081-015-9283-0; text read: Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2014–16, March 2014)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Le Digabel, C. Tribes (p. 4) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the paper behind NOMAD's XMesh. It was tested in the "beta version 3.7.0" (p. 13) and announced for "release 3.7.0" (p. 17). The 3.7.2 guide lists it under 3.7.1 as a major change, with the default on (S202 p. 104, p. 80). Funding: NSERC 239436 (Audet), NSERC 418250 (Le Digabel) and AFOSR FA9550-12-1-0198 (p. 5). **Read**: all 21 pages, including the convergence appendix (pp. 18–20). Figures 1–6 are partly lost (pp. 12–18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: "Scaling variables can greatly affect the performance of an optimization algorithm", and MADS is no exception (p. 6). In default MADS a single mesh size parameter fixes the mesh, "Hence, with the Mads default form, the relative mesh aspect ratios remain invariant" (p. 6). The asymptotic theory does not help under a finite budget: "if the scaling of the variables is inappropriate, the progress made by the algorithm within a budget of evaluations might be marginal" (p. 6). The paper has a second aim: "to revisit the Mads method in order to ease its presentation and to reflect recent developments" (abstract, p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *What changes.* The scalar mesh size becomes a vector δ^k ∈ ℝⁿ₊, and the poll size becomes a vector Δ^k (pp. 6, 8). On success, the mesh is coarsened only along variables that moved significantly (p. 6).
  - *Definitions.* The mesh is M^k = V^k + {diag(δ^k) z : z ∈ ℤⁿ} (eq. 2, p. 8). The frame is a box of half-widths Δ^k_j (Def. 2.1, p. 8). The mesh size is δ^k_j = min{Δ⁰_j, Δ^k_j}² / (√n Δ⁰_j) (Def. 2.2, p. 9), so δ → 0 faster than Δ (p. 9).
  - *Update rules* (§3, pp. 11–12):
    - on failure, halve every Δ_j;
    - on success along d, increment r_j only if |d_j| > ‖d‖∞/n;
    - a catch-up rule raises any mesh index that falls below both −2 and twice the largest index (p. 12). This appears to be the mechanism promised for property (5) (pp. 9–10).
  - *Initial poll size* (§3.1, p. 11): (u_j − l_j)/10 with bounds; |x⁰_j − b_j|/10 with one bound; |x⁰_j|/10 without; otherwise 1.
  - *Poll directions.* A Householder basis from a random unit vector is stretched by diag(Δ^k) and rounded to the mesh. The directions are no longer orthogonal but are still called OrthoMADS, and they are "no more restrained to the use of the quasi-random Halton sequence" (§3.4, pp. 12–13).
  - *What is kept.* The Search is optional and "not necessary for the convergence analysis" (p. 8). The Poll still uses positive spanning sets on the mesh (p. 8).
  - *What is simplified.* The general direction matrix D of MADS is dropped (D = [−I I]) because "this flexibility has never been exploited in practice" (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - constraints are handled only by the extreme barrier f_Ω (p. 7);
  - x⁰ ∈ V⁰ ∩ Ω with f(x⁰) finite, and all iterates lie in a bounded set (p. 18);
  - each β_j > 1 has a rational square (p. 9). The paper defends this as necessary, not a proof convenience, citing the 2004 tightness counterexamples [4] (p. 19);
  - mechanism (5) links the component-wise and norm convergence of Δ (pp. 9–10);
  - Theorem A.5 needs dense refining directions, f Lipschitz near x*, and a nonempty hypertangent cone (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The classical two-step skeleton is re-proved for vector meshes (p. 18):
  - Lemma 2.3 writes Δ and δ through an integer mesh index r^k_j (p. 9).
  - Prop. 2.4 gives a coordinate-wise condition |d_j| ≤ β_j^{|r_j|} that keeps poll points inside the frame (p. 10).
  - Prop. A.1 (lim inf ‖δ^k‖ = 0) argues by contradiction. A bounded mesh index plus the rational β_j² puts every trial point on a fixed lattice x⁰ + (δ⁰_j/q_j^ρ)ℤ, and boundedness then leaves finitely many points. That contradicts infinitely many successes (p. 19).
  - Thm A.4 obtains Clarke f°(x*; v*) ≥ 0 from the lim-sup definition, by choosing y = x^k, t = ‖v^k‖ along refining directions (p. 20).
  - Thm A.5 turns density into Clarke stationarity (p. 20).
  - "The main results are identical to those of Mads [6] but the analysis differs" (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: NOMAD 3.7.0 beta, OrthoMADS 2n with opportunistic polling, quadratic models **disabled** in both variants "to clearly isolate the sole effect of dynamic scaling", and the same random-direction generator in both (pp. 13–14).
  - *Analytic set*: 212 Moré–Wild instances (n = 2–12; smooth, nonsmooth and two noisy variants) × 10 seeds = 4240 runs. Data profiles at τ = 10⁻³ are measured in groups of n+1 evaluations. Aniso dominates iso and solves about 10% more (p. 14; Fig. 3, p. 15).
  - *Supersonic business jet MDF problem*: simplified by fixing 18 of the 29 design variables (the structural thicknesses), leaving 11 by my arithmetic, and by changing the wing-area reference from 500 to 650 sq ft. The stated reason is that NOMAD then reaches solutions in a narrower objective range (p. 15). It has 46 EB constraints, a phase-one start, 100 random starts within ±10% and a budget of 20,000. Profiles use τ = 5·10⁻² (pp. 15–16). A paired histogram at 15,000 evaluations reads: aniso better 53/100, comparable 21/100 (within 250 lbs), iso better 26/100 (p. 16; Fig. 5, p. 17). An internal-state plot tracks the min/max mesh-size ratio over one run (Fig. 6, pp. 16–18).
  - Two extraction or typing points. The random direction formula reads as a scalar sum over a norm (p. 14); a vector is presumably meant. There is a typo: "34,263,54 lbs" (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The paper offers an algorithmic framework, an implementation and a simplified re-presentation of MADS with its own convergence proof (pp. 5, 17). Limits are stated as future work (pp. 17–18):
  - adapting the progressive barrier to the new mesh is the "Priority";
  - linking the anisotropic mesh sizes to variable sensitivities and to variable-decomposition methods [2, 8].

  The only competitor is the isotropic version of the same code (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the guarantee ladder is restated in the introduction (p. 6) and fully re-proved for the new mesh (pp. 18–20). A hypothesis (rational β²) is defended as necessary by pointing to the author's own counterexample paper (p. 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Scale per variable using the success direction: after a success along d, coarsen only the coordinates with |d_j| > ‖d‖∞/n. Add a catch-up rule so that no coordinate's step size falls too far behind the largest one (pp. 11–12).

### S069 · Optimal adaptive sequential designs for crossover bioequivalence studies (Pharmaceutical Statistics 2016, 10.1002/pst.1721; text read: Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2015–70, July 2015)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Xu (Merck, first author), C.E. DiLiberti, W.W. Hauck, T.H. Montague and A.F. Parr (GSK), D. Potvin, and D.J. Schuirmann (FDA) (p. 4) · **Position in trajectory**: The paper extends this group's earlier sequential designs, Potvin et al. 2008 [1] and Montague et al. 2012 [2] (p. 6). It was funded by the Product Quality Research Institute and carries an FDA disclaimer (p. 5). Audet is the only author with an optimization affiliation (GERAD; p. 4). The paper does not state each author's role, so the claim that he ran the NOMAD part is an inference from the optimization section (p. 12). **Read**: all 22 pages. Figures 1–8 are captions only (pp. 8, 13, 16–18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: How many subjects should a crossover bioequivalence (BE) study enrol when the intra-subject CV (ISCV) is uncertain, as it is for generic makers (p. 6)? The earlier designs B, C and D "were in some ways demonstrations of what is possible", had "no attempt to optimize their performance" and set no maximum sample size (p. 6). The entry is to optimize the designs, adding a futility stop and a cap on total size, separately over two ISCV ranges (10–30% and 30–55%). The ranges are chosen with the scaled-average-BE alternative in mind (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Two two-stage procedures, E and F, are flowcharted (pp. 7–8). Each has four design variables: α₁, α₂, n₁ (an integer) and the futility half-width f. The cap nmax is fixed at 42 or 180 rather than optimized (pp. 10, 12).
  - Each candidate design is simulated on a GMR × ISCV grid, e.g. 7 × 5 points for the low range (p. 9).
  - **Gate first**: type I error (at GMR 0.80) and power (at 0.95) are checked on their grid subsets. If any fails, "no simulations were done for the remaining points" (p. 10).
  - The objective averages an **asymmetric** cost over the grid: quadratic when the design needs more subjects than a single-stage design, linear when it needs fewer. This stops cheap grid points from hiding expensive ones (eq. 3, p. 12).
  - Importance sampling (40,000 simulations) estimates type I error and power from one set of runs under the alternative. It "failed to converge properly" for ISCV < 15%, where plain Monte Carlo (100,000) is used instead (p. 11).
  - The search is done by NOMAD/MADS: 4 bounded variables and 8 constraints on power and type I error (p. 12). "Unacceptable designs were assigned an extremely high cost value" (p. 12). The start is a design already feasible on all ISCVs (p. 12).
  - The paper argues that "The optimal design will be located on the edge" of the sawtooth feasibility boundary. It credits NOMAD, unlike Nelder–Mead, with the ability "to search effectively along this edge" (p. 12; Fig. 3, p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Log-differences are normal with variance 2σ² (p. 11). Power uses a formula adapted from Hauschke et al. (eq. 1, p. 11). By symmetry only GMR ≤ 1 is simulated (p. 9). n₁ ≥ 12, the smallest pivotal BE study FDA accepts (p. 10). The low-range cap of 42 comes from equating cost with the smallest practical scaled-average-BE study: 27 subjects × 3 periods = 81 administrations (p. 10). The 180 cap is "practical" (p. 10). The paper does not say whether the simulation seed was fixed across design variants (pp. 11–13).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no optimization theory. The statistical devices are:
  - the importance-sampling estimator with variance no larger than plain Monte Carlo (eq. 2, p. 11);
  - the active-constraint argument above (p. 12);
  - a fold-range argument explaining why n₁ over-powers the low end: single-stage sizes span 5.57-fold over 10–30% but 2.95-fold over 30–55% (pp. 15–16).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: About 100 design variants per optimization at about 3 min each, roughly 5 h on a 5–6 PC Linux cluster (p. 13). The optimized designs have α₁ ≈ 0.025, α₂ ≈ 0.035–0.036, and n₁ = 18 (low range) or 48 (high range). Maximum type I error is 0.050 and minimum power 0.80, i.e. both constraints are active (Table 1, p. 14). Average sample-size penalty versus single stage: +1.9 (low range) and −6.1/−6.7 (high range), against 4.8–12.2 and 9.5–16.3 for the earlier designs B/C/D (Tables 3–4, pp. 19–20). The optimizer is compared with nothing, and no restarts are reported.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The paper delivers four ready-to-use designs with a worked example (pp. 20–21). The limits are specific:
  - power falls below 0.80 when the true ISCV is above the design range (p. 19; Table 5, p. 20);
  - over-powering at the low end reflects a "fundamental limitation" of one sequential design spanning a wide ISCV range (p. 15);
  - the comparison is deliberately conservative, since the single-stage comparator gets no minimum n and no even-n rule (p. 15).

  Two anomalies: Table 5 lists 0.459 as F's type I error at ISCV 35% (p. 20), which contradicts the text's "the type I error rate is not inflated" (p. 19) and is presumably a typo. The MADS citation [16] points to the progressive-barrier paper (pp. 12, 22).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence (extended): here the tuned "algorithm" is a statistical decision procedure. Its four design parameters are optimized by MADS with a Monte-Carlo simulator as the blackbox (pp. 9–12).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the eight constraints are quantifiable simulation outputs (p. 12), yet they are enforced by "an extremely high cost value", i.e. an EB-style barrier, starting from a feasible design (p. 12). The paper describes them as NOMAD-handled constraints (p. 12) but does not name EB/PB. PB is not considered, even though the optimum lies on the constraint boundary (p. 12). The manual's advice would be PB for difficult constraints (S202 p. 74).
- Research Taste 1 ✅ evidence: a real, expensive and noisy blackbox, with an irregular feasibility edge that is shown and explained (pp. 12–13).
- new-pattern candidate: *Cheap-first evaluation gating* (already raised in c02/S019 and c07). Constraint subsets are simulated first, and the objective grid is skipped for infeasible variants (p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: *asymmetric aggregate over a scenario grid*. When the objective averages over scenarios, penalize losses quadratically and reward gains linearly, so that good scenarios cannot buy bad ones (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When each objective evaluation is a grid of simulations, evaluate the constraint-bearing grid points first and stop at the first violation. Only feasible candidates pay for the full grid (p. 10).
- (D2) For an objective that averages over scenarios, use an asymmetric per-scenario cost: quadratic above the reference, linear below (eq. 3, p. 12).
- (D5) Estimate the rare-event constraint (type I error) and the power from the same importance-sampled runs. Say where importance sampling fails and what replaces it (p. 11).
- (D8) Report out-of-range behaviour of the optimized design (Table 5), not only in-range performance (pp. 19–20).

**Quotes**:
- "Unacceptable designs were assigned an extremely high cost value in order to keep the search within the acceptable region of the design space." (p. 12)
- "The optimal design will be located on the edge, as otherwise, it can be improved with a larger alpha level or smaller power." (p. 12)
