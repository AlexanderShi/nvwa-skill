# Paper cards · batch s03 (supplement)

Batch intent: See how Audet's group, in the 2019–2021 cluster funded largely through Hydro-Québec / Rio Tinto / EDF / IVADO partnerships, turns partner pathologies (expensive hydrological calibration, binary and hidden constraints, engineers' monotonicity intuition, Monte-Carlo noise with tunable precision, multimodality) into MADS add-ons. ### S167 · Low-cost and representative surrogate hydrological models. Part I – Construction of surrogates (Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2019–07, 2019, no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P.-L. Huot, A. Poulin (ÉTS), S. Alarie (IREQ, Hydro-Québec) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: This is Part I of a two-paper project. Part II embeds these surrogates in the hybrid DDS-MADS calibrator of Huot et al. (2017) (pp. 6, 20). The paper is hydrology-led and supported by an NSERC PhD scholarship; the data come from DEH and Hydro-Québec (p. 4). Audet is third author. **Read**: all prose on pp. 1–20, references skimmed. Tables 1–6, Figures 1–5 and Appendix I (the parameter list, p. 20) are **not in the extracted text** (only captions), so numbers below are those stated in the prose.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an application cost. One distributed HYDROTEL simulation "may easily consume more than one minute", and a calibration needs thousands (abstract, p. 4; p. 5). The authors start from Razavi et al.'s four avenues against expensive calibration (efficient optimizers, pre-emption, surrogates, parallelism) and add a fifth, hardware progress, illustrated by Audet & Orban's 55-problem run falling from 3 h 45 min to 5 min 40 s (p. 5). The stated gap: reduced-fidelity (physically based, non-adaptive) surrogates are underused in the water-resources community, blamed on their lack of versatility and on the expertise needed (p. 6). The problem is stated operationally. A good surrogate must meet two "mandatory" conditions, high representativeness and an important decrease in computational time (§2.2, p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: Three simplifications of the simulator itself, not data-fitted models. (1) Fewer meteorological stations, removed in a checkerboard pattern to keep spatial coverage (p. 9). (2) A shorter calibration period (p. 10). (3) Coarser spatial discretization, i.e. fewer RHHU sub-basins (p. 10). The three are then combined (§4.4, p. 17). Each surrogate is judged on 5,000 Latin-hypercube parameter sets by R² *and* Spearman's rank correlation R_s between the original and surrogate 1−NSE values, plus scatter plots zoomed on the optimal zone (pp. 8, 11–12). The key argument is that ranking and the alignment of optimal zones matter more than value agreement: a surrogate with the better R² can misplace the optima (hypothetical Fig. 1, p. 8). The speed-up r is binned: unusable ≤ 1.5, poor ≤ 4, medium ≤ 8, superior ≤ 15, ideal > 15 (p. 9). Before any experiment, the authors predict each surrogate's likely ratio from which submodels of the pipeline it speeds up (Table 1 discussion, pp. 7, 9–10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The objective is 1−NSE on daily streamflow (eq. 1, p. 11). There are two formulations, HYDROTEL 10 (10 most sensitive parameters) and HYDROTEL 19 (+9 parameters) (pp. 6–7), on three Québec watersheds (Ceizur 6,928 km², Cowansville 215 km², Toulnustouc 8,109 km²), for six calibration problems (p. 10). The 0.9 threshold for R² and R_s is borrowed from Toal (2015); the authors note that it was meant for response-surface surrogates, not reduced-fidelity ones (p. 8). CPU time is the mean of 1,000 random simulations on an i7 at 3.40 GHz (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There are no theorems. The analysis is mechanistic. Recomputing geomorphological hydrographs (step 2 of the pipeline) explains why HYDROTEL 19 costs 5–25% more (p. 7) and why fewer RHHUs, which means longer flow paths, gives unusable ratios for HYDROTEL 19 (pp. 10, 16). For HYDROTEL 10, the period-length ratio approximately predicts the CPU ratio (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: Results are shown on Toulnustouc, "the worst watershed candidate" (p. 12), with best and worst instances per level (4 station-removal instances per level, p. 12; 2/4/4/4 instances for the 18/12/9/6-month periods, p. 14). Failures are reported. The worst 87.5%-removal instance falls to R_s 0.638 and R² 0.411 (p. 12). Six-month periods never exceed R_s 0.512 or R² 0.392 (p. 14). Dry-period windows were tried and dropped (p. 14). Combined surrogates reach ratios of 44, 16 and 32 for HYDROTEL 10, but only about 2 for HYDROTEL 19 (p. 18), and 3 of the 6 combined surrogates fall below 0.9 (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is methodological and applied: a construction-and-evaluation protocol for reduced-fidelity surrogates. The limits are concrete: poor ratios for HYDROTEL 19, and representativeness below 0.9 on half of the combined surrogates (pp. 18–19). Two questions are explicitly deferred to Part II: whether R² > 0.9 is really needed for reduced-fidelity surrogates, and whether an "ideal" ratio matters (p. 20). A third future direction is to use the reduced models as the operational models themselves (p. 20).

**Method links**: Method 4 ⚠ variant: it starts from a real partner blackbox, but the output is a surrogate-building protocol and no public release of the problem is mentioned (data credited to DEH and Hydro-Québec, p. 4). Method 1 ✅ evidence (indirect): the surrogates are destined for DDS-MADS, which merges "the convergence analysis and robust local refinement" of MADS with DDS's global heuristic exploration (p. 20). Method 5 ⚠ variant: the currency here is the CPU-time ratio, and representativeness is measured by rank correlation rather than by optimization runs. New-pattern candidate: **surrogates judged and used as rankers** (see S072, S143).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before building a surrogate, write down two measurable acceptance criteria (a rank-fidelity threshold and a speed-up band) and bin the speed-up into named classes, so every surrogate gets a verdict.
- Judge a surrogate for optimization with Spearman's rank correlation next to R², and zoom the original-vs-surrogate scatter plot on the best-known region, because order near the optimum is what the optimizer uses.
- Report the worst instance of each reduction level and the watershed (problem) where surrogates do worst, not only the average.

**Quotes**:
- "However, as can be seen in Figure 1, a high R2 value does not guarantee a high representativeness near the optimal zones." (p. 8)
- "Hence, sharing similarities does not mean that the objective function values obtained on the surrogate versus the original must be as similar as possible." (p. 8)

### S072 · Binary, unrelaxable and hidden constraints in blackbox optimization (Operations Research Letters 2020, 10.1016/j.orl.2020.05.011)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: G. Caporossi, S. Jacquet · **Position in trajectory**: The text read is the GERAD preprint G–2019–76 (October 2019); the published ORL version was not read. It comes with a companion report on constraint scaling in MADS (G–2019–65, ref. [3], p. 11). Support: NSERC Discovery Grant (p. 4). **Read**: whole text (pp. 4–12).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min f subject to binary constraints b_i: ℝⁿ → {0,1}, quantitative constraints c_i ≤ 0, and x ∈ X, where X collects the unrelaxable and hidden constraints (problem (1), p. 5). The vocabulary comes from the Le Digabel–Wild taxonomy (p. 5). The entry is a mismatch between semantics and mechanism: binary constraints are relaxable by definition, yet MADS treats them with the extreme barrier, like unrelaxable or hidden ones (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: First, all unrelaxable and hidden constraints are merged into a single binary constraint b₀, "necessarily unquantifiable" (§3.1, p. 7). Classification surrogates b̃_i are then built by weighted k-nearest-neighbours with an Epanechnikov kernel (§3.2, pp. 8–9). The neighbour count and bandwidth depend on |V^k|, the number of points evaluated so far; the extracted formula is garbled, and I read it as ⌈√|V^k|⌉ (p. 9). These feed seven surrogate constraint-violation functions (pp. 7–8):
  - h̃₁: additive;
  - h̃₂: max(b̃₀, b̃_i) terms;
  - h̃₃: a multiplicative (1+b̃₀) penalty;
  - h̃₄–h̃₇: the same forms with each c̃_i divided by a_i^k, the largest violation observed so far.

  **What changes** is only the *order* in which candidates are evaluated under the opportunistic strategy: x goes before y if it is no worse in both surrogate f̃ and h̃ and strictly better in one (pp. 6, 8). **What does not change** is barrier acceptance and the PB convergence analysis (p. 6). Algebraic simplification: for a binary b_i, max(0, b_i)² = b_i (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: No smoothness assumptions are stated. h uses squared violations "so that no additional non-smoothness is introduced" (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no theorem. The only formal claim is that changing the ordering "has no impact on the convergence analysis" of the progressive barrier (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: Three blackboxes, 30 instances each; the text does not state how the instances differ (p. 9):
  - STYRENE: 4 binary constraints plus hidden ones, with about 14% of evaluated points failing, and 7 relaxable constraints;
  - MDO: its first constraint is *converted* to binary and its second to unrelaxable, to create the pathology;
  - Lockwood: converted the same way.

  Setup: NOMAD 3.8.0 with OrthoMADS, quadratic models for f̃ and c̃_i, 1,500 evaluations (p. 9). Data profiles at τ = 10⁻² and 10⁻⁴, with f_L the best value found by all compared versions (p. 9). All surrogate variants beat the default extreme barrier, h̃₁ is best among the unscaled ones, weighting "did not bring much improvements" (p. 10), and h̃₇ is recommended (p. 11). No seeds or number of replications are given, and there is no competitor outside NOMAD.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: This is a small algorithmic contribution to NOMAD's ordering. The authors state that the differences between versions are "minimal" and that the gain from weighting is "not significant" (p. 11). Future direction: use the surrogate's probability estimate for binary relaxable constraints inside the true h itself (p. 11).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ evidence: the paper refines the taxonomy (binary constraints are relaxable but unquantifiable; unrelaxable and hidden constraints merge into one unquantifiable binary) and gives the new class its own mechanism (pp. 5, 7). Method 1 ✅ variant: the new idea lives in neither the Search nor the Poll but in the *evaluation order* under opportunism, a third theory-free slot (p. 6). Method 5 ⚠ variant: data profiles are used, but the comparison is only against NOMAD's own default and replications are unreported (p. 9). New-pattern candidate: **surrogates judged and used as rankers** (with S167 and S143).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If you cannot prove anything about a new mechanism, confine it to the order in which an opportunistic solver evaluates its candidate list. The candidate set, and hence the convergence proof, is untouched.
- To test a method on a constraint class that no benchmark has, convert constraints of an existing realistic benchmark into that class (quantitative → binary or unrelaxable) and say so.
- Normalize heterogeneous constraint surrogates by the largest violation observed so far (a_i^k), a scaling that needs no user input (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Despite the binary constraints, as defined in the optimization problem being relaxable, Mads handles them as if they were unrelaxable or hidden." (p. 5)
- "It has an impact on the ordering strategy, thus on the order in which elements will be evaluated. This has no impact on the convergence analysis from [4]." (p. 6)

### S143 · Monotonic grey box direct search optimization (Optimization Letters 14(1):3–18, 2020, 10.1007/s11590-019-01497-8)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Côté (Rio Tinto), C. Poissant (her MSc thesis is ref. [18]), C. Tribes · **Position in trajectory**: The text read is the GERAD preprint G–2019–15 (February 2019), titled "Monotonic grey box optimization"; the published version was not read. Funding: NSERC CRD grant with Hydro-Québec and Rio Tinto (p. 4). **Read**: whole text, including the appendix of trend matrices (pp. 4–17).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is industrial. On Rio Tinto's weekly Kemano hydro-management blackbox, engineers found a feasible point by hand, then launched NOMAD from it. That was faster than starting NOMAD from an infeasible point, because they knew which variables to move (pp. 12, 13–14). This is formalized as a *monotonic grey box*: the sign of the effect of increasing a variable on a constraint is known, its size is not (abstract, p. 4; Definition 1, p. 5). The goal is deliberately narrow: reach feasibility as fast as possible (abstract, p. 4; p. 10).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: The user's knowledge is encoded in an n×m **trend matrix** T with entries −1, 1, 0 or N/A (Definition 3, p. 7), from which trend cones are built (Definition 4, p. 7). Two directions are derived over the violated or active constraints J(x):
  - the **strict trend direction** d_T, which zeroes a coordinate whenever any relevant entry is N/A (Definition 5, p. 8);
  - the looser **trend direction** d̃_T, which ignores N/A entries (Definition 6, p. 9).

  Use: MADS poll candidates are reordered by their angle with −d_T instead of with the last successful direction. A parameter-free switch hands over to model-based ordering once n+1 affinely independent points exist near the incumbent (§3.2, p. 10). The poll directions, mesh and progressive barrier are unchanged (§3.2, p. 10). T can be filled analytically, by sampling (LHS points plus coordinate steps, a necessary-but-not-sufficient test), or from the designer's intuition, which the authors call "the most dangerous" (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Cone-monotonicity (Definition 2) is extended to functions valued in ℝ ∪ {±∞}, because a failed evaluation is assigned ∞ (p. 5). Neither differentiability nor continuity is required (p. 6). Proposition 3 needs −d_T to be feasible with respect to X and the strictly satisfied constraints to be upper semicontinuous (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Four results carry the argument.
  - Proposition 2: monotonicity on two cones implies monotonicity on their convex hull. The proof is a two-step chain, g(x) ≤ g(x+αd₁) ≤ g(x+αd₁+(1−α)d₂) (p. 6).
  - Corollary 2 bridges to the MADS analysis: d ∈ K implies g°(x; d) ≥ 0 (Clarke) (p. 6).
  - Corollary 4: d_T lies in every trend cone of J(x), proved case by case on the sign of each component (p. 8).
  - Proposition 3: −d_T is a descent direction for h. The proof splits h into the violated part S_J, which is monotone along the cone because sums, maxima and squares preserve monotonicity (Poissant), and the satisfied part S_J̄, which is zero nearby by upper semicontinuity (p. 9).

  Reader's note: the proof establishes h(x − α d_T) ≤ h(x), i.e. non-increase, which the paper calls "descent" (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Setup: NOMAD 3.9.1, quadratic-model search off, 2n poll directions; the objective is to minimize h until feasible; data profiles show the share of starting points reaching feasibility against evaluations (p. 10).
  - Analytic problems: 8 of them (n = 4–13, m = 4–11), each with 10 infeasible starts × 10 seeds (p. 11). Strict-trend-then-model ordering dominates everywhere except ZHAOWANG F5 (p. 11).
  - MDO: T is sampled from 200 LHS points with δ = 10 (p. 12).
  - Kemano: n = 5, m = 5, 90 s per evaluation, budget 100, 20 infeasible starts, and two trend matrices, the engineer's T_int and a sampled T_samp (pp. 12–13).
  - Negative results are reported: on Kemano the strict trend direction "shows a very poor performance" because d_T(x⁰) = 0 for 18 of 20 starts. The best combination is the looser trend direction with T_samp (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory (cone-monotonicity results), an algorithmic ordering, and an application. Stated limits: only the feasibility phase is addressed; the strict direction can vanish; a sampled T is only a conjecture (pp. 11, 13). Future work: exploit trends of the objective, and balance feasibility and objective effort inside the progressive barrier (p. 14).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ variant: poll *ordering* as the insertion slot, with a parameter-free fallback to models (p. 10). Method 2 ✅ evidence: constraint-level structure is modeled per constraint and failures are kept as +∞ (pp. 5, 7). Research Heuristic 9 ✅: this paper is its cited case. Method 3 ⚠ variant: the theory certifies a property of the heuristic direction, with no new convergence theorem and no counterexamples. Methods 4/5 ⚠: the Kemano blackbox is not described as released, and there are no cross-family competitors. New-pattern candidates: **surrogates judged and used as rankers** (with S072, S167); **encode practitioner intuition as a typed solver input** (trend matrix; compare the user-declared EB/PB constraint types of Method 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When practitioners beat your solver by hand, write down the qualitative information they use and give it a typed input format (here a matrix of signs with an explicit "unknown" entry), with three ways to fill it: analytic, by sampling, or from intuition.
- Use a parameter-free switch from a structural heuristic to a data-driven model, triggered by the model becoming buildable (n+1 affinely independent points in the region, p. 10).
- To show a heuristic direction helps feasibility, split the violation function into violated and strictly satisfied parts. Handle the first by monotonicity and the second by semicontinuity.
- Keep both a strict and a relaxed version of the heuristic and report the case where the strict one degenerates (d_T = 0).

**Quotes**:
- "Finally, the third technique is certainly the most dangerous one and consists in trusting the intuition of the blackbox simulation designer." (p. 11)
- "The present work proposes a way to mechanize this process, by supplying the engineers with a tool to translate their intuition about the monotonic behavior of some functions." (p. 14)

### S084 · Optimization of stochastic blackboxes with adaptive precision (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2021, 10.1137/20m1318894; arXiv 1911.05846)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Alarie (IREQ, Hydro-Québec), P.-Y. Bouchet, S. Le Digabel · **Position in trajectory**: The text read is arXiv v1 (13 Nov 2019), titled "Optimization of noisy blackboxes with adaptive precision"; the SIAM version was not read. Funding: NSERC CRD grant with Hydro-Québec and Rio Tinto, plus an InnovÉÉ grant (p. 1). The industrial test problem comes from EDF R&D (pp. 24, 28). SKILL.md's Heuristic 10 already lists this paper as a thesis-to-paper case (Bouchet). **Read**: §1–§3 in full (pp. 1–19); §4 prose and tables (pp. 19–27; plot contents not extractable); §5 (pp. 27–28). References skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The target is an "adaptive precision" blackbox: the noise source is known and controllable (e.g. the number of Monte-Carlo draws), with σ ∝ 1/√N, so cost grows like 1/σ² (p. 2). The literature is sorted into three paradigms (pp. 2–3): fix the noise level beforehand; decrease it monotonically; adapt it both ways. MpMads is placed in the second (a generalization of Polak–Wetter's GPS scheme) and DpMads in the third (pp. 3, 5).
- **D2 Core idea & design**:
  - Estimates: the maximum-likelihood (inverse-variance weighted) f^k(x) and its σ^k(x) over the cache (eq. 3, p. 4).
  - Poll: the incumbent and poll points are re-sampled until σ^k(x) ≤ σ^k (Algorithm 1, p. 6).
  - Decisions: every comparison is a p-value, p^k = Φ((f^k(x_c) − f^k(x_s))/√(σ^k(x_c)² + σ^k(x_s)²)) for the hypothesis f(x_c) < f(x_s) (p. 7). Frame expansion and shrinkage require p^k beyond β_u or β_ℓ (Algorithm 2, p. 9).
  - Condition CDp forces the precision index up whenever p^k ∈ [β_ℓ, β_u]. MpMads adds CMp: the precision stays unchanged otherwise (p. 8). *The two algorithms differ only in UpdateR* (p. 8), so one proof covers both.
  - Defaults: DpMads uses [15%, 85%] (p. 8). For MpMads the text says [0.03%, 99.7%] while Fig. 3 marks 0.003 and 0.997 (reader's note: probably a typo for 0.3%, pp. 8, 11).
  - Other components: a DpMads Search re-estimates plausible points cheaply (τ = 0.25, p. 10), and a ρ(r) map sends the precision index to σ in [σ_min, σ_max] (p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Additive Gaussian noise whose σ does not depend on x (p. 3). The analysis also assumes (p. 11; summary p. 19):
  - D_f = ℝⁿ and bounded level sets;
  - σ_min = 0;
  - 0 < β_ℓ ≤ 0.5 and σ_max < ∞;
  - CDp.

  The authors show σ_min = 0 is needed when the Search is off, since otherwise the Poll cannot drive σ^k below σ_min (p. 10). The problem is unconstrained only.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The analysis is a chain of reductions to finite sets.
  - Lemma 3.2 (strong law of large numbers): the true minimizer over a finite set eventually minimizes the estimates (p. 12), extended to full orderings (Cor. 3.3, p. 13).
  - Lemma 3.4: p-values tend to 0 or 1 for distinct values, via σ^k ≤ σ_max/√k (pp. 13–14). Lemma 3.5 covers ties (p. 14).
  - Lemma 3.6: all points lie on one mesh (pp. 14–15).
  - Thm 3.7: incumbents stay bounded, by Borel–Cantelli with Lemma 3.1 (a max-sum-of-products bound equal to 1) giving P(x^k_* ∉ L_i) ≤ Φ(−i/(√2 σ_max)) (pp. 15–16).
  - Thm 3.8: liminf δ_m = 0 almost surely, by contradiction on the finite set mesh ∩ ball, with ties treated as a random walk on frame sizes (pp. 16–17).
  - Thm 3.9: Clarke stationarity almost surely at a refined point (p. 18).

  Reader's note: Thm 3.7 conditions on the cache while treating the estimates as independent Gaussians (a product over points), and Thm 3.9 goes from a per-iteration bound Φ(−µ/(√2 σ_max)) < 1 to an almost-sure statement without naming the independence used. I did not verify these steps (pp. 15–16, 18).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Currency: benchmarking is redefined in **Monte-Carlo draws = 1/σ²**, with a "fictive" Monte-Carlo interpretation when the noise is not draw-based. Performance, data and accuracy profiles are rebuilt on it (pp. 19–20).
  - Competitor and machine: Robust-MADS (NOMAD 3.9.1) against Python prototypes (p. 19).
  - Analytic problems: Norm2 (p. 20) and Moustache, a thin-ribbon domain (pp. 21–22), each with 500 runs (pp. 21, 23).
  - Industrial problem: EDF's VME asset-management simulator, with 5 integer dates in [1, 350], σ = 1800 at N = 2¹⁰ draws, and a hidden cap on draws that gives σ_min ≈ 34.1, set to 50 (pp. 24–25). Deterministic MADS is run at fixed draws per evaluation (1,024 up to 10⁶; 32 min up to 25.3 days) and so is Robust-MADS (Tables 1–2, pp. 25–26).
  - Result: Robust-MADS needs about 100 times more draws than DpMads to reach the optimal solution (p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and theoretical, plus a profile methodology for variable-cost evaluations. The stated limits are concrete (p. 28):
  - parameters set ad hoc;
  - the monotone strategy can make run times explode;
  - the dynamic strategy struggles on flat objectives;
  - precision is updated per iteration rather than per evaluation.

  Future work: constraints affected by adaptive-precision noise, non-Gaussian noise, and models (p. 28).

**Method links**: Method 5 ✅ evidence: step 1 (fix the evaluation currency first) is taken to its conclusion. Evaluation counts are rejected as "irrelevant" and all three profile types are re-derived in draws (p. 19). Method 3 ⚠ variant: the Clarke-type ladder is carried over to noise with an explicit list of hypotheses (p. 19), and the need for σ_min = 0 is argued (p. 10), but there are no counterexamples, and the probabilistic steps are loosely stated (see D4). Method 6 ✅ evidence (weak): the comparator comes from NOMAD 3.9.1 while the new methods are Python prototypes (p. 19), consistent with SKILL.md's remark that StoMads-type features lag NOMAD. Method 4 ⚠: the EDF problem is used, but no release is mentioned. New-pattern candidate: **hypothesis-test acceptance with an explicit p-value** (the statistical decision replaces the deterministic comparison; one proof for a family of update rules).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a noisy setting, make every accept/reject/expand/shrink decision a hypothesis test with an explicit p-value and two thresholds, and let the variants you compare differ in *one* function (here UpdateR). A single convergence proof then covers the whole family.
- To port a deterministic direct-search proof to noise, first prove that the estimates eventually order any finite set correctly (Lemma 3.2); the mesh-plus-bounded-ball argument then reduces everything else to finite sets.
- When evaluation cost depends on a user-chosen precision, redefine the x-axis of performance, data and accuracy profiles in cost units (draws = 1/σ²) and say why evaluation counts are misleading.
- Classify the literature by how it controls the resource (fixed, monotone, dynamic) and place each proposed algorithm in one cell.

**Quotes**:

### S094 · Using symbolic calculations to determine largest small polygons (Journal of Global Optimization 2021, 10.1007/s10898-020-00908-w)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen, D. Svrtan (Zagreb) · **Position in trajectory**: The text read is the GERAD preprint G–2019–57 (August 2019); the JOGO version was not read. The paper belongs to Audet's long-running extremal-geometry line, cited in its references: the largest small octagon (2002), the small octagon with longest perimeter (2007), ranking small regular polygons (2009), and equilateral small polygons (Amer. Math. Monthly 2017) (p. 10). **Read**: whole text (pp. 4–10).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: A polygon is small if its diameter is 1; the problem is the maximal area of small n-gons (p. 5). Known results: the regular polygon is optimal for odd n (Reinhardt 1922); n = 4 is solved; for n = 6, Graham obtained the value by factoring a degree-40 polynomial; for n = 8 only numerical values (4 and 7 digits) were known (p. 5). The entry is a new formulation, in the complex plane, that lowers the algebraic degree for the hexagon and makes the axially symmetric octagon tractable (pp. 5, 8).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: The pending edge of the diameter graph is fixed at 0 and i; symmetry gives z₄ = −z̄₁ (p. 6). The area is written in Re/Im form together with the cycle-closure equation (eq. 1, p. 7). Variables are eliminated with resultants and discriminants in Maple (footnote, p. 7).
  - Hexagon: a degree-14 polynomial that factors as p₆ (degree 10, Graham's) × (2A−1)²(2A+1)² (p. 7).
  - Octagon: a resultant, then a discriminant (degree 32 in z, 10 in A), then a discriminant of degree 344, whose factor p₈ of degree 42 carries the area (p. 9).

  The root is selected in [0.6, π/4] or [0.7, π/4], using the regular polygon as a lower bound and the small circle as an upper bound (pp. 7, 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The work relies on the Foster–Szabó theorem (the diameter graph of an optimal even n-gon is a cycle of length n−1 plus a pending edge) and on Reinhardt's perpendicularity property (pp. 5–6). The octagon result is conditional on the axial-symmetry conjecture, which is stated in the theorem itself (abstract, p. 4; Thm 3.1, p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Thm 2.1 re-proves Yuan's hexagon symmetry by *symmetrization-by-averaging*: if α ≠ β, replace both angles by (α+β)/2, show through a trigonometric inequality chain that the diameter stays 1, and show the area grows by the area of a triangle, a contradiction (p. 6). Theorems 2.2 and 3.1 use the device that a critical value A of a polynomial t makes the discriminant of t(x) − A vanish (p. 7). Reader's note: the text asserts, rather than argues, that the root in the bracketing interval is unique and is the maximum (pp. 7, 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: Only computational effort is reported: negligible for the hexagon, about 90 s and 100 MB for the octagon; the decagon failed with 100 GB of memory (p. 9). The values are A ≈ 0.67498144293010470369 (p. 7) and A ≈ 0.72686848275162676684 (p. 9), consistent with the Henrion–Messine bounds (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The paper claims the first analytical value for the (symmetric) largest small octagon and a shorter derivation for the hexagon (p. 9). Limits: the octagon result depends on the symmetry conjecture, and the method does not scale to the decagon because of memory (p. 9). The promised "comments on larger n-gons" (p. 5) reduce to that memory remark (p. 9).

**Method links**: Method 3 ⚠ variant: outside DFO, but it shares the habit of stating a result relative to an explicit, named hypothesis (the symmetry conjecture, p. 8) and of re-proving known results (Graham, Yuan) by a different route (pp. 6–7). No counterexamples are involved. New-pattern candidate: **extremal-geometry side line: numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later** (the 2002 numeric octagon → the 2019 algebraic value, refs p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To get the exact optimal value of a low-dimensional polynomial program, use the device critical value ⇒ discriminant of t(x) − A = 0, eliminate the other variables with resultants, and isolate the right root with cheap a priori bounds (a regular-polygon lower bound and a circle upper bound).
- Symmetrization-by-averaging: to show an optimizer is symmetric, average its two asymmetric parameters, verify that the averaged object stays feasible, and show a strict improvement.
- Report the computational wall (memory, time) at which the method fails for the next case, as a concrete limit.

**Quotes**:
- "The latter is the first analytical solution for the octagon, as only numerical approximations of the optimal area were known." (p. 9)
- "We have applied the same methodology to the symmetrical decagon using a larger computer, but 100 gygabytes of memory was insufficient to compute the discriminant." (p. 9)

### S133 · Combining cross-entropy and MADS methods for inequality constrained global optimization (Operations Research Forum 2(3):33, 2021, 10.1007/s43069-021-00075-y; arXiv 2012.10173)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Bigeon (CNRS, LS2N), R. Couderc (GERAD / Grenoble INP) · **Position in trajectory**: The text read is arXiv v2 (11 Aug 2023); the published version was not read. Funding: an IVADO fundamental research grant for Audet and a French ministerial grant for Couderc. Code: NOMAD plus custom code (p. 1). **Read**: §1, §2.1, §3, §4 and §5 (pp. 1–5, 8–18). The CE background derivation of §2.2 (pp. 5–7) and the Appendix A problem table (pp. 18–20) were **skimmed**. Plot contents are not extractable.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper names two blackbox difficulties: costly evaluations, and multiple local minima that can trap MADS (p. 2). Global heuristics (CMA-ES, GA, DE, PSO) need many evaluations and have no specific constraint mechanism. MADS add-ons (surrogates, quadratic models, Nelder–Mead) improve efficiency but do not address local optima (p. 2). The entry is a complementarity: cross-entropy (CE) reaches promising regions quickly but has no guarantees and refines slowly (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: CE is placed as a MADS **Search** step whose trial points are projected onto the mesh (pp. 3, 10). The differences from classical CE:
  1. The elite points come from the whole cache, so any MADS point can serve. They are ranked by a Best(x, y) function built on progressive-barrier dominance and h, not by a penalty (Definition 31, p. 8).
  2. Initialization: µ is the poll centre and σ = 2(u − ℓ), with artificial bounds x̄ ± τ⁻¹Δ^max for infinite bounds (eqs. 12–14, pp. 8–9).
  3. Truncated normal sampling, with α = 0.7 smoothing because cache elites shrink σ too fast (p. 9).
  4. The Search runs only when ‖σ^k‖ < ‖σ^p‖, i.e. the elite spread has shrunk since the last CE step, plus a restart at twice the initial σ when feasibility is not reached (p. 9).

  Defaults: α = 0.7, N_e = 4, N_s = 2n (p. 11). Reader's note: Algorithm 3 (p. 10) samples from N(µ^k, 2σ^k), initializes µ with x̄⁰ (the text's eq. 13 says x̄^k), and does not show the α smoothing described on p. 9.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Problem (1) has bound and inequality constraints, with f and c valued in the extended reals (p. 2). The guarantee is inherited from MADS-PB: if all trial points lie in a bounded set, there is a limit point with f°(x*; d) ≥ 0 on hypertangent directions when it is feasible (pp. 4–5). There is no new theorem.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none. Convergence is claimed to be retained because CE points are finitely many and lie on the mesh (abstract, p. 1; p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Calibration: 100 analytic problems (n = 2–60, 28 constrained), split into 69 unconstrained, 25 constrained and 6 large ones. Budget 1000(n+1), 3 seeds, NOMAD 3.9.1 with the NM search and without models; grids over (N_e, N_s) and α (pp. 10–11). Findings: insensitive to parameters; CE not useful at n = 50–60 (p. 11).
  - Cross-family comparison: against pymoo GA, DE, CMA-ES and PSO on 19 shared unconstrained problems, 5 seeds, 3,000 evaluations. Population sizes were set "after few tests" (pp. 11–14).
  - Engineering problems: MDO, STYRENE (two minima) and LOCKWOOD, each with 20 random starts × 3 seeds, against MADS default, LH-MADS (100 + 10 LH points) and VNS-MADS (pp. 15–17).
  - Results: CE-MADS is best on MDO for every τ (p. 15). On LOCKWOOD, CE- and LH-MADS always reach feasibility, against 41/60 runs for MADS and 46/60 for VNS-MADS (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic: one Search step. Limits: CE is not useful at large n, and this could not be checked on large engineering problems for lack of access (p. 11). The authors call the win over heuristics on engineering problems "not particularly relevant" (p. 18). Future work: link the mesh size to the CE standard deviation (p. 18).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence (textbook case): a guarantee-free heuristic admitted as a Search, with the claim that the combination "retains the convergence properties" (p. 1). Warning sign 6 and Taste mark 3 ✅: already cited in SKILL.md (cross-entropy + MADS, 2021). Method 2 ✅ evidence: constraint handling is transplanted into the heuristic through PB dominance instead of a penalty (p. 8). Method 5 ✅ evidence with ⚠: there are cross-family competitors, seeds and data profiles in groups of (n+1) evaluations (pp. 10, 12), but the heuristic competitors meet only unconstrained analytic problems (plus MDO with pymoo's penalty) and their populations are tuned informally (pp. 11–16). Method 4 ✅ evidence: the realistic benchmarks STYRENE, MDO and LOCKWOOD are reused (pp. 15–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When grafting a population heuristic onto a solver, draw its elite set from the solver's whole cache and rank it with the solver's own constraint-dominance rule. The heuristic then inherits constraint handling without a penalty parameter.
- Gate an expensive exploration step with a data-driven trigger (the spread of the elites has shrunk since the last call), not a fixed frequency, and add a restart rule for the infeasible case.
- Calibrate new hyper-parameters on separate unconstrained, constrained and large-n subsets, and report where the method stops helping (here n ≥ 50).
- Report, per competitor, how many of the runs reach feasibility on a hard realistic problem (41/60 vs 60/60), not only profile curves.

**Quotes**:
- "The result of this combination retains the convergence properties of Mads and allows an efficient exploration in order to move away from local minima." (p. 1)
- "Second, CE-Mads outperforms the heuristic on the engineering problems, which is not particularly relevant because it benefits to the Mads ability of performing well on this kind of problems." (p. 18)
