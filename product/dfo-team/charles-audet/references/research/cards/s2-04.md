# Batch s2-04 · Charles Audet · 2013–2017 side lines and applied MADS: polygons, games, likelihoods, fleets, linear equalities, hydropower (supplement)

**Batch intent**: The eight papers fall into three groups.
- *Exact-optimization side lines*: two 2013 extremal-polygon papers (S093, S117) and a 2014 game-theory enumeration note (S134). These show how Audet gets rigor outside the Clarke-derivative ladder: closed-form bounds, finite enumeration and certified global solvers.
- *MADS meets structure or another community*: NOMAD embedded in a genetics package (S066), NOMAD relayed with a gradient-based solver on a fleet-maintenance NLP (S145), and a theory-plus-wrapper paper that adds linear equalities to MADS (S064).
- *Rio Tinto hydropower*: two 2017 stochastic-programming papers. The second (S060) turns the first's (S032) rolling horizon into a blackbox and tunes it with NOMAD.

For each paper, the questions are:
- Does the constraint-semantics habit (Method 2) hold when MADS runs inside someone else's software?
- How are comparisons set up (Method 5)?
- Is Heuristic 7 practised (treat an algorithm or pipeline as a blackbox)?
- Which proof or certification devices recur across the non-DFO papers?

**Focus dimensions**: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line), D8. These are supplement short cards. The emphasis is on D3/D4 for the theory papers (S093, S117, S134, S064) and D5 for the applied ones (S066, S145, S032, S060).
**Material roles**: S093 supplement · S117 supplement · S066 supplement · S134 supplement · S145 supplement · S064 supplement · S032 supplement · S060 supplement.

### S093 · Maximal perimeter, diameter and area of equilateral unit-width convex polygons (Journal of Global Optimization 2013, 10.1007/s10898-011-9780-4)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Ninin (ENSEEIHT-IRIT, Toulouse) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: belongs to the *2002–2013 Extremal small polygons* row of SKILL.md. It solves three problems left open in the Audet–Hansen–Messine surveys, its refs [1, 3] (p. 2), and S117 cites it as ref. 1 (S117 p. 11). **Read**: all 12 pages. Figures survive only as vertex labels.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a gap in a catalogue. Two survey papers list extremal problems for convex polygons, say which are trivial and which are open, and this paper takes the three open ones: maximize the perimeter, the diameter and the area of unit-width equilateral convex polygons (p. 2). It first disposes of the trivial cases (pp. 2–3, Fig. 1):
  - without the equilateral condition, all three are unbounded;
  - with n even, they are also unbounded (an elongated parallelogram, Fig. 1).

  So the problem is n = 2m+1, where the minimal width is positive because the vertices cannot be collinear (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: One limiting shape solves all three problems: the trapezoid T̂_n with non-parallel sides ĉ_n = 2/√3, approached by (2m+1)-gons "arbitrarily close" to it (p. 3). The values are P̂ = 2(2m+1)/√3 for the perimeter, D̂ = 2m/√3 for the diameter and Â = (2m−1)/√3 for the area (Fig. 1, p. 3; Thm 2.2 p. 6; Cor. 2.3 p. 7; Thm 3.3 p. 9).
  - The perimeter is proved through the equivalent problem of minimizing the width at fixed perimeter (p. 6).
  - The diameter follows as a corollary (p. 7).
  - The area needs its own argument (pp. 7–10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The polygons are convex and equilateral with n = 2m+1 (n = 3 is trivial, p. 3). The normalization puts one side on y = 0 and a vertex on y = W_n (p. 3). The optimum is a supremum and is not attained, because the trapezoid has only four sides; the paper says so explicitly (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - *Normal form*: vertices A, B and C are defined by extreme coordinates. Assuming B = C would put the polygon inside a triangle of width W_n cos θ < W_n, a contradiction (pp. 3–4).
  - *Improving perturbation*: Lemma 2.1 builds a new equilateral convex polygon with the same perimeter and a strictly smaller width. It does this by rigid motions of two vertex chains along circular arcs, which contradicts minimality (pp. 5–6).
  - *Parity count*: with p sides on the top line and q on the bottom, q ≤ p+2 and p+q+2 odd force q = p+1, i.e. the trapezoid (p. 6).
  - *Area*: Lemma 3.1 bounds the side length c_n using Yaglom–Boltyanskii's maximal area (2P−π)/4 of a unit-width figure (p. 7). The pentagon is handled with triangle heights (Lemma 3.2, pp. 8–9). For n ≥ 7, two corner regions α are subtracted, then concavity in y_C and convexity in c_n move the bound to interval endpoints (pp. 9–10). The authors note that this last step "does not hold for m = 2", which is why the pentagon lemma is needed (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The paper is purely analytic (pp. 1–11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical. It closes three open problems and, by homothety, the equivalent minimum-width problems (pp. 2, 10). Table 1 marks each cell of the family as solved (with the optimal shape and value) or open. Maximum width at unit area remains open (p. 11). There is no limitations paragraph: the conclusion is one paragraph plus the table (pp. 10–11).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: rigor comes from a closed-form bound plus a limiting construction showing tightness, not from a convergence ladder. When a step fails for the smallest case (m = 2), that case is proved separately rather than dropped (pp. 6–10). No counterexamples are used.
- Research Trajectory ✅ evidence: the *2002–2013 Extremal small polygons* row. The references are five Audet–Hansen–Messine polygon papers from 2002–2009 (pp. 11–12).
- new-pattern candidate: *catalogue open cells, then fill them*. The paper takes the "open" cells of the group's own survey tables (p. 2) and returns an updated table with solved and open cells (p. 11). S117 does the same with Bezdek–Fodor's open cases.
- new-pattern candidate: *equivalent-problem swap*: maximize perimeter at unit width ⇔ minimize width at unit perimeter. It is used in the proof (p. 6) and in the final table (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To prove a candidate extremum optimal, prove structural lemmas by an improving perturbation: keep the constraint value fixed, strictly improve the objective, derive a contradiction. Then close with a counting or parity argument that leaves one configuration (pp. 5–6).
- (D4) When the general bound argument breaks for the smallest instance, prove that instance separately and state why in one sentence (p. 10).
- (D8) End an extremal-problem paper with a table of the whole problem family, each cell marked with the optimal shape and value or "open" (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "We show that when the number is odd, the optimal solution to these three problems is identical, and arbitrarily close to a trapezoid." (p. 1)
- "The proof consists in constructing a new (2m+1)-unit-sided convex polygon with the same perimeter but with a smaller width than the original one." (p. 5)

### S117 · The small octagons of maximal width (Discrete & Computational Geometry 2013, 10.1007/s00454-013-9489-x)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen (GERAD/HEC Montréal), F. Messine (ENSEEIHT-IRIT), J. Ninin (ENSTA-Bretagne) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: this is the DCG 2013 entry of SKILL.md's polygon row. It answers the first open case, n = 8, of Bezdek and Fodor's (2000) maximal-width problem for unit-diameter polygons (pp. 1–2). Its equilateral sibling is S142 (2022, card s04). **Read**: all 12 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem was posed by others. For unit-diameter ("small") n-gons, find the maximal width. Bezdek and Fodor solved odd n, the Reuleaux-based cases and n = 4, and gave cos(π/2n) as a general upper bound (p. 2). The main result, Theorem 1.1, says:
  - the optimal width is ŵ = ¼√(10+2√7) ≈ 0.977608773427833;
  - it is attained by octagons whose diameter graph is a 5-cycle with three non-consecutive pending edges;
  - there are infinitely many optimal octagons, because two pending vertices can move without changing diameter or width (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The proof has two stages.
  1. Geometric and analytic lemmas cut the configuration space to two height-graph configurations (Prop. 2.6, p. 6) and allow each pending edge to be set to length one (Prop. 2.7, p. 7).
  2. The interval branch-and-bound code IBBA certifies the two remaining nonconvex problems (pp. 8–10). IBBA uses interval and affine arithmetic, constraint propagation, and an affine relaxation solved by CPLEX with safe bounds (p. 8).

  The general formulation (2) "cannot be solved directly", so the lemmas become constraints (pp. 9–10): bounds ŵ ≤ w ≤ 0.980693, side-length bounds, fixed diameters, fixed vertices v8 = (0,0) and v4 = (0,1), and a symmetry-breaking ordering of y-coordinates. The resulting model has 13 variables and 65 nonconvex constraints (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - The analysis assumes a convex octagon of unit diameter with width ≥ ŵ, and derives contradictions from that assumption (p. 3).
  - The claim is numerical: optimal to a "certified numerical accuracy of 1 × 10−7" (pp. 1, 10).
  - The certificate relies on IBBA accounting for all rounding errors, and on a safe-bounds method for the LP solver (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - *Bound chaining*: the paper combines two known results, max W at unit perimeter ≤ (1/16)cot(π/16) and max P at unit diameter ≤ 3.1211472. This gives W < 0.980693, which improves Bezdek–Fodor's cos(π/16) by almost 10⁻⁴ (pp. 3–4).
  - *Numeric contradictions*: Lemma 2.3 ends with 0.096 < … ≤ … < 0.089 (p. 5).
  - *WLOG symmetry steps*: these reduce the height graph to two cases (p. 6, Fig. 3).
  - *Reduction to a smaller n*: if moving a pending vertex reduced the width, a heptagon of width > ŵ would exist. That contradicts Bezdek–Fodor's heptagon optimum cos(π/14) < ŵ (p. 7).
  - *Certificate*:
    - case (3d) is proved infeasible in 1.40 s and 742 iterations;
    - case (3e) is bracketed in [0.97760877, 0.97760980] after one day;
    - adding w ≥ ŵ + 10⁻⁷ is then certified infeasible in 7 h 33 min (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: IBBA was run on an AMD Athlon II at 2.4 GHz with 2 GB of RAM, with the times above (p. 10). No other solver is compared.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - The contribution solves the n = 8 case with certified accuracy.
  - Among the optimal octagons, the one with maximal area is unique and symmetric, with area √2/2, equal to that of the regular small octagon (p. 10).
  - The next open case is n = 16 (p. 10).
  - Limits: the result holds to a tolerance of 10⁻⁷ rather than exactly. The authors note that the optimality proofs of the four octagon problems in the family "differ", so there is no general method (p. 11). No code or model release is mentioned anywhere in the paper.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the claim is fenced by a stated tolerance and an explicit certificate. The hypotheses are geometric, not smoothness assumptions, and no counterexamples are used (pp. 1, 10).
- Research Trajectory ✅ evidence: the polygon row (DCG 2009, 2013). The conclusion lists three earlier octagon results that were also "solved by combining geometric reasoning with the use of global optimization codes" (p. 11).
- Method 4 ⚠ contrast: S142 (2022) releases AMPL models so its certificate can be rerun. This paper releases nothing.
- new-pattern candidate: *analytic pruning before a global-solver certificate* (same name as in S142). The lemmas become bounds and constraints until IBBA closes each case (pp. 3–10).
- new-pattern candidate: *ε-uniqueness by exclusion constraint* (same device as S142). Here it is applied to the objective value (ε-optimality), not to the solution: w ≥ ŵ + 10⁻⁷ is certified infeasible (p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: *catalogue open cells, then fill them*. The paper answers Bezdek–Fodor's first open case and names the next one, n = 16 (pp. 1, 10).
- new-pattern candidate: *chain sibling extremal results into a new bound* (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Before computing, chain known results for sibling problems (max W | P = 1 and max P | D = 1) into a bound for your problem (max W | D = 1) (pp. 3–4).
- (D2) Turn every lemma into a bound or a constraint of the global-solver model. Fix vertices, add symmetry-breaking orderings, and report the final model size (13 variables, 65 constraints) (pp. 9–10).
- (D4) Certify an optimal value with two runs. First bracket it. Then add the constraint *objective ≥ claimed value + ε* and ask the solver for an infeasibility certificate (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "The proof combines geometric and analytical reasoning as well as the use of a recent version of the deterministic and reliable global optimization code IBBA based on interval and affine arithmetics." (p. 1)
- "Unfortunately, this general formulation of the problem cannot be solved directly in a reasonable amount of time." (p. 9)

### S066 · PSEUDOMARKER 2.0: efficient computation of likelihoods using NOMAD (BMC Bioinformatics 2014, 10.1186/1471-2105-15-47)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: E.M. Gertz (NCBI/NIH, first and corresponding author), T. Hiekkalinna, S. Le Digabel, J.D. Terwilliger, A.A. Schäffer (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: belongs to the *2010–2016 Applications and practical MADS* row. NOMAD enters a bioinformatics package, with "Thanks to John Dennis for stimulating this collaboration" (p. 8). Audet and Le Digabel "helped integrate NOMAD with FASTLINK, suggested tests, and edited the manuscript" (p. 8). **Read**: all 9 marker pages (marker 1 is the repository cover sheet). The additional files (Tables S1–S6) are not in the text.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a user's run-time bottleneck. PSEUDOMARKER's run time grows with the number of likelihood evaluations, and each evaluation is a pedigree peeling computation in ILINK (p. 3). Version 1 used GPS, and replacing it with an algorithm "known to outperform GPS in some other optimization problems" could cut the number of evaluations (p. 3). The problem is to maximize four likelihoods (p. 3):
  - over allele frequencies, which are probabilities in [0,1] summing to 1;
  - and a recombination fraction in [0, 0.5].
- **D2 Core idea**: GPS is replaced by NOMAD, called in library mode through a C++ callback class (p. 4). NOMAD is run with 2n orthogonal directions and its default quadratic models (VNS is not used), and stops when the minimum poll size is below 10⁻⁴ (p. 4).
  - *Constraints*: bounds are handled explicitly. The sum-to-one constraint is handled by representing one frequency implicitly. When that implicit frequency leaves [0,1], ILINK reports the point as infeasible and NOMAD discards it (extreme barrier) (p. 4).
  - *Invocation also changes*: NOMAD is called once per problem. GPS had been restarted from its own solution and from several initial points (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The blackbox is an exact likelihood computed by a generalization of Elston–Stewart peeling (p. 3). A logit reparametrization was rejected because maximum-likelihood estimates are often exactly zero, which would push a logit variable to −∞ and break NOMAD's convergence assumptions (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no analysis. Theory is used twice: to motivate MADS over GPS (its dense directions give stronger convergence properties, p. 4), and as a criterion for rejecting a reformulation (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - *Data*: 14 data sets, real Finnish pedigrees and simulated ones (Tables 1–2, p. 5).
  - *Evaluation counts* (12 sets, dominant and recessive models, all four likelihoods): NOMAD uses fewer evaluations on all 12, e.g. x.linked 470,517 → 140,986 and fin3 81,887 → 10,765 (Table 3, p. 7).
  - *Solution quality* (288 problems): NOMAD is worse by ≥ 0.005 in log-likelihood 7 times and better 68 times. Its largest gain is 28 and its largest loss 0.1 (p. 6; Table 4, p. 7).
  - *Wall-clock on two genome scans*: FHS 88 h 24 → 21 h 45 (4.1×) and FinnTwin12 38 h 05 → 6 h 08 (6.2×, mean of 10 repeats) (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is software: PSEUDOMARKER 2.0 uses NOMAD by default, with files to rebuild against another NOMAD under LGPL 3.0 (p. 4). It also introduces NOMAD to bioinformatics and genetic epidemiology (p. 7). The limits are stated honestly:
  - the gain mixes algorithm and invocation protocol (p. 6);
  - there are "no obvious patterns" for when NOMAD wins (p. 6);
  - the robustness claim rests on preliminary tests "(data not shown)" (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: NOMAD in library mode inside another group's package, shipped by default in the binaries, with an LGPL rebuild path (pp. 4, 8).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: bounds are kept as bounds. The sum-to-one equality is handled by an implicit variable plus the extreme barrier. A reparametrization that sends boundary optima to infinity is refused (pp. 4, 7).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant:
  - it follows the method in counting likelihood evaluations as the "apples-to-apples" currency, adding wall-clock on real scans, and setting a threshold for substantive differences (p. 6);
  - but GPS and NOMAD run under different invocation protocols, and there are no profiles (p. 6).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (weak): convergence hypotheses are used as a modelling criterion to reject the logit transform (p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate: *implicit variable + extreme barrier for a known linear equality* (p. 4). Reader's inference: this is the one-equality special case of S064's BN converter.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) For a sum-to-one or other single linear equality, optimize over n−1 variables, compute the last one implicitly, and let the evaluator reject points where it leaves its bounds (p. 4).
- (D5) Before counting wins and losses, fix what counts as a substantive difference (0.005 in log-likelihood, i.e. ≤ 0.01 in the log likelihood ratio), then report counts at several thresholds (p. 6; Table 4, p. 7).
- (D3) Before reparametrizing to remove bounds (logit, exp), check whether optima sit on the boundary. If they do, the transform sends them to infinity (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "As we discuss below, NOMAD is invoked somewhat differently from GPS on the same optimization problems" (p. 6)
- "This case breaks the assumptions of the theoretical convergence theory of NOMAD [6], and poses practical problems for the implementation." (p. 7)

### S134 · A note on bimatrix game maximal Selten subsets (Arabian Journal of Mathematics 2014, 10.1007/s40065-014-0101-x)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Belhaiza (first author, KFUPM; the work was partly done during a stay at GERAD), P. Hansen (pp. 1, 13) · **Position in trajectory**: continues the game-equilibrium enumeration line of SKILL.md's *1997–2002 Exact global optimization … game equilibria* row. The line runs through EEE (2001), Eχ-MIP (2006), a sequence-form paper (2009) and an Automatica paper on proper refinement (2012) (refs, p. 13), so it extends to 2014. **Read**: all 13 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper extends the group's own enumeration algorithms from Nash equilibria to a refinement. It wants all extreme perfect equilibria, all maximal Selten subsets (sets of interchangeable perfect equilibria) and their dimensions (pp. 1, 3). The motivation is that the number of extreme equilibria can be very large, which is why refinements exist (pp. 2–3). The novelty claim is that computing all maximal Selten subsets "was not achieved before" (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: A four-step pipeline.
  1. Enumerate the extreme equilibria. Build the interchangeability graph; maximal Nash subsets are its maximal cliques, found with Bron–Kerbosch (C++, in the XGame1.0 package) (p. 3).
  2. Compute each subset's dimension with Jansen's rank formula at a relative-interior point, obtained as a convex combination of the subset's extreme points, with ranks computed in exact arithmetic (p. 4).
  3. Test perfectness. Van Damme's characterization (no weakly dominating mixed strategy) becomes a pair of LPs that maximize slack sums, merged into one LP Q: an equilibrium is perfect iff Q's optimum is 0 (Def. 3.3, Prop. 3.6, pp. 5–7).
  4. Run Eχ-MIPerfect and EEE-Perfect, which call Q at each new extreme equilibrium (pp. 7–11).

  An implementation detail: all branching fixings are encoded in two aggregated rows (4.2)–(4.4), instead of n+m single-entry rows that add slacks and degeneracy (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Bimatrix games; the mixed 0–1 program uses big-M constants "adequately chosen" (p. 2).
  - The Selten-subset dimension formula needs A, B > 0, obtained by adding 1+|min| to every entry (p. 6).
  - All computations use exact arithmetic (pp. 4, 6, 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - The LP characterization is argued in both directions for Definition 3.3. The LPs are always feasible (take x = x̂ with zero slack). A positive optimum means some slack is positive, which means weak domination (p. 6).
  - The written proof of Prop. 3.6 covers only the direction from a zero optimum to perfectness; the converse rests on the p. 6 argument (p. 7).
  - Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 (finite enumeration) have two-sentence proofs: every way of satisfying the complementarity conditions is explored (pp. 9, 11).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - *Setup*: 30 random games for each size (2×2 to 7×7) and density (25–100%), on Windows XP with an i5 at 3.3 GHz (pp. 11–12).
  - *Reported*: numbers of extreme and perfect equilibria, the perfect percentage, Selten subsets, dimension, and time for both algorithms (Table 4, p. 12).
  - *Timing*: EEE-Perfect is much faster, e.g. 7×7 at 25% density takes 17,402 s with Eχ-MIPerfect vs 2,411 s with EEE-Perfect (p. 12). Sparse games are the slowest (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is procedures, two algorithms and computational evidence that the share of perfect equilibria grows with density (pp. 11, 13). Correctness is shown only on the Borm et al. example (pp. 3–7). Eχ-MIP is slower but is the only algorithm the authors know of for polymatrix games. The stated future work is to extend EEE to polymatrix games and to improve the implementation (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Research Trajectory ⚠ variant: the game-equilibria row is dated 1997–2002, but this line continues to 2014 with the same GERAD coauthors (pp. 1, 13).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: correctness is stated as finite-enumeration theorems with path-existence proofs, and exact arithmetic avoids floating-point doubts (pp. 4, 9, 11). No hypotheses are audited and no counterexamples are used.
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: the code is shipped as a package (XGame1.0), but it is a separate tool, not NOMAD (p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: *definition-to-LP certificate*: a definition of the form *no improving object exists* becomes an LP whose optimum is zero iff the property holds (pp. 5–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Turn an existence or non-existence definition into an LP with a zero-optimum test. Show the LP is always feasible by exhibiting a trivial solution (p. 6).
- (D2) To evaluate a rank-based dimension formula on a polytope known only by its vertices, take a strict convex combination of the vertices as the relative-interior point and compute ranks in exact arithmetic (p. 4).
- (D2) In LP-based branching, encode all current fixings in two aggregated rows instead of one row per fixed variable (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "To the best of our knowledge, the implementation of computational methods to enumerate all maximal Selten subsets of bimatrix games was not achieved before." (p. 5)
- "The results also show that the percentage of extreme perfect equilibria increases with an increase in the bimatrix games density." (p. 11)

### S145 · Replacement scheduling of a fleet of hydroelectric generators: a case study (International Journal of Performability Engineering 10(6):615–630, 2014, no DOI in list)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: H. Aoudjit (corresponding author; post-doc, PhD 2010 at EPM), M.-S. Ouali (EPM, industrial engineering) (pp. 1, 16) · **Position in trajectory**: belongs to the *2010–2016 Applications* row, and thanks the Hydro-Québec Reliability and Performance Team (p. 14). Card s01's S204 appears to report the same Problems I/II with the same Lindo→NOMAD relay, according to the s01 digest. **Read**: all 16 pages. Tables and regression output were read; plots survive only as axis labels.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: This is a practitioner's problem.
  - The fleet is aging, and the standard age-replacement policy cannot be applied because many units reach the replacement age together: one plant holds 38 units installed "roughly the same year" (p. 1).
  - Current plans come from know-how and heuristics "without any evaluation of performances relative to the optimality" (pp. 1–2).
  - The paper argues for formulating the objective independently of the optimization technique and treating it as a black box (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A chain of models.
  1. A load model: linear regressions of starts and cumulative load, from hourly data on 14 of the 90 units (pp. 4–5).
  2. A proportional-hazards failure model with cumulative load as covariate. Kumar–Westberg's time-dependent extension is used after Schoenfeld residuals reject proportionality (pp. 6–8).
  3. A per-component age-replacement cost rate from the renewal-reward theorem (eq. 9), summed over components and units, so the objective is separable (pp. 8–9).
  4. An NLP over replacement periods τ with organisational constraints, checked by filling an n×t status matrix (pp. 9–11).

  The NLP is solved by NOMAD and by Lindo API (successive linear programming / generalized reduced gradient) (p. 10). Two plan indices are added: AURI (reliability of the units at withdrawal) and AFRI (reliability of the units still operating) (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - A withdrawal replaces all components of a unit (pp. 2, 11), and a unit's commissioning date is its oldest component's (p. 11).
  - Cumulative load is the only predictor, with a constant base hazard (p. 7).
  - Costs are approximate, and failure cost is set to 2× or 1.5× the preventive cost (pp. 11–12).
  - The horizon is 50 years with 38-week overhauls (p. 12).
  - The starting plan (replace at the 50-year depreciation age) is infeasible (p. 12).
  - Constraint outputs are −1 when a constraint is satisfied (p. 11), but how they are passed to NOMAD (EB or PB) is not stated.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no analysis. Only the classical first-order condition for the unconstrained age-replacement optimum is given (eq. 10, p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: Two problems on 90 units, single runs, no seeds.
  - *Problem I*: NOMAD finds no feasible plan with 10⁴ and then 5·10⁵ evaluations. Lindo returns a feasible but not optimal plan. NOMAD restarted from it lowers J from 7.709 to 7.566 k$/h using 5·10⁴ evaluations in 33 min (pp. 12–13, Table 7).
  - *Problem II* (+50% load): the same relay gives 9.799 → 9.562 k$/h (p. 13, Table 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an application framework plus the plan indices (p. 14). The findings (pp. 13–14):
  - a 50% load increase brings withdrawals about 10 years earlier;
  - AFRI drops by 5.17%.

  Limits and future work: only a sample of 90 units was used; the approach is "limited only by the ability of nonlinear solvers"; future work covers the whole fleet, other policies, and a biobjective cost-vs-AFRI version (p. 14). There is an inconsistency: p. 14 says "when doubling the load", but the scenario is a 50% increase (pp. 11, 13–14).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (as for S204): the constraints are quantifiable (Table 7 prints crews, MW and M$ values) and x0 is infeasible. The EB/PB choice is not stated, and no progressive-barrier or LH-search remedy is tried when NOMAD cannot reach feasibility (pp. 11–12).
- Taste warning sign 5 ✅ evidence (my inference, not drawn by the paper): τ has 90 variables, and the gradient-based SLP/GRG code reached feasibility where NOMAD did not (pp. 9, 12).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: evaluation counts and times are reported, but the solvers are chained, not compared, and each is run once (pp. 12–13).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: Hydro-Québec data are used and nothing is released (p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:

### S064 · Linear equalities in blackbox optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2015, 10.1007/s10589-014-9708-2)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Le Digabel, M. Peyrega (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: this is the *linear equalities (COAP 2015)* cited in SKILL.md under Method 2 and Heuristic 9. The text is the article reprinted "avec permission" as Chapter 4 of a thesis (p. 1). The header does not name the thesis author; that it is Peyrega's (PhD 2016 in SKILL.md's Downstream list) is my inference. **Read**: all 24 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a gap in the group's own method: MADS supports equalities "neither in theory nor in practice" (p. 1). The scope is linear equalities Ax = 0 with A known and of full row rank (p. 1). Motivation: one chemical-engineering example, and inexact-restoration methods whose subproblems carry linear equalities (p. 2). The naive baseline, splitting each equality into two inequalities, is rejected on two counts (p. 4):
  - *Theory*: the hypertangent cone is open, so with equalities it is empty everywhere, and only MADS's most basic result survives.
  - *Practice*: almost all trial points fall outside the nullspace. MADS finds no feasible point on any test problem.
- **D2 Core idea**: A wrapper plus a "converter", a surjective linear map ϕ: R^nz → null(A) (Def. 4.3.1, p. 5).
  - *Wrapper*: MADS runs on z. The bounds L ≤ ϕ(z) ≤ U are checked before the blackbox runs, and violating points get +∞ at no simulation cost (p. 6, Fig. 4.1). LP preprocessing gives bounds on z, which are used for scaling (p. 7).
  - *Four converters* (pp. 11–13):
    - orthogonal projection (nz = nx, not bijective);
    - QR;
    - SVD;
    - basic/nonbasic partition, BN (nz = nx − m).

  MADS itself is not modified. On top of the converters come a BN partition heuristic based on the starting point's distance to the bounds and on condition numbers (p. 19), and a two-phase algorithm (pp. 20–22):
  - Phase 1 runs SVD to learn which variables move toward their bounds and at what scale.
  - Phase 2 runs BN with the partition and scaling derived from Phase 1.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - A has full row rank, and F and C may return +∞ to model simulation failures (p. 2).
  - The standard MADS assumptions hold: bounded iterates and asymptotically dense normalized poll directions (p. 10).
  - F is locally Lipschitz near x* (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 4.3.5 shows that hypertangent cones commute with the converter: T^H_Ωx(ϕ(z)) = ϕ(T^H_Ωz(z)). The proof is a double inclusion, using continuity for one inclusion and the open mapping theorem for the other (pp. 8–9).
  - Theorem 4.3.6 then transports MADS's refined-point result: F°(x*; v) ≥ 0 for every v hypertangent relative to the nullspace S (p. 10). To allow this, the Clarke derivative and the hypertangent cone are defined in a normed space that may be a strict subspace (pp. 7–8).
  - A contingent-KKT corollary is sketched under strict differentiability and regularity (pp. 10–11).
  - Reading note: the statement of Thm 4.3.5 names the spaces in swapped order relative to its formula (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - *Problems*: 10 CUTEst problems (6 from Hock–Schittkowski), with n = nx − m from 2 to 40 and 100 random feasible starts each, i.e. 1,000 instances (pp. 14–15).
  - *Protocol*: budget 100(n+1), data profiles at α = 1% (p. 14). NOMAD 3.6.0 runs with models disabled; HOPSPACK 2.0.2 (GSS) runs with its tolerance set to 1E-13 to match NOMAD's (p. 15).
  - *BN study*: an exhaustive run of all 12,464 feasible (B, N) partitions of HS119 (pp. 16–18).
  - *Results*:
    - the projection converter is dominated;
    - HOPSPACK leads early, but by log inspection only on the smallest problems;
    - SVD:50–BN:50 is best (pp. 19–23);
    - a four-phase variant did worse, and those results are not reported (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a theorem that transports the MADS convergence result, a problem wrapper, and a practical rule: spend the first 50 groups of n+1 evaluations in Phase 1 (pp. 23–24). The limits are that only linear equalities are handled and only 10 analytic problems are tested. The results are similar to HOPSPACK on small and medium problems and better on the larger ones (p. 24). Future work: integration into NOMAD, linear inequalities, and use inside inexact restoration (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: equalities are a separate constraint class with their own mechanism, and bounds on ϕ(z) are a priori constraints that skip the simulation (pp. 4, 6).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the naive reformulation is rejected by a theory argument (an empty hypertangent cone). The Clarke ladder is carried over, with a KKT corollary (pp. 4, 10–11). No counterexamples are built.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: MADS is left untouched, but the new idea lives in a problem wrapper rather than in the Search step. So the proof is transported by a linear map rather than protected by the Poll (pp. 6, 10).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the budget is counted in groups of n+1, with data profiles and 100 starts per problem. The competitor comes from another direct-search framework (GSS) with an aligned tolerance, and the logs are inspected to explain the crossover (pp. 14–19).
- Heuristic 9 ✅ evidence: exploit known linear structure and keep the direct-search backbone (pp. 1, 24).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: NOMAD integration is future work here (p. 24). SKILL.md's current NOMAD route for equalities (EQPB with Mads-PIP) is a different mechanism.
- new-pattern candidate: *exhaustive enumeration on a small instance as ground truth*. All 12,464 partitions of HS119 are run; final value is plotted against cond(B), then stratified by |I_N ∩ A| (pp. 16–18). S060 does the same kind of thing.
- new-pattern candidate: *phase-1 run as a diagnostic for phase-2 configuration* (pp. 20–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) If your reformulation is a linear surjection, prove that the tangent object your theory needs (here the hypertangent cone) maps onto its counterpart. Then transport the existing convergence theorem instead of re-proving it (pp. 8–10).
- (D2) Check cheap a priori constraints in the wrapper and return +∞ without calling the simulator (p. 6).
- (D5) To understand a discrete design choice, enumerate all its values on one instance and plot final quality against a candidate diagnostic (here the condition number). Then split the plot by a structural quantity to reveal the second factor (pp. 16–18).
- (D2) Spend part of the budget with a robust configuration and read its trajectory (|x⁰_i − x¹_i|, distance to bounds) to pick the partition and the scaling for the remaining budget (pp. 20–22).

**Quotes**:
- "Currently, MADS does not support equalities, neither in theory nor in practice." (p. 1)
- "In addition, with such reformulations, MADS fails to find a feasible point for all the problems tested in Section 4.5." (p. 4)

### S032 · Stochastic short-term hydropower planning with inflow scenario trees (European Journal of Operational Research 2017, 10.1016/j.ejor.2016.11.028)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Séguin (first and corresponding author), S.-E. Fleten and A. Pichler (NTNU), P. Côté (Rio Tinto) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: belongs to the *2017–2022* row, with Rio Tinto as funder (p. 29). It extends the Séguin–Côté–Audet 2016 deterministic model, ref. [5] (p. 5). Its future work, on scenario-tree complexity and fans, is exactly S060's question (p. 29). **Read**: all 36 pages. Figures survive only as axis labels, and the notation appendix was skimmed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Short-term hydropower models are usually deterministic, or they neglect head variation. Few study stochastic short-term operation with head dependence (pp. 2–3, 5). Only inflow uncertainty matters here, because Quebec producers other than Hydro-Québec do not bid on spot markets (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: The paper chains a scenario generator, a tree builder and a two-phase optimization model.
  - *Scenario fan* (p. 6):
    - the 7-day precipitation forecast is split into 3 + 4 days;
    - 7 analog forecasts are drawn from 15 years of history for each part, giving 7² = 49 combinations;
    - these are combined with 62 years of realizations, giving 3,038 scenarios;
    - the scenarios are passed through the CEQUEAU hydrological model.
  - *Scenario tree* by Pflug–Pichler's nested-distance method (pp. 7–12):
    - the structure is fixed and the tree initialized by k-means;
    - each iteration draws a new path by conditional kernel density sampling (logistic kernel, Silverman bandwidth);
    - it then updates the closest tree path, chosen greedily child by child, with step size 1/(p+30)^{3/4};
    - node probabilities come from visit counts.
  - *Two-phase multistage model* (pp. 13–18, 20):
    - loading is a continuous relaxation solved by IPOPT, whose relaxed surface choice gives integer solutions per [5];
    - unit commitment is a linear integer program solved by XPRESS, with a start-up penalty.
  - *End-of-horizon water*: a water-value function is used only for Lac-St-Jean; the small reservoirs get full-reservoir targets (p. 19).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Forecast errors in the two day groups are independent, and the forecast "has no value after 7 days" (p. 6).
  - Relaxing the surface choice is assumed to yield integer solutions, as shown in [5] (p. 16).
  - Convergence of the stochastic approximation is asserted from step-size conditions (p. 11), and consistency of the probabilities from the law of large numbers (p. 12). Neither is proved here.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: None of its own. Convergence is delegated to [24, 28] (pp. 7, 11).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - *Protocol*: a 31-day rolling-horizon backtest on 6 monthly cases, with 5–7 stages × 16, 32 or 48 scenarios, against a rolling median scenario (pp. 19–23).
  - *Results*: the stochastic model is better in 4 cases, the median in October, and August is mixed (p. 22; Table 1, p. 23). Gains are 0.025–0.16%, about 10.9 M$ for June at 20,000 $/GWh (p. 22).
  - *Cost*: under 42 s per day (p. 21).
  - *Stability*: an in-sample test regenerates 6 random trees for June and July (Table 2, p. 24).
  - *Reporting issue*: the July "variance 0.9308 TW h" (p. 25) is inconsistent with the Table 2 spread of 0.1916–0.2213 TWh.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a stochastic extension of the model plus nonparametric scenario trees built from real data (pp. 5, 28–29). The limits are stated:
  - when the median forecast is close to the realization, as in October, stochastic modelling gains nothing, and the authors add a caution (pp. 27–28);
  - a 50-plant system would take about 350 minutes and would need decomposition (pp. 21–22).

  Future work: the complexity of the tree structure, and scenario fans (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the paper uses a real partner system with data provided by Rio Tinto (pp. 18–19, 29), and nothing is released.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: a rolling-horizon backtest against the simplest operational baseline, with water left at the end valued so that consuming more is not rewarded (p. 26), plus an in-sample stability test (p. 24). However, Table 1 uses one tree per configuration (pp. 22–23).
- Research Trajectory ✅ evidence: Rio Tinto as funder and partner in the 2017–2022 row (p. 29).
- Scope note: no DFO appears in this paper. Audet's contribution cannot be identified from the text.
- new-pattern candidate: *challenge the sophisticated model with the simplest operational baseline*: the rolling median scenario, which S060 extends to fans (pp. 19–20, 27–28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Evaluate a stochastic planning model by rolling-horizon backtest. Re-solve every day, implement only the first stage, update the state with realized data, and convert leftover resources into value so that methods which consume more are not favoured (pp. 19–20, 26).
- (D5) Run an in-sample stability test for a randomized input generator: regenerate the input several times with the same settings and report the spread of the objective (p. 24).
- (D5) Explain the case where your method loses, using the data. In October the median forecast is close to the realization (pp. 27–28, Fig. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Few papers have looked specifically into short-term hydropower models with uncertain inflows." (p. 2)
- "Despite the low percentages, this represents huge savings for the producer." (p. 22)

### S060 · Scenario-tree modeling for stochastic short-term hydropower operations planning (Journal of Water Resources Planning and Management 2017, 10.1061/(asce)wr.1943-5452.0000854)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Séguin (first author), P. Côté (GERAD & Rio Tinto) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the direct follow-up of S032. It answers S032's future-work question and is a Heuristic 7 case that cites OPAL (Audet, Dang & Orban 2014) (p. 3). **Read**: all 14 pages. Histograms and boxplots survive only as axis labels.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: S032's tree generator has three parameters: number of stages, child nodes per stage, and days aggregated per stage. The question is whether complex trees are worth their cost in a rolling horizon (pp. 1–3). Three approaches are compared: tuned trees, the median scenario, and scenario fans (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: The whole 31-day rolling horizon becomes one blackbox RH(α, κ, D). It covers tree generation, loading, unit commitment and reservoir updates, and its output is energy plus end-of-horizon water value (pp. 3–7, Figs 1 and 3). NOMAD tunes α (child nodes) and κ (aggregation) for a fixed D (p. 7).
  - The constraints are Σκ_r = 29, 2 ≤ Πα_q ≤ 50, and integrality (p. 7).
  - How the equality (6) and the product constraint (7) were passed to NOMAD is not stated.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - The blackbox is stochastic, because tree generation is random (p. 7).
  - Evaluation cost grows with tree size, not with the number of variables: 32 min for a 52-node tree, 1 h for a 162-node tree (p. 7).
  - Constraint (8) is written "αq, κr > 1" but described as nonnegativity, and it is violated by the α = (1, 1, 2, 1) used in the validation (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no theory. Validation takes its place:
  - *Noise census*: 100 evaluations at fixed parameters give a standard deviation of 7.4622 GWh against a mean of 7.0478×10⁵ GWh (p. 8).
  - *Exhaustive enumeration*: all 331 feasible α (27 h) vs NOMAD (57 evaluations, best found after 23, 7 h). Both reach 804.63 TWh, but with different α (p. 8).
  - *Signal vs noise*: the improvement over the start (0.17 TWh) is set against the noise (7.4 GWh) (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - *Setup*: 6 data series and D = 5–9 stages (pp. 8–11). Two tuning settings are compared with the median scenario:
    - (a) the tree structure only;
    - (b) the structure and the aggregation.
  - *Fans*: scenario fans of 3–25 scenarios are also tested (pp. 11–12).
  - *Noise band*: boxplots carry a ±16 GWh (2σ) band (pp. 11–12).
  - *Findings*:
    - with fixed aggregation, the number of stages barely matters; aggregation does matter (p. 11);
    - fans with more than 15 scenarios match the tuned trees (p. 12);
    - the paper's reading of a t-test between 15 and 25 scenarios is ambiguous (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a method (blackbox tuning of a scenario pipeline) and a result against complexity. The first-day decision depends mostly on total inflow volume, so fans suffice at lower cost (pp. 11–13). The limits:
  - with fans, only the first-node solution is meaningful (p. 13);
  - because of noise, NOMAD's answer cannot be confirmed as global (pp. 8–9);
  - there is one system of three plants (p. 8).

  Writing slips: in this maximization the objective is said to be "substantially decreased" (p. 8), and the best point "truly is a minimum" (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence: the parameters of a data-to-decision pipeline are tuned by NOMAD, explicitly following the OPAL methodology (pp. 3, 5–7).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: noise is measured before optimizing and used as a significance band, exhaustive enumeration serves as ground truth, and evaluation counts and times are reported (pp. 7–9, 11–12). No other solver is compared.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the blackbox problem has an equality and a product constraint, and their treatment is not stated (p. 7). Contrast S064, which is devoted to equalities.
- Research Taste ✅ evidence: the paper concedes that the simpler alternative (fans) is as good as the tuned complex one (pp. 12–13).
- new-pattern candidate: *exhaustive enumeration on a small instance as ground truth* (p. 8; also S064).
- new-pattern candidate: *replicate-at-a-point noise census, then 2σ band* (pp. 7–8, 11–12).
- new-pattern candidate: *challenge the sophisticated model with the simplest operational baseline* (pp. 9–13; also S032).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Before optimizing a stochastic blackbox, evaluate one point 100 times and report the mean and σ. Draw ±2σ on every later comparison plot (pp. 7–8, 11).
- (D5) On a sub-case small enough to enumerate (331 points), compare the DFO solver's answer and cost with the exhaustive optimum (p. 8).
- (D2) Treat the whole operational loop (generate scenarios → optimize → implement the first stage → update the state) as one blackbox whose inputs are the modelling choices (pp. 3–7).

**Quotes**:
- "Numerical experiments on three hydropower plants in series suggest that using a set of scenario trees is preferable to using the median scenario, but using a fan of scenarios yields a comparable solution with less computational effort." (p. 1)
- "Computational time required to evaluate the blackbox increases with the complexity of the scenario trees, rather than with the number of optimization variables." (p. 7)
