### S015 · Pooling problem: Alternate formulations and solution methods (Management Science 2004, 10.1287/mnsc.1030.0207)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. Brimberg, P. Hansen, S. Le Digabel, N. Mladenović · **Position in trajectory**: Received July 19, 2002, with the authors 1 month for 2 revisions (p. 1). Audet's support is an NSERC fellowship (PDF-207432-1998) and the CRPC, the Center for Research on Parallel Computation (p. 15). The project was funded by Ultramar Canada (p. 15). The exact solver is the authors' own branch-and-cut code for nonconvex QCQP (Audet, Hansen, Jaumard, Savard, Math. Programming 2000; pp. 8, 16). In SKILL.md's Research Trajectory this is the tail of the 1997–2002 exact global-optimization period. The VNS here, written with the VNS originators Hansen and Mladenović (cited p. 12), precedes the VNS Search inside MADS (JOGO 2008, listed in SKILL.md). The co-author Le Digabel is later the NOMAD co-lead (SKILL.md, Academic Lineage). **Read**: all 16 pages, appendix included (p. 15). The reference list (pp. 15–16) was skimmed. Text-file caveats: Table 7 (p. 14) is extracted as one run-on line and is only partly readable; Tables 3–6 (pp. 9–13) lost their decimal points; the formulas of Figures 1–2 (pp. 5–6) are partly garbled but readable with the surrounding text. The paper has no conclusion section; it ends with the observation bullets of §4.3.2 (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is first stated in words, as a question: given feed availabilities, how much to mix in intermediate pools so that the final blends meet attribute requirements while maximizing revenue minus feed cost (p. 1). The classical blending problem is an LP; intermediate pools make it a bilinear program, a special nonconvex QCQP (p. 1). BLP is strongly NP-hard and even finding a feasible point is NP-hard (p. 2). The entry is not a new algorithm but a question of fit: how best to apply the authors' own recent branch-and-cut QP algorithm to this application class (abstract, p. 1). Three gaps are named: Haverly's recursion and SLP, the industrial standard since Shell (1961) and Exxon (1985), only reach local optima (pp. 2, 8); the Al-Khayyal–Falk branch-and-bound is very sensitive to variable bounds (p. 3); and larger instances need heuristics, both to be solved approximately and to give exact methods a starting incumbent (p. 3). The BLP is written generically with a split into linear, noncomplicating and complicating variables, obtained from a minimal transversal of the co-occurrence graph of bilinear terms (pp. 1–2).
  - Transferable technique: when you own a general-purpose solver, write an application paper that asks which formulation of the class suits your solver and what must feed it, instead of presenting yet another solver.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Four moves.
  - *Formulation as the main lever.* The flow model (flow + pool attribute variables t, after Haverly and Foulds et al.) is compared with the proportion model (flow + inflow-proportion variables q, after Ben-Tal et al.) (§2.1–2.2, pp. 4–6). Nonlinear variables: nA·nP + |Y| in the flow model (p. 4) versus |W| − nP + |Y| in the proportion model, independent of the number of attributes (p. 5). The crossover for bilinear variables is at nA = |W|/nP − 1, the average number of inflows per pool minus one (p. 6). In both models, fixing one block of the bilinear pair leaves an LP (pp. 4–5).
  - *Hybrid for the generalized problem.* When pools feed pools, the proportion model gains q·q terms and stops being bilinear, so the hybrid uses flow variables for pools fed by other pools and proportions elsewhere (§2.4, p. 6).
  - *Instance reduction before solving* (§2.5, pp. 7–9; see D4).
  - *Structure-exploiting heuristics.* ALT alternately fixes the complicating or the noncomplicating block and solves the LP in the rest (Fig. 6, p. 11). The paper sets it beside Haverly's recursion, which fixes both flow blocks, updates t directly from the pool equalities, and in its original form does not extend to the proportion model, whereas ALT applies to both models (p. 11). VNS keeps the Hansen–Mladenović skeleton (shake, local search, move or not) but defines its neighborhoods by LP bases: N_k(s) is the set of extreme points k basis exchanges away in one of the two LP polyhedra, a random point is drawn, ALT restarts in the other subspace, and the subspace alternates at each shake (Fig. 7, p. 12).
  - What is not changed: the exact algorithm of Audet et al. (2000) is used as is. Its four improvements over Al-Khayyal–Falk and RLT are recalled: a branching value that minimizes the resulting error rather than the midpoint, only violated envelope inequalities added, cuts from underapproximating a convex paraboloid, and cuts valid at every node (pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable technique: for a bilinear (or block-multiconvex) problem, build the local search from the blocks (fix one, solve the easy problem in the other) and the diversification from the easy problem's combinatorics (basis exchanges), so that no continuous step size has to be tuned.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Attribute qualities blend linearly by volume. More precise octane and distillation models (with logarithms or fourth-order terms) exist but are excluded because of their complexity (p. 4); this is a convenience assumption and the paper says so. Each intermediate pool has at least two inflows and two outflows, otherwise it is merged away (p. 3). Flow conservation removes one inflow variable per pool (index i_j, p. 4). Prop. 4.1 assumes a unique LP solution at every ALT step and a bounded feasible region, which a well-formulated pooling problem has (p. 11). The exact runs use an absolute tolerance of 10⁻⁶ (p. 9). Random instances draw their parameters within intervals derived from feasibility requirements (p. 15); the generator is not described further.
  - Transferable technique: when you drop a more realistic physical model for tractability, name the model you dropped and say why (here: the functional form that makes it intractable), in one sentence next to the modeling assumption.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The only formal result is Prop. 4.1: under unique LP solutions, ALT converges to a local optimum, which the statement itself defines as a point where varying (x, y) or (x, z) does not improve the objective (p. 11). Proof skeleton, four sentences (p. 11): every LP stays feasible, so the objective sequence is monotone; the region is bounded, so the values converge; no ties, so there is a unique attraction point; the two subspaces allow no local ascent. Reader's note: the step from convergent values to a unique attraction point is asserted from the no-ties assumption rather than derived. Other devices:
  - *Without-loss-of-generality flow transfer.* A flow that can be rerouted onto unconstrained arcs without changing feasibility or objective is fixed to 0. In RT1 this cascades: pools become single-output, merge with a blend or a feed, and the instance becomes an LP (pp. 7–8, Fig. 4).
  - *Unit-cost decomposition.* In Foulds et al.'s F3–F5, the cost of a unit of blend with attribute α is 30 − 10α whichever feeds are blended, so only the two extreme feeds are needed and the optimum is analytic (pp. 8–9, Fig. 5).
  - *Symmetry breaking.* In RT2 every feed reaches every pool and every pool every blend, so one may assume the larger pool carries at least the flow of the smaller (appendix, p. 15).
  - *Size accounting.* Table 1 counts linear variables, bilinear variables split by block, bilinear terms and L≤/Q≤/Q= constraints per model (p. 7), with one worked count (RT2 flow model: 4×3 + 4×3 = 24 bilinear terms, p. 6).
  - *Combinatorial neighborhood metric.* ρ(s1, s2) = k when the two basic sets differ in k elements (symmetric difference), and |N_1(s)| ≤ 2n1 + n2 + n3 with equality for nondegenerate LPs (p. 12). Reader's note: as a statement about adjacent extreme points the bound needs nondegeneracy; the paper later blames degeneracy of the AST instances for inefficient shaking (p. 15).
  - The authors flag the open theory themselves: the extreme-point discretization depends on the current solution, and unlike combinatorial optimization the global optimum need not lie in the union of the N_k(s) (p. 12).
  - No complexity or rate results; NP-hardness is only cited (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: before running any solver on a literature instance, try dominance/rerouting arguments that fix variables to zero; report the instances that collapse to an LP or a closed form.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Exact solver.* C++ on a Sun Ultra-60 (360 MHz, LINPACK 10 Mflops/s), absolute tolerance 10⁻⁶ (p. 9). 12 literature instances from five sources plus the authors' generalized instance GP1, 13 in all, with their optimal values (Table 3, p. 9). Reported per run: preprocessing time, tree time, total, nodes, and variables and constraints added by the algorithm (p. 9).
  - *Two-execution protocol.* Each instance is run from scratch, then again with the optimum fed in plus a constraint bounding the objective, to measure the cost of the optimality proof alone, i.e., what a perfect heuristic would buy (p. 9).
  - *Failures are shown.* AST2 and AST3 under the flow model hit the memory limit (Table 4(i), pp. 9–10). For AST4, proving optimality costs more than solving from scratch (p. 9). For the proportion model of AST and RT2, the added objective constraint makes the region small and hard to approximate by outer approximations (p. 9).
  - *Formulation findings.* The proportion model is significantly easier for the larger instances, even for BT5 where it has more bilinear variables and terms, but fewer bilinear constraints, 11 versus 16 (p. 9). For GP1 the hybrid seems easiest (p. 10); in the extracted Table 4(v) the node counts are readable (flow 47, proportion 35, hybrid 27), the time columns lost their decimals (p. 10).
  - *Cross-study comparison.* Table 5 lists CPU times of four earlier algorithms, each on its own machine (p. 11). The text concedes that Adhya et al. (1999) is faster on the easy instances and reports raw and CPU-speed-adjusted speed-ups on AST1–AST3 (p. 10); the extracted range lost its decimals (reader's reading against Table 5: about 4.6–28.2 raw and 1.7–10.4 adjusted). Reader's note: AST4, the fourth Adhya et al. instance, is in Table 5 (p. 11) but not in the text's speed-up sentence; its entries (182 for Adhya et al.; 17798 for Audet et al., i.e. 2.83 + 175.15 = 177.98 s by the sum in Table 4(iii)) show no advantage once the CPU-speed ratio is applied, wherever the lost decimal of 182 belongs.
  - *Heuristics.* SLPR (Palacios-Gomez et al. 1982), ALT and VNS, all in C++ on the same machine (p. 12); multistart versions MSLP and MALT; VNS starts from the best MALT point and its reported time includes MALT's (p. 13). Metrics: value, CPU time, % error against the exact optimum (Table 6, p. 13). The parameters nsp, kmax, nt are set per instance, and kmax, nt are set to 0 when VNS cannot improve (p. 13). Heuristics show no significant flow-vs-proportion difference except AST3 and F2 (p. 13), in contrast to the exact solver.
  - *Feasibility of starts is measured.* For RT2, 0 of 10,000 random proportion vectors led ALT to a feasible point; two tricks raise this to about 25%: zero each variable with probability 0.5 because LP solutions are sparse, and randomize which block is fixed first (pp. 12, 15).
  - *Random set.* 19 generated instances (nF 6–12, nP 3–10, nB 4–11, nA 3–30), flow model, nsp = 100, kmax = 100, nt = 1 (p. 15). The exact code solves R1–R12; R13 and above exceed its memory (pp. 14–15). VNS is always best, MALT and VNS beat MSLP everywhere, and VNS's gains cost little extra time (p. 15). VNS misses the optimum only on the AST instances, blamed on degeneracy (p. 15).
  - Not found: seeds or number of replications for the randomized heuristics (pp. 12–15); no profiles, only per-instance tables.
  - Transferable technique: when a heuristic is meant to seed an exact method, run the exact method twice (cold, and warm with the optimum plus an objective cut) and report the instances where the warm start hurts, with a mechanism.
- **D6 Positioning**: A two-page review (pp. 2–3) sorted by what each method guarantees. Haverly's recursion may not reach a global optimum (p. 2); SLP, the commercial distributive recursion shown equivalent to it (Lasdon and Joffe) and Benders-based decomposition are local (p. 2); GOP and Al-Khayyal–Falk give ε-global optima (p. 2); Ben-Tal et al. solve the proportion model through semi-infinite duality (p. 3); Adhya et al. with BARON are the latest (p. 3). Criticism is factual and tied to mechanisms, e.g. bound sensitivity and the growth of time with variables not at a bound for Al-Khayyal–Falk (p. 3); no polemic. Industrial lineage is cited: Shell (Griffith and Stewart 1961), Exxon (Baker and Lasdon 1985), Texaco (DeWitt et al. 1989), the New Zealand Refining Company (Amos et al. 1997) (pp. 2–4). Reformulation is presented as a research tool with a self-citation (Audet et al. 1997, p. 2). Against Adhya et al., called the state of the art, the text concedes the easy instances (p. 10); against SLP, the commonly used method, the heuristics win everywhere (pp. 11, 15).
  - Transferable technique: organize the related-work section by the guarantee each method gives (local, ε-global, finite), so the gap your paper fills is visible without polemic.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The core claim appears in the introduction's second paragraph, before any notation (p. 1; Quote 1). The roadmap paragraph previews each part's result, not only its topic (p. 3). Hedging is systematic in result sentences: *suggest*, *appears*, *seems* (pp. 3, 9, 10). The novelty claim for ALT is carefully bounded: the method was suggested before in other contexts, and the paper claims only the first computational experience, to the authors' best knowledge (p. 11). One proposition; the rest is formulations, figures and tables, and a worked count explains how to read Table 1 (p. 6). The honest-limits sentence for VNS is placed right after its description (p. 12). No conclusion; the paper closes with six observation bullets (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: put a one-line worked example next to the first dense table (for RT2, 4×3 + 4×3 = 24 bilinear terms) so readers can decode every column.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Layers: modeling (flow vs proportion comparison, hybrid for generalized pooling, reductions of RT1 and F3–F5; §2), computational (literature instances solved exactly, hard AST1–AST3 much faster than before, larger random instances solved; §3, §4.3.2), algorithmic (ALT with a convergence proposition, VNS for BLP; §4), data (all instance data at www.gerad.ca/Charles.Audet, p. 5; RT2, whose source is a private communication, printed in the appendix, pp. 15–16). Limits are concrete: the memory limit of the current implementation (pp. 9, 15), degeneracy defeating VNS shaking on AST (p. 15), open theory for the VNS discretization (p. 12), linear blending only (p. 4), and feasibility of a starting point is itself very hard (p. 12). Future work: none stated (no conclusion section).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll) — variant ⚠. Same division of labour, different certifier: heuristics (MALT, VNS) propose incumbents and the branch-and-cut proves global optimality (pp. 3, 9, 10–11). The two-execution protocol measures what the proposer is worth (p. 9), and AST4 shows a good incumbent can make the certifier slower (p. 9). The VNS here is the ancestor of the VNS Search in MADS (JOGO 2008, SKILL.md).
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first) — variant ⚠. Same principle (model before algorithm), applied to variable choice instead of constraint classification: flow, proportion or hybrid decides solvability (pp. 1, 9, 10), and pools can be eliminated before solving (pp. 7–9).
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder, bounded by counterexamples) — variant ⚠ (weak). The guarantee is stated at a precisely defined weak level (block-wise local optimum, under unique LP solutions, p. 11), and the missing global theory for VNS is admitted (p. 12). No counterexamples and no test of whether the hypotheses are needed.
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → public benchmark artifacts) — evidence ✅ (early form). An industry-funded problem (Ultramar Canada, p. 15); all instance data put online (p. 5); an instance obtained by private communication published in the appendix (pp. 15–16); a random-instance family with stated size ranges (p. 15). No executable, since this is not a blackbox problem.
- Method 5 (Budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking) — evidence ✅ for conceding the competitor's ground (Adhya et al. faster on easy instances, p. 10), for adjusting cross-machine times by relative CPU speed (p. 10), for reporting failures (memory limits, AST4 proof cost, AST degeneracy; pp. 9, 15) and for testing against the industrial default (SLP, pp. 11–15). Variant ⚠: the currency is CPU time, heuristic parameters are set per instance (p. 13), no profiles and no replications are reported.
- Method 6 (The solver as research instrument) — variant ⚠ (weak). The authors' own C++ branch-and-cut code (2000) is reused as the instrument, and its limits are reported as limits of the current implementation (pp. 9, 15); no long-lived product is involved.
- new-pattern candidate: **formulation-size accounting** — count bilinear variables, terms and constraints per formulation, derive the crossover, then check with solve times, noting where counts mislead (pp. 6–7, 9).
- new-pattern candidate: **audit the literature instances** — show which standard test instances reduce to an LP or a closed form before benchmarking on them (pp. 7–9).
- new-pattern candidate: **proposer–certifier protocol** — cold vs warm runs of the exact method to price the heuristic (p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Before benchmarking on inherited instances, apply rerouting/dominance arguments to fix variables and merge nodes; publish the reductions (RT1 → LP, F3–F5 → closed form, pp. 7–9).
- [D2/D4] Compare alternative formulations with a size table (linear vars; bilinear vars per block; bilinear terms; L≤/Q≤/Q=), state the crossover formula, then validate by solving and report where the counts mislead (BT5, pp. 6–7, 9).
- [D5] Price a heuristic by running the exact method cold and warm (optimum + objective cut); report and explain cases where warm is slower (pp. 9–10).
- [D2] For bilinear problems: local search = alternate LP solves over the two blocks; shaking = random extreme point k basis exchanges away, alternating subspaces; distance = symmetric difference of basic sets (pp. 11–12).
- [D5] When feasibility of a start is hard, report the feasible-start rate and the tricks that raised it (0/10,000 → about 25% on RT2 by sparse random starts and random block choice, pp. 12, 15).
- [D5] In cross-paper timing tables, give both raw ratios and ratios adjusted by relative CPU speed, and concede the instances the competitor wins (p. 10).
- [D5] When a heuristic seeds another method, include its time in the second method's time (p. 13).
- [D4/D7] Define the weak optimality notion inside the proposition statement itself, so the guarantee cannot be over-read (p. 11).
