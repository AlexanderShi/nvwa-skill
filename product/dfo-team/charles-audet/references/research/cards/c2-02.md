# Batch c2-02 · Charles Audet · the 2006 MADS launch triplet (core)

**Batch intent**: The three 2006 SIAM J. Optim. papers are the launch of MADS: the framework and its first-order theory with Dennis (S001), the first use of MADS as an instrument, tuning another algorithm's parameters, with Orban (S016), and the second-order extension with Abramson (S020). The question is how Audet launches a new algorithm class. How is a framework paper built so that others can plug in instances? How is the theory pushed up the guarantee ladder and bounded by examples? How quickly is the new method turned into a tool for a different community? A second question is which parts of SKILL.md's Methods 1, 3, 5 and 6 and Heuristics 7–8 are already present in 2004–2005, and in what form.
**Focus dimensions**: D1–D8 (all three are core). Extra weight on D2 (what MADS changes relative to GPS and what it keeps), D4 (proof devices and the counterexamples that bound each theorem) and D5 (how the first MADS experiments were designed).
**Material roles**: S001 core · S016 core · S020 core.

Reading note: all three text files are pre-publication versions, so page references are to the preprint pages given by the `[[page N]]` markers, not to journal pages.
- S001 is the version formatted for submission to SIAM ("Paper submitted to Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics", p. 1).
- S016 is the 1 December 2004 preprint, titled "Finding optimal algorithmic parameters using a mesh adaptive direct search" (p. 1). S020 cites it as GERAD report G-2004-96 (S020 p. 16).
- S020 is the 17 August 2005 preprint, titled "Second-Order Convergence of Mesh-Adaptive Direct Search" (p. 1).

The published versions were not read. All three papers were read in full, every proof included, and their reference lists were read. Figures with plots are garbled in the extraction (S001 Figs. 5.2–5.8, pp. 20–25; S016 Figs. 2–5, pp. 24–27), so results come from the authors' prose and tables.

### S001 · Mesh adaptive direct search algorithms for constrained optimization (SIAM J. Optim. 2006, 10.1137/040603371)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. E. Dennis Jr. · **Position in trajectory**:
- **Origin.** The paper follows directly from Audet's own GPS work: the convergence hierarchy of *Analysis of generalized pattern searches* (2003, ref. [3]), Example F of the tightness paper (2004, ref. [2]), and the pattern-search filter (2004, ref. [4]) (pp. 1–2, 26). SKILL.md's Signature Work Anatomy marks the GPS → MADS link as inferred from abstracts. The paper states the link itself (pp. 1–2, 26; Quote 1).
- **Funding.** FCAR and NSERC (Audet), with AFOSR, The Boeing Company and ExxonMobil funding both authors (p. 1).
- **Software.** NOMAD, the C++ implementation of MADS and GPS, was coded by Gilles Couture. Abramson, Booker and Le Digabel are thanked (p. 26).
- **Report number.** The MADS report is GERAD G-2004-04, cited by S016 (p. 27) and S020 (p. 16) while this paper was still unpublished.
- **Read.** All 27 pages. Figures 5.2–5.8 are unreadable (pp. 20–25).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - *Entry point.* The problem is min f(x) over x ∈ Ω, where f : Rⁿ → R ∪ {+∞} may be nonsmooth and Ω may be given only by an oracle that answers yes or no to feasibility (p. 1). The entry is a limitation of the authors' own previous method. GPS polls a finite set of directions, which the authors call "the primary drawback of GPS algorithms in our opinion" (p. 1; Quote 1).
  - *Mechanism of the gap.* GPS guarantees f°(x̂; d) ≥ 0 only for finitely many refining directions (eq. 1.1, p. 2). For a Lipschitz f this falls short of Clarke stationarity, and Example F of ref. [2] shows the failure (p. 2).
  - *Constraints.* With bound constraints the directional dependence cannot be overcome "by any amount of smoothness, by using penalty functions, or by the use of the filter approach" (p. 2). The last item is the authors' own 2004 filter GPS.
  - *Barrier requirement.* Lewis and Torczon's barrier proof for GPS requires the tangent-cone generators to be known at every iteration. MADS needs no such knowledge (p. 2).
  - *Target statement.* The goal is stated as one inequality: MADS should produce a limit point with f°(x̂; v) ≥ 0 for all v in the Clarke tangent cone (eq. 1.4, p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: open a methods paper with the precise weakness of your own previous method, stated as the inequality it fails to deliver (here (1.1) versus (1.2)/(1.4), p. 2). Then state the new target as the inequality you will prove.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *One parameter becomes two.* GPS has a single Δ_k. MADS separates the mesh size Δᵐ_k, which fixes the lattice on which trial points lie, from the poll size Δᵖ_k, which bounds how far poll points may be from x_k. The constraints are Δᵐ_k ≤ Δᵖ_k and Δᵐ → 0 exactly when Δᵖ → 0 on any subsequence (eq. 2.2, p. 4).
  - *Why this works.* Δᵐ can go to 0 faster than Δᵖ, so the frame can use ever more lattice directions inside the poll region, and the poll directions are no longer confined to a finite set (pp. 5–6). Figures 2.1 and 2.2 contrast GPS frames with MADS frames (pp. 5–6). In the R² example, 56 distinct frames are possible at the finest level shown (p. 17).
  - *What is kept from GPS.* The mesh is still defined as the union over all evaluated points (Def. 2.1, p. 3). The mesh update is still Δᵐ_{k+1} = τ^{w_k} Δᵐ_k (eq. 2.1, p. 4). Only simple decrease is required: "There are no sufficient decrease requirements" (p. 4). The optional SEARCH on the mesh is unchanged. "The difference between the MADS and the GPS algorithms lies exactly in this POLL step" (p. 4).
  - *Frames.* A frame is a positive spanning set D_k whose elements are nonnegative integer combinations of D, whose points lie within a constant times Δᵖ_k of x_k, and whose normalized limits are positive spanning sets (Def. 2.2, p. 5). The authors describe it as "a less general choice of frames than the choices allowed by Coope and Price", chosen to be "easy to implement" (p. 1).
  - *Constraints by extreme barrier.* MADS is applied to f_Ω, which equals +∞ outside Ω. The constraints are tested first, cheapest first, and f is evaluated only if the point is feasible (p. 4). That rule "saves computation, and it is needed in the proof of Theorem 3.12" (p. 4).
  - *The LTMADS instance* (§4.1, pp. 14–17):
    - Parameters are G = I, D = [I −I], τ = 4, w⁻ = −1 and w⁺ = 1. Δᵐ is always a power of 4 and never exceeds 1 (p. 14).
    - A special direction b(ℓ) is drawn once per mesh index ℓ = −log₄ Δᵐ_k and reused whenever that mesh size recurs. One randomly chosen component is ±2^ℓ and the others are uniform integers with absolute value below 2^ℓ (p. 14).
    - A random lower-triangular (n−1)×(n−1) matrix with ±2^ℓ on the diagonal is completed with b(ℓ). Its rows and columns are randomly permuted, and the result is completed to a positive basis in one of two ways: minimal (n+1 directions, Δᵖ = n√Δᵐ) or maximal (2n directions, Δᵖ = √Δᵐ) (p. 15).
    - A worked R⁵ example follows (Example 4.1, pp. 15–16).
  - *Dynamic ordering, recast as a SEARCH.* GPS's trick of re-polling the last successful direction first would put points off the mesh after the mesh is enlarged. It is therefore re-implemented as a one-point SEARCH, s_k = x_{k−1} + 4Δᵐ_{k−1}d, which provably lies on the current mesh (p. 17).
  - *Why randomness.* The authors "have not found a deterministic strategy that achieves a good distribution of poll directions when the process is terminated after a reasonable number of iterations" (p. 14; repeated on p. 26). The reason is finite-time behaviour, not theory.
  - Transferable technique: when a method's limitation comes from one parameter doing two jobs, split it into two parameters linked by an asymptotic coupling condition like (2.2). The old method then becomes the special case where the two are equal (p. 4: in GPS, Δ_k = Δᵖ_k = Δᵐ_k).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Standing assumptions.* x₀ ∈ Ω, f(x₀) finite, and all iterates in a compact set (p. 7). The authors plan to remove the feasible start "by incorporating the filter approach" (p. 7), a lead toward the later progressive barrier.
  - *Smoothness.* The results are tied to the local smoothness of f at x̂ (Lipschitz near x̂, or strictly differentiable), not to f_Ω, which is discontinuous on ∂Ω (p. 12).
  - *Constraint qualification.* The CQ is that the hypertangent cone at x̂ is nonempty (Rockafellar; p. 12). Theorem 3.4 shows that for C¹ inequality constraints, hypertangency is equivalent to ∇cᵢ(x̂)ᵀv < 0 on the active constraints with nonzero gradient, which relates it to the Gould–Tolle CQ (p. 8).
  - *Algorithmic assumption.* The refining directions must be dense in T^H_Ω(x̂) (Thm. 3.13, p. 12). For LTMADS this holds with probability 1, and only if the iterates tied to the refining directions converge to a single x̂ (abstract, p. 1; Thm. 4.4, p. 19).
  - *Newton and SQP analogy.* The authors calibrate their assumptions against these two classical methods. Newton assumes Lipschitz first derivatives and MADS assumes only a Lipschitz f. SQP assumes linear independence of constraint gradients, and MADS needs the weaker nonempty-hypertangent condition (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: when your guarantee needs an unusual hypothesis (here a nonempty hypertangent cone), prove its equivalence to a textbook CQ in the smooth case (Thm. 3.4, p. 8), so readers can place it.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**:
  - *Skeleton.*
    - (i) liminf Δᵖ = liminf Δᵐ = 0, which gives infinitely many minimal frame centers. The proof is Torczon's lattice argument, since x_N = x₀ + Σ Δᵐ_k D z_k still holds (Prop. 3.1, p. 7).
    - (ii) Refining subsequences and refining directions are defined (Def. 3.2, p. 7).
    - (iii) Three tangent cones, hypertangent ⊆ Clarke ⊆ contingent, together with set regularity (Defs. 3.3–3.7, pp. 7–9).
    - (iv) The derivative used is Jahn's constrained Clarke derivative (1.3), which only looks at points in Ω (p. 2). Lemma 3.8 shows it is λ-Lipschitz across hypertangent directions (p. 9). Prop. 3.9 shows it is continuous from T^H onto the Clarke cone (pp. 9–11). Both had to be re-proved because the standard calculus is for (1.1) (p. 9).
    - (v) The key Theorem 3.12, detailed below (p. 12).
    - (vi) The hierarchy built on it: Clarke stationarity (Thm. 3.13), Clarke KKT under strict differentiability (Cor. 3.14), contingent stationarity and contingent KKT under regularity (Prop. 3.15, Cor. 3.16), and 0 ∈ ∂f(x̂) when x̂ is interior (Thm. 3.17) (pp. 12–13).
  - *Key device (Thm. 3.12).* At minimal frame centers, the inequality f(x_k + Δᵐ_k d_k) ≥ f(x_k) that the algorithm has already verified is itself one difference quotient in the limsup that defines f°(x̂; v). It is read with y = x_k and t = Δᵐ_k‖d_k‖, and t → 0 because it is bounded by Δᵖ_k·max‖d′‖ (p. 12). The proof is five lines once the cone and derivative machinery is in place.
  - *Boundary example.* Example 3.10 (pp. 11–12) shows that Prop. 3.9 fails when T^H is empty. Ω is the union of the nonnegative quadrant and the curve b = −a², and f = −max{0, a}. Then f°(0; w) ≥ 0 on the interior of the contingent cone, but f°(0; e₁) = −1 on the Clarke cone.
  - *LTMADS density (Thm. 4.3, p. 18).* The proof bounds, uniformly in k, the probability that the normalized u = 2^{−ℓ}b(ℓ) lies within ε of any target direction v. The bound is (ε/4)^{n−1}·1/(2n), obtained by counting integers in an interval, once √Δᵐ_k ≤ ε/2. Theorem 4.4 upgrades density of poll directions to density of refining directions when the whole sequence converges (p. 19).
  - *Frame conditions (Prop. 4.2, pp. 16–17).* The frame conditions are checked by integrality and determinant bookkeeping: |det B| = 2^{ℓn}, entries lie in [−2^ℓ, 2^ℓ], and the Coope–Price bounded-entries condition holds.
  - Reader's note: b(ℓ) is reused whenever a mesh index recurs (p. 14). The draws that are actually independent are therefore indexed by distinct ℓ, not by every k ∈ K, although the proof says "Each one of these bases is generated independently" (p. 18). The argument still goes through because ℓ → ∞ along a refining subsequence, but the text does not say so.
  - *Form of the guarantees.* All results are asymptotic stationarity statements. There are no rates and no complexity bounds.
  - Transferable technique: design the algorithm's acceptance test so that a failed step is literally one term of the generalized derivative you want to bound (Thm. 3.12, p. 12). Then the convergence proof reduces to showing that the step directions become dense.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Four problems, "Each problem is intended to make a point about MADS" (p. 19). The authors concede that "four examples are not conclusive evidence" (p. 19).
  - *Isolating the POLL.* Comparisons use empty or very simple SEARCH steps "in order to isolate the value of the MADS POLL step" (p. 4). GPS runs use "our standard NOMAD implementation of GPS" and a favourable poll ordering (pp. 19–20). Every plot has the number of evaluations on the x-axis (Figs. 5.2–5.8 captions).
  - *(1) Kolda–Lewis–Torczon's 2-D function where GPS stagnates* (§5.1, pp. 19–21). f = (1 − exp(−‖x‖²))·max{‖x−c‖², ‖x−d‖²}, started from (−2.1, 1.7). LTMADS was run 5 times because it is random. Runs stop when a minimal frame has Δᵖ < 10⁻¹⁰. Every GPS run without a SEARCH stalls on the nondifferentiable line x₂ = −¾x₁, at points satisfying the GPS theory. GPS with a random 2n-point SEARCH and all LTMADS runs reach the global minimizer at the origin (pp. 20–21).
  - *(2) The competitor's home ground* (§5.2, pp. 21–23). Catalytic-combustion kinetic parameter fitting (Hayes et al. 2003, co-authored by Audet): 4 bound-constrained variables and 3 data sets, with a budget of 500 evaluations or Δᵖ = 2⁻⁶. The authors concede that GPS "decreases more steadily" because its fixed directions are "an excellent choice for this problem" (p. 23). On a fixed budget, MADS with the dynamic SEARCH is better, and MADS with n+1 dynamic directions gave the best solution on every data set. The fit improves by about 1% at specific times (p. 23).
  - *(3) Linear objective on a hypersphere* (§5.3, pp. 23–24). min Σxᵢ s.t. Σxᵢ² ≤ 3n, from the origin, budget 600n evaluations or Δᵖ < 10⁻¹². Four values of n: 10, 20 and 50 are named in the text, and the fourth is illegible in the garbled figure. GPS runs with the barrier, with the filter, and with a SEARCH made of a 5n-point LHS then n/5 random points. MADS converged to the global optimum for every n. GPS with the barrier stalls on the boundary, GPS with the filter stops at another suboptimal point, and GPS with the random SEARCH escapes but falls short within the budget (p. 24).
  - *(4) A deliberately hypothesis-violating limit test* (§5.4, p. 25). min a s.t. eᵃ ≤ b ≤ 2eᵃ, whose optimum is at −∞. The runs continue until Δᵐ < 10⁻³²³: "We admit that this is excessive, but we wanted to run the algorithms to their limits" (p. 25). All methods fail. The failure is explained in the theory's terms, and the theory "is not violated – yet" (p. 25).
  - *Missing.* No performance or data profiles, no solver from another family, and no statistics beyond showing 5 runs.
  - Transferable technique: write down, before the results, which single point each test problem is meant to make (p. 3 and p. 19). Include one problem where the competitor is known to be good (§5.2), and one that violates your hypotheses, to show where the method stops (§5.4).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Relative to other frameworks.* In the unconstrained case, MADS sits "somewhere between" GPS (Torczon, as organized by Audet–Dennis) and Coope–Price frame methods (p. 1). The authors are explicit that MADS is less general than Coope–Price in two ways: its frames (p. 1) and its restriction of SEARCH points to the mesh (p. 4).
  - *Self-criticism.* The strongest criticism is aimed at the authors' own work: the GPS hierarchy [3], the tightness examples [2] and the filter GPS [4] (pp. 1–2, 13, 26).
  - *Other prior work.* Lewis–Torczon's barrier and augmented Lagrangian extensions are reframed by what they need, such as known tangent-cone generators (p. 2). They are not dismissed.
  - *Borrowed machinery.* The nonsmooth tools come from Clarke, Rockafellar, Jahn and Gould–Tolle (pp. 2, 8–9).
  - *Pointers outward.* Finkel–Kelley's DIRECT analysis is cited as "a quite different algorithm analyzed using the same concepts" (p. 3). The work is expected to apply to choosing templates for implicit filtering (p. 26).
  - *Self-citation.* 5 of the 22 references are Audet's (pp. 26–27).
  - Transferable technique: place a new method between two named families. State in which respects it is less general than the more general one, and justify that choice by implementability (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Abstract.* It gives the main result and then its two limits: probability 1 only, and only if the iterates converge to a single x̂. Its last sentence is "We also illustrate the limitation of our results with examples" (p. 1).
  - *Motivation in the first person.* "in our opinion" and "our main motivation" (p. 1).
  - *Roadmap.* The organization paragraph states what each numerical example will show, not just its topic (p. 3).
  - *Explaining the technical parts.* The text stops for intuition at the hardest definitions: "Since the definition of a hypertangent is rather technical and crucial to our results, we will pause for a short discussion" (p. 8). It gives frame pictures (pp. 5–6) and a worked R⁵ basis (pp. 15–16).
  - *Hedging.* Numerical claims are hedged ("suggests", "appears", "seems"; pp. 17, 21, 23). Theorems are not.
  - *Discussion section.* It hands the framework to others: "We expect others will find more, and perhaps better, implementations" (p. 26; Quote 2).
  - Transferable technique: after the general framework, explain your assumptions by analogy with the canonical smooth method's assumptions (the Newton/SQP paragraph, p. 13). Then say explicitly that the next section supplies the "practical algorithmic procedures" that SQP papers usually provide (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Layers.*
    - Algorithm class: the MADS framework (§2).
    - Theory: a hierarchy based on the Clarke calculus and three tangent cones (§3).
    - Implementable instance: LTMADS, with a probability-1 density proof (§4).
    - Numerical demonstration (§5).
    - Software: NOMAD (p. 26).
  - *Limits named in the paper.*
    - The results hold only with probability 1, and density of refining directions needs the whole sequence to converge (pp. 1, 19).
    - A feasible x₀ is required (p. 7).
    - Four examples are not conclusive (p. 19).
    - MADS stalls on a narrowing feasible region (p. 25).
    - At small n, GPS with a random SEARCH behaves like MADS. The gap opens as n grows because "the relative cost of the POLL step becomes more important" (p. 26).
  - *Future directions.* A filter version (p. 7), other and better instances (p. 26), and templates for implicit filtering (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll): evidence ✅. The paper states the division of labour. SEARCH is any finite set of mesh points, but "it cannot be used in the convergence analysis – except to provide counterexamples" (p. 4). All theory lives in the POLL (§3). GPS's dynamic-ordering trick is moved into a one-point SEARCH so that it cannot break the proof (p. 17). The experiments empty the SEARCH to measure the POLL alone (p. 4), which is SKILL.md step 4 run in reverse. Nuance: §5.1 and p. 26 show that a random SEARCH can make up for a weak POLL at small n.
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder, bounded by counterexamples): evidence ✅, and the origin of the MADS rung. The hierarchy is tied to the smoothness of f and to the local geometry of Ω (Thms. 3.12–3.17, pp. 12–13). Example 3.10 marks where Prop. 3.9 fails (pp. 11–12). §5.4 violates the hypotheses on purpose (p. 25). The abstract names the limitation (p. 1). Variant ⚠ on step 5 (keep deterministic variants): in 2006 the authors chose randomness on purpose, for finite-time direction spread, and noted that the deterministic strategies they considered spread the directions poorly (pp. 14, 26). The deterministic OrthoMADS of 2009 (SKILL.md Heuristic 8) therefore answers an open problem this paper states.
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first): variant ⚠, early form. There is only one class, unrelaxable constraints given by a yes/no oracle and handled by the extreme barrier (pp. 1–2, 4). The constraints are ordered cheapest first before f is evaluated (p. 4). Relaxable constraints are deferred to "the filter approach" (p. 7). Hidden constraints are not discussed.
- Method 5 (Budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking): variant ⚠, early form. Evaluations are the currency and budgets are capped (500, 600n; pp. 23–24). The competitor's home ground is conceded (§5.2, p. 23). Repeated runs are used for the random method (p. 19). But there are 4 problems, the only baseline is the same-family GPS, and no profiles are drawn.
- Method 6 (The solver as research instrument): evidence ✅. NOMAD already implements both GPS and MADS, and the baselines are run in it (pp. 19, 26).
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → public benchmark artifacts): weak evidence ✅. An application paper co-authored by Audet (catalytic combustion, Hayes et al. 2003) is reused as a test problem (p. 21). Nothing is released.
- new-pattern candidate: **Framework theorem with plug-in instance**, a name already used in c08. The paper proves everything under one instance-level assumption, that refining directions are dense in T^H, and verifies it for one instance separately: "One only need to show that the new instance generates a dense set of refining directions" (p. 26; also p. 13).
- new-pattern candidate: **Generalize-and-recover**, already used in c05. GPS is recovered as the MADS case Δᵖ = Δᵐ (p. 4).
- new-pattern candidate: **Purpose-labelled test problems.** Each numerical example is announced with the one claim it supports, including one problem conceded to the competitor and one that violates the hypotheses (pp. 3, 19, 25).
- new-pattern candidate: **Cheap-first evaluation gating**, already used in c02, c07 and s01. Constraints are tested cheapest first and f is skipped outside Ω, and the proof depends on this rule (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Split an overloaded step-size parameter into a lattice parameter and a reach parameter, coupled only by the condition that one goes to 0 exactly when the other does (eq. 2.2, p. 4). Tie the growing direction set to the gap between the two (Δᵖ = n√Δᵐ or √Δᵐ, p. 15).
- [D4] Make a rejected trial point equal one difference quotient of the generalized derivative, then take limsup along the refining subsequence (Thm. 3.12, p. 12).
- [D4] If the algorithm evaluates f only on Ω, use a derivative definition restricted to Ω (Jahn's (1.3)), so the theorems depend on f's smoothness, not on the barrier function's discontinuity (pp. 2, 12). Re-prove the calculus facts you need (Lemma 3.8, Prop. 3.9) and give the example where they fail (Ex. 3.10).
- [D4] Probabilistic density by counting integers: one reused special direction per mesh level, with one coordinate ±2^ℓ and the rest uniform integers, gives a lower bound on the probability of landing within ε of any v that does not depend on k (pp. 14, 18).
- [D2] If a speed-up trick would break the lattice or the proof, re-implement it as a one-point SEARCH on the current mesh (dynamic ordering → s_k = x_{k−1} + 4Δᵐ_{k−1}d, p. 17).
- [D5] Isolate the changed component: run all methods with an empty or trivial SEARCH (p. 4). Run the random variant several times (5 runs, p. 19).
- [D5] Include a stress test that deliberately violates the hypotheses, run to machine limits, and explain the outcome in the theory's own terms (§5.4, p. 25).
- [D3/D7] Tie a new constraint qualification to a textbook one by proving an equivalence for smooth constraints (Thm. 3.4, p. 8). Explain your hypotheses by analogy with Newton/SQP (p. 13).

- "One only need to show that the new instance generates a dense set of refining directions." (p. 26)

### S016 · Finding optimal algorithmic parameters using derivative-free optimization (SIAM J. Optim. 2006, 10.1137/040620886)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: D. Orban · **Position in trajectory**:
- **Version and date.** The file is the 1 December 2004 preprint (p. 1). It is titled "Finding optimal algorithmic parameters using a mesh adaptive direct search", S020 cites it as G-2004-96, submitted to SIOPT (S020 p. 16), and the published title differs.
- **Timing.** It was written within months of the MADS report G-2004-04 (ref. [3], p. 27). It is the first paper to use MADS as an instrument rather than to study it.
- **Coauthor's tools.** Orban contributes the smooth-optimization toolchain: GALAHAD/GLTR (p. 16) and CUTEr (p. 18), both Gould–Orban–Toint (p. 31). He also contributes the related discretized sensitivity study by Gould, Orban, Sartenaer and Toint (ref. [15], p. 3).
- **Funding.** FCAR, NSERC, AFOSR and ExxonMobil (Audet), and NSERC (Orban) (p. 1).
- **Later work.** SKILL.md Heuristic 7 cites this paper as the origin of tuning an algorithm with direct search. OPAL (2014) and HyperNOMAD continue the line (SKILL.md).
- **Read.** All 32 pages, including the appendix tables (pp. 27–29). Figures 2–5 are unreadable (pp. 24–27). A minor inconsistency in the preprint: the text refers to the trust-region radius update as "Step 4" (pp. 12, 18), but Algorithm 3.1 puts it in Step 3 (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - *General question.* Most algorithms depend on continuous, discrete or categorical parameters, and "it remains unclear how a user should proceed to determine good, let alone optimal, values" (p. 2).
  - *The gap in prior work.* Gould, Orban, Sartenaer and Toint (ref. [15]) discretize the parameter space and search it exhaustively. Comparing that many variants is itself hard, since performance profiles are unclear beyond "five or six" algorithms. The authors instead delegate the comparison to an optimizer (p. 3; Quote 2).
  - *Concrete test case.* The four trust-region update constants (η₁, η₂, α₁, α₂). The classical values (¼, ¾, ½, 2) are recommended in at least six references (p. 18), and the authors aim to show they are "arbitrary" (p. 18; Quote 1).
  - *Why this is a blackbox problem.* One evaluation solves 55 CUTEr problems and takes hours. It has no derivatives, it can fail, and it is not repeatable, because CPU time varies with machine load (pp. 3, 16).
  - Transferable technique: frame a folklore default ("classical parameter values", p. 18) as a blackbox optimization problem. Promise either to beat it or to show it is locally optimal, and start the optimizer from that default (p. 18).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *The framework* (§4.1, pp. 15–16). An algorithm A with continuous parameters p ∈ Ω ⊂ R^ℓ, where ℓ is small. A set P_O of representative instances. A measure ψ(p) of A's performance on P_O (total CPU time, iterations, function evaluations, or failures). A surrogate σ(p), defined as the same measure on an easier set P_S, possibly with S ⊂ O. The quality of σ against its cost is set by the choice of P_S (p. 16).
  - *MADS specialized for the task* (§2.2, pp. 6–9; Algorithm 2.1, p. 9). 2ℓ poll points p_k ± Δ_k d, where D_k is a basis with ‖Δ_k d‖∞ = O(√Δ_k). The mesh is divided by 4 after a failure and multiplied by 4 after a success while Δ ≤ ¼ (p. 7).
  - *Three roles for the surrogate* (pp. 5–6, 20):
    - (1) Warm start. Minimize σ from p^C first, obtaining p^S, then start the truth run from S₀ = {p^C, p^S}.
    - (2) Ordering. Sort both SEARCH and POLL candidates by σ, with opportunistic evaluation.
    - (3) Pruning, in the SEARCH only. Discard candidates with σ_Ω(q) > σ_Ω(p_k) + v|σ_Ω(p_k)|, with v = 0.1. "The convergence theory prohibits pruning candidates given by the poll step" (p. 6).
  - *SEARCH strategy* (p. 20). A 64-point Latin hypercube at k = 0. At k ≥ 1, 8 random mesh points screened by the surrogate, plus the dynamic search of the MADS paper after a success.
  - *Trust-region side* (§§3, 4.2, pp. 11–17). A textbook Algorithm 3.1 built from GALAHAD pieces: GLTR subproblem solves, stopping at ‖∇f‖₂ ≤ 10⁻⁵, 1000 iterations at most (a failure then sets ψ = +∞), and a rule for δ₀ (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: when the expensive objective is performance on a hard instance set, build the surrogate as the same performance measure on an easy set. Use it three ways (warm start, ordering, search-only pruning), and never prune the POLL (pp. 6, 20).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *MADS side.* Assumption 1: at least one finite start and iterates in a compact set (p. 8). Ω is restricted to polyhedral, and in practice to bounds (p. 3).
  - *Constraint handling.* The linear constraint η₁ < η₂ is "hardcoded into mads", so violations return +∞. The theory then only needs bounds (p. 18).
  - *A bound added for the proof.* α₂ ≤ 10 is added, the authors say, "on the one hand to satisfy Assumption 1". The other reason is that enlarging the region by an arbitrary factor is not useful (p. 17). This is an assumption imposed for the proof's sake, and it is labelled as such.
  - *Scaling.* The parameters are scaled by (1000, 100, 100, 10) so that a mesh step has a comparable effect on each (eq. 10, p. 18).
  - *Honesty about the model.* "optimality is not a well-defined concept in this case" (p. 3). Because CPU time is noisy, "technically, ψ is not a function in the mathematical sense" (footnote 1, p. 16). For noisy functions, "the best optimality condition which can be hoped for is to find a refined point" (p. 4).
  - *Restated theory.* Theorem 2.5 gives the MADS hierarchy for bound constraints. It adds a lower-semicontinuity rung: ψ(p̂) ≤ lim ψ(p_k). The results "rely only on the poll step, and are independent of the surrogate function and of the search step" (p. 10).
  - *Trust-region side.* Assumption 2: f bounded below, bounded Hessian, and a first-order-consistent model (p. 13). Theorems 3.1–3.3 are recalled without proof, and Yuan's example shows why η₁ > 0 matters (pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable technique: when a theorem needs compactness that the application does not supply, add the mildest bound that gives it (α₂ ≤ 10), and say in the text which reason is the proof's and which is the modeller's (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**:
  - No new theorems. All MADS results are specialized from the MADS report, with proofs deferred to it (p. 8). The trust-region results are cited from Conn, Gould and Toint (p. 14).
  - The analytical work is the problem formulation instead: domain (9), the barrier for the linear constraint, scaling (10), the failure → +∞ convention (pp. 17–18), and the design of the surrogate lists (p. 19).
  - The theory is used as a design constraint. Search candidates may be pruned by the surrogate, but POLL candidates may not, because the analysis rests on the POLL (pp. 6, 10).
  - Transferable technique: in an application-of-theory paper, restate the specialized theorem (Thm. 2.5, p. 10) and draw the design rules from it: what the surrogate may and may not do (pp. 6, 10).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Test-list construction* (p. 19). The trust-region method was run on the 163 unconstrained CUTEr problems at default sizes. The surrogate list keeps problems with n < 1000 and 0.01 ≤ τ(p^C) ≤ 30 s: 54 problems, 2 ≤ n ≤ 500, 68.2 s in total. The objective list keeps problems with n ≥ 1000 and τ(p^C) ≤ 3600 s: 55 problems, 1000 ≤ n ≤ 20000, 13461 s (3 h 44 min) per evaluation. Full per-problem results at p^C are in the appendix (Tables 4–5, pp. 28–29).
  - *Measures.* ψ₁ is the total number of function evaluations and ψ₂ the total CPU time (pp. 18–19). The runs reported use ψ₂ and σ₂ on a 500 MHz Sun Blade 100, with NOMAD downloadable from gerad.ca (p. 20).
  - *Phase 1, on the surrogate* (p. 21). 310 MADS evaluations, stopped at Δ < 10⁻⁶, with the best point found at evaluation 289. The incumbent trajectory is tabulated (Table 1). The truth value drops from 13461 to 11498 s. Re-evaluating σ at p^C differs by about 1%, "an illustration of the non-deterministic aspect" (p. 21).
  - *Phase 2, on the truth* (pp. 22–23). A cap of 150 truth evaluations, which "amounts to an expected total running time of just about three weeks" (p. 22). The best point is p* = (0.22125, 0.94457, 0.37934, 2.30430), with ψ₂ = 10192 s, a 25% cut. p* is "clearly in favour of a sufficient decrease condition rather than of η1 ≈ 0" (p. 22).
  - *Comparison.* Dolan–Moré performance profiles in CPU time and in function evaluations compare p^C and p* on the objective list (Figs. 2–3). Their shapes are not readable here.
  - *An honest anomaly* (pp. 23–25). A preliminary run without scaling and without the surrogate gave p^A = (0.000008, 0.9906, 0.3032, 3.4021), close to ref. - [D2] Parameter tuning as blackbox optimization: ψ(p) is an aggregate measure over a representative instance set. Failures give +∞. Linear constraints between parameters are enforced as a barrier inside the solver, so the theory sees only bounds (pp. 15–18).
- [D2] Easy-set surrogate plus two-phase run: optimize σ from the default to get p^S. Then run the truth from {p^C, p^S}, with σ ordering all candidates and pruning only SEARCH candidates (threshold v = 0.1) (pp. 5–6, 20).

- [D5] Build the test lists by explicit rules (n and runtime at the default, p. 19). Publish the per-problem baseline table (pp. 28–29).
- [D5] Re-evaluate the default once to measure the noise level before interpreting improvements (≈1%, p. 21).
- [D5] Report the incumbent trajectory (evaluation number, value, parameters) and the total wall-clock cost of the tuning run (Tables 1–2, pp. 21–23).
- [D5/D7] When the aggregate and the profile disagree, find the per-instance outliers (Table 3, p. 25) and say whether the optimizer or the measure is at fault (p. 24).

### S020 · Convergence of mesh adaptive direct search to second-order stationary points (SIAM J. Optim. 2006, 10.1137/050638382)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M. A. Abramson (Air Force Institute of Technology, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**:
- **Version and date.** The file is the 17 August 2005 preprint, titled "Second-Order Convergence of Mesh-Adaptive Direct Search" (p. 1).
- **What it extends.** It carries Abramson's second-order analysis of GPS (ref. [1], a Rice technical report, "To appear in SIAM J. Optim.", p. 16) over to MADS (abstract, p. 1).
- **Funding.** FCAR, NSERC, AFOSR and ExxonMobil Upstream Research (p. 1).
- **Place on the ladder.** In SKILL.md Method 3's list it is the rung second-order MADS (2006). It also names the problem that the deterministic OrthoMADS (2009) solves (p. 16).
- **Read.** All 17 pages, every proof included. There are no numerical experiments.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - *Entry point.* A previous analysis of second-order behaviour for GPS is extended to the more general MADS class for general set constraints (abstract, p. 1).
  - *The hook.* Abramson's GPS result says that GPS iterates on a smooth problem cannot converge in infinitely many steps to a local maximizer. This paper shows that they can converge to a saddle point (Quote 1). MADS, with dense directions, cannot (p. 2).
  - *The constraint issue.* Constraints are handled by the barrier, which gives no constraint gradients. Second-order conditions in terms of the Lagrangian are therefore out of reach, and the paper first builds set-constrained second-order conditions based on feasible directions (pp. 2, 9).
  - Transferable technique: extend a guarantee from a special case to the general class by first finding a failure mode that the special case provably has and the general class provably avoids (GPS → saddle point, pp. 2, 13–15).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *No new algorithm.* §2 restates the MADS framework (pp. 3–5) and notes that "GPS methods now become the specific MADS instance" with Δ_k = Δᵖ_k = Δᵐ_k (p. 4).
  - *The one idea.* Asymptotically dense refining directions, combined with a C^{1,1} objective and the Hiriart-Urruty–Strodiot–Nguyen generalized Hessian, are enough for second-order conditions along every feasible direction (§4.3, pp. 12–13).
  - *Kept.* The first-order results are restated as Theorem 3.9, which bundles four theorems of the MADS paper (pp. 7–8). The SEARCH "adds nothing to the convergence theory, but well-chosen search strategies can greatly improve algorithm performance" (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when your algorithm class strictly contains an older one, restate the old method as a parameter choice in the new one (p. 4). Every result then comes with a built-in comparison.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Standing assumptions.* A feasible x₀ with finite f(x₀), and iterates in a compact set (p. 5).
  - *Necessary condition (Thm. 4.8, p. 12).* f is C^{1,1} near x̂, T^H_Ω(x̂) ≠ ∅, Ω is regular near x̂, and the refining directions are dense in T^H.
  - *Sufficient condition (Thm. 4.9, p. 13).* f is twice strictly differentiable at x̂, T^H ≠ ∅, Ω is convex near x̂, ∇²f(x̂) is nonsingular, and the directions are dense.
  - *An assumption made for the proof.* The Taylor theorem holds on open sets, but x̂ may lie on ∂Ω. The authors therefore "make certain local smoothness assumptions on f that are independent of" the feasible set, so they can expand f in any direction (p. 9). This asks f to be smooth at infeasible points where the barrier never evaluates it. The text states the choice but not this cost.
  - *Honesty about density.* Density of directions cannot be implemented. LTMADS gets it only almost surely, so "the convergence results both in [4] and in this paper are also achieved almost surely" (pp. 15–16; Quote 2).
  - Transferable technique: state which assumptions are on f alone, independent of Ω, and why the proof needs that independence (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**:
  - *Build the optimality conditions first* (§4.2, pp. 9–11). Definition 4.3 introduces the cone of feasible directions T^F. Two examples show that without regularity T^F and T^Cl can be ordered either way (p. 10).
  - *Necessary condition (Thm. 4.4, p. 10).* Proved by a Taylor expansion along x* + v/k and a limit using the local boundedness and closedness of ∂²f. Example 4.5 (p. 10) shows that the condition fails for Clarke or contingent directions that are not feasible: f = −(a² + b²) on a disk, with v = (1, 0) at (0, 2).
  - *Sufficient condition (Thm. 4.6, p. 11).* Proved by contraposition from a sequence y_k → x* with f(y_k) ≤ f(x*). Example 4.7 (p. 11) shows that regularity is not enough and local convexity is needed: f = a² + 2b on 2a² + b ≥ 0.
  - *Main device (Thm. 4.8, pp. 12–13).* A contradiction by a central second difference:
    - Add the Taylor expansions at x_k + t d_k and x_k − t d_k. Divided by t², the sum equals w_kᵀA_kw_k with A_k = ½(A⁺_k + A⁻_k) ∈ ∂²f, by convexity of ∂²f.
    - If vᵀAv < 0 for all A, the right side is ≤ γ < 0.
    - The + quotient is ≥ 0 because x_k is a minimal frame center, so the − quotient must be ≤ γ < 0.
    - In the limit, ∇f(x̂)ᵀv > 0, which contradicts ∇f(x̂)ᵀv = 0.
    - The authors add that f "is never actually evaluated" at x_k − t d_k, so the result does not need 2n directions or feasibility of the mirrored point (p. 13).
  - *Thm. 4.9 (p. 13).* Combines Thm. 4.8 (vᵀ∇²f v ≥ 0 on the critical feasible directions), nonsingularity ("this inequality is strict"), first-order contingent stationarity from Thm. 3.9, and Thm. 4.6.
  - *Separating MADS from GPS* (pp. 13–15). GPS with coordinate directions started at 0 stays at the global maximizer of f = −(ab)² (p. 13). Example 4.10 fixes a GPS instance (τ = 2, w⁻ = −4, w⁺ = 3, a mesh rule that depends on the previous success, and an iteration-dependent poll order). Proposition 4.11 proves by induction, with a period-3 table of the iterates, that it converges to the saddle point of a² + b² + 3ab. The direction v = (−1, 1) then certifies that MADS cannot converge there (p. 15).
  - Reader's note on notation: Definition 2.2 puts frame points at x_k + Δᵐ_k d (p. 4), but the proof of Thm. 4.8 expands f(x_k ± Δᵖ_k d_k) (p. 12). The argument only needs the actual step length to go to 0, so this is a notational slip. Also, eq. (3) prints w⁻ ≤ 1 (p. 4), where the MADS paper has w⁻ ≤ −1.
  - Reader's check (mine, not in the paper; the published version was not read and may state the theorem differently): the step "is assumed to be nonsingular, this inequality is strict" (p. 13) holds when the critical set is a subspace, for example x̂ interior with ∇f(x̂) = 0. It does not hold on a cone.
    - Example: f(a, b) = ab on Ω = R²₊, with x₀ = (0, 0).
    - No feasible point has f < 0, so every iteration fails and x_k = 0 for all k. Directions are dense with probability 1 (whole-sequence convergence, MADS Thm. 4.4).
    - T^H(0) ≠ ∅, Ω is convex, and ∇²f = [[0, 1], [1, 0]] is nonsingular. Every hypothesis of Thm. 4.9 holds.
    - But f(t, 0) = 0 = f(0, 0) for all t ≥ 0, so 0 is not a strict local minimizer.
    - So Thm. 4.9 as printed here holds for interior limit points and needs strict copositivity, not nonsingularity, on the boundary. This is the kind of audit SKILL.md Method 3, step 4 prescribes.
  - *Form of the guarantees.* Asymptotic results only, with no rates.
  - Transferable technique: to prove a second-order property from zeroth-order polling, symmetrize. Use the one side you know is nonnegative (the failed poll point) to force the mirrored side negative, and conclude with a first-order contradiction. The mirrored point never needs to be evaluated (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none. The paper has illustrative constructions only: Examples 4.5, 4.7 and 4.10, and Figure 1 (p. 14), which draws the first GPS iterates and the ⊗ trial points on level sets.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Borrowed tools.* The paper builds on Abramson [1] for GPS second order and on Hiriart-Urruty et al. [15] for the generalized Hessian and the Taylor theorem (pp. 8–9). It names Cominetti–Correa [10] as an alternative second-order derivative (p. 8) and Chong–Zak [8] for set-constrained conditions (p. 9).
  - *Other constraint approaches.* The barrier is contrasted with the penalty [18] and filter [5] approaches (p. 2).
  - *The same analysis elsewhere.* Finkel–Kelley's first-order analysis of DIRECT is cited as "similar" (p. 2).
  - *Claim over GPS.* "Second order results for GPS are presented in [1]. They are not as strong as those presented here for MADS" (p. 13), backed by the two GPS examples.
  - *Self-citation.* 6 of the 22 references are Audet's or Abramson's (pp. 16–17), including the still-unpublished S016 cited as a SEARCH example (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: back every claim of superiority over the predecessor with an explicit example of the predecessor failing in the way the new theorem excludes (pp. 13–15).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Framing.* The abstract claims reasonable conditions (p. 1). The introduction calls the results "important and surprisingly strong" (p. 2) and, in the same paragraph, admits a negative for GPS: "unfortunately" it can converge to a saddle (p. 2; Quote 1).
  - *Structure.* "The next two results are the main contributions of this paper" labels the core (p. 12). Each optimality theorem is followed at once by an example showing its limit (Ex. 4.5 after Thm. 4.4, Ex. 4.7 after Thm. 4.6).
  - *Pre-empting a misreading.* The authors write that "it might appear to the reader that the result depends on the usage of 2n positive spanning directions at each iteration", and then "However, this is not the case" (p. 13).
  - *Conclusion.* It first compares the assumptions with Newton's ("conditions weaker than standard Newton assumptions", p. 15). It then states plainly that density "is not possible" in practice (Quote 2) and that the results are only almost sure, "a weaker measure of convergence, but it works well in practice" (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: end a theory paper with the gap between the idealized hypothesis and any implementation. Name the research direction that would close it (p. 16: "the clever enumeration of these directions so that the stronger type of convergence is retained").
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Layers.* Theory only: set-constrained second-order necessary and sufficient conditions (§4.2), second-order convergence of MADS (§4.3), and the GPS saddle counterexample (§4.3).
  - *Stated limits.* Density cannot be implemented. The results hold almost surely through LTMADS (pp. 15–16). Regularity is needed for necessity and local convexity for sufficiency (pp. 11–12).
  - *Future direction.* A deterministic enumeration of directions (p. 16). This is the OrthoMADS problem.
  - *Limit not stated.* The reader's check under D4 on Theorem 4.9 at boundary points.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder, bounded by counterexamples): evidence ✅, strong. The ladder gains two second-order rungs. Each condition is bounded by a small example: Ex. 4.5 on which cone the condition holds in (p. 10), Ex. 4.7 on why regularity is not enough (p. 11), and Ex. 4.10 with Prop. 4.11 on GPS converging to a saddle (pp. 13–15). - Method 2 (Constraint semantics first): variant ⚠. The choice of constraint treatment decides which theory is possible. The barrier gives no constraint gradients, so Lagrangian conditions are replaced by feasible-direction conditions (p. 9). Only unrelaxable constraints are covered.
- new-pattern candidate: **Generalize-and-recover** (c05). GPS is recovered as the MADS instance Δᵖ = Δᵐ (p. 4), and GPS failure examples separate the two classes (pp. 13–15).
- new-pattern candidate: **Rival-failure toy examples** (c05). A hand-tuned GPS instance, with its parameters and poll order, is proved by induction to converge to a saddle (pp. 13–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Central-difference symmetrization for zeroth-order methods: add the two Taylor expansions, use convexity of ∂²f to merge the two Hessian elements, bound the known side below by the poll's failure, and derive a first-order contradiction from the other side (pp. 12–13).
- [D4] When a barrier hides constraint gradients, first prove set-constrained optimality conditions based on feasible directions (Thms. 4.4 and 4.6), each paired with an example showing which cone it cannot be extended to (pp. 9–11).
- [D4] To show that a rival method fails, choose all of its free parameters (τ, w±, mesh rule, poll order) adversarially. Prove convergence to a bad point by induction over a periodic block of iterations, presented as a table of trial values (Prop. 4.11, pp. 14–15).
- [D4] Audit your own strict-inequality steps on cones. Nonsingularity gives strictness only on a subspace, so check a boundary example such as f = ab on R²₊ (reader's check, p. 13).
