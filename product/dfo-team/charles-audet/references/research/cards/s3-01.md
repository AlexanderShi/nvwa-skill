# Paper cards · batch s3-01 · Charles Audet · 2001: two OCR-recovered papers from the Rice postdoc years (supplement re-read)

**Batch intent**: Batch s2-01 marked these two papers unreadable because their PDF text layers were font garbage. Both text files have now been re-extracted by OCR, and these cards supersede the s2-01 stubs. The papers come from the same moment, Audet's NSERC postdoc at Rice, but from his two research lives:
- S028 is the engineering-venue companion of the mixed-variable pattern search paper (card S011). The GPS/MVP algorithm is applied to a thermal-insulation design from the cryogenics literature.
- S050 is an exact global-optimization paper that continues his thesis and S041 on disjoint bilinear programming.

The batch asks three questions:
- In S028, where does engineering knowledge enter: the Search, the Poll's neighbor set, or the model? How is a published design turned into a validation baseline?
- In S050, how are a budget-capped heuristic cutting phase and an exact branch and bound combined without losing finiteness? Is this a pre-DFO analogue of Method 1?
- Do the two papers add evidence to existing candidates? The ones in view are *user-defined neighborhood defines optimality*, *proposer–certifier protocol*, *reformulation as a lens*, *certify or improve the published solutions of literature instances* and *report cost-to-find vs cost-to-certify*.

**Focus dimensions**: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line), D8. These are supplement short cards; both papers were nevertheless read end to end. D6 and D7 observations appear only in the digest file.
**Material roles**: S028 supplement · S050 supplement.

### S028 · Mixed variable optimization of the number and composition of heat intercepts in a thermal insulation system (Optimization and Engineering 2001, 10.1023/a:1011860702585)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Kokkolaras (U. Michigan), J.E. Dennis Jr. (Rice) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Audet is listed at École Polytechnique de Montréal (p. 1), and the work was done during his NSERC postdoc at Rice (p. 24). Funding: DOE, AFOSR, Boeing, Sandia, ExxonMobil, CRPC (p. 24). The algorithm is the Audet–Dennis MVP paper [4] (card S011), so S028 is the application companion. The nonsmooth GPS analysis [3] and the filter paper [2] are cited as companion extensions (pp. 8, 24). Card b2-02 (S213, Abramson's thesis, pp. 133–137) shows that the next thesis reused this problem, its initial design and its neighbor definition, for comparability (that card's wording). **Read**: all 26 pages (OCR), references included.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: The entry is a modeling limitation shared by a whole engineering literature. Heat-intercept designs had been optimized only in the continuous variables (locations, temperatures), while the number of intercepts and the insulator types were fixed by parametric studies (abstract, p. 1; pp. 2–3). The cause named is the optimizer: Hilal and Boom's gradient-based method "could not handle categorical variables" (p. 2). The survey of six application papers ends in one diagnosis: categorical variables "are not treated as optimization variables but as parameters" (p. 3).
  - The paper then defines categorical variables operationally: variables for which f or the constraints "can not be evaluated unless the variables take one of a prescribed enumerable set of values" (p. 1, again p. 2). The model cannot evaluate "1.5 intercepts" or a steel–aluminum mixture, so branch and bound is "out" (p. 4).
  - For the same reason the authors refuse the name "nonlinear mixed integer programming" and use *mixed variable programming* (p. 4).
  - The stated purpose is demonstrative: to show that MVP applies "to a broad class of optimization problems in engineering" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: The MVP/GPS algorithm of [4] is applied to a formulation in which n, the number of intercepts, is itself a variable. The dimension therefore changes during the run: n + 1 categorical and 2n continuous variables, 3n + 2 in all (pp. 6–8). Design moves (pp. 9–13, 16):
  - *Poll = continuous mesh poll + user-defined discrete neighbors + extended poll.* The neighbors are built by three constructive rules (pp. 11–13):
    - remove an intercept with the insulator to its left, redistributing its thickness;
    - add an intercept with an insulator of the same type as the i-th, halving the i-th thickness;
    - change one insulator type.

    For a 4-intercept incumbent this gives 23 poll points (9 continuous, 5 type changes, 5 additions, 4 removals; Table 1, pp. 11–13). A neighbor within ε of the incumbent triggers an extended poll in the continuous variables. The paper describes this as "an incomplete local parametric study controlled by the user" (p. 10).
  - *Directions*: four positive spanning sets are cycled: [I, −1], a regular simplex basis from [14], and their negatives. This is done "to increase the number of directions for which the convergence results hold" (p. 10).
  - *Search*: deliberately minimal. It makes at most one evaluation, extrapolating a successful continuous step, and only after a success that left the discrete variables unchanged (p. 10). The goal is "to convince the reader that the algorithm works" without engineering intuition in the Search (p. 10).
  - *Mesh schedule*: Δ₀ = 10, with more aggressive refinement at each successive local mesh optimizer (10 → 5 → 1.25 → 0.15625), stopping at the fourth one (p. 16; the formula of eq. (12) is garbled).
  - *Domain knowledge in the neighbor set*: conductivity curves (Fig. 4, p. 11) suggest that good insulator sequences have the form Nylon…Teflon…Epoxy…Teflon (set Z, pp. 10–11). Restricting the type-change neighbors to Z shrinks the poll, e.g. 5 instead of 10 type-change points (p. 13).
  - *Model refinements once n is free*: a cap n_max (p. 20), and an extra cost term under which an intercept is added only if it gains at least γ percent (p. 21; the formula is OCR-garbled).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - Bounds on n, Δx and T are kept as explicit bounds. The other constraints (total thickness ≤ L, ordered temperatures) are enforced by setting f = +∞, which "keeps every iterate feasible" and is justified in [4] (p. 7).
  - Insulator–temperature compatibility is checked before a neighbor is accepted (p. 7).
  - Physical data: tabulated conductivities fitted by cubic splines (p. 10), and a piecewise-constant cycle-efficiency coefficient C with a jump at 71 K (eq. (10), p. 14).
  - The conclusion states the blackbox stance: no derivatives, "no smoothness or finite value assumptions", and the blackbox may return +∞ or "no value at all" (pp. 23–24).
  - No theorem is stated in the paper. The convergence analysis is delegated to [4] and [3] (pp. 4, 8, 24).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: The paper proves nothing itself. Its one theory passage is the *Remark* at the end of §4.2 (p. 13), added after a referee "raised an important point concerning the convergence" (p. 24).
  - *The gap*: after n changes, neighbor points need not lie on the mesh, and an infinite sequence alternating between adding and removing an intercept "could converge to a solution that does not satisfy any optimality conditions" (p. 13).
  - *The repair*: add finitely many extra columns to the mesh-generating matrix S so that every neighbor lies on the mesh. These directions "do not need to be used in the POLL step" (p. 13).
  - *The hedge*: the concern "does not apply to the practical instances solved in this paper", since n does not oscillate (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments** (pp. 13–23):
  - *Setup*: Matlab 5.3 (pp. 9, 13).
  - *Validation*: the published Hilal–Boom designs are reproduced with the insulator fixed and n capped (Tables 2–3, pp. 14–15). Their reported solutions are re-evaluated with the authors' own routine "in order to have a common comparison basis" (p. 14). For n = 1 the readable rows give MVP 1910 vs 1927 and 140 vs 145 (pp. 14–15); the other MVP rows are OCR-garbled. The 65% gain is measured against the recomputed 68.6 (p. 17), which p. 17 calls the value "reported by Hilal and Boom", although their own printed figure was 71.9 (p. 14).
  - *Landscape probe*: a plot of the n = 1 landscape, zoomed, shows a local optimum caused by the jump in C at 71 K (p. 15; Fig. 5, p. 16).
  - *Four settings*, one run each, reported as objective vs evaluations with n annotated (Figs. 6–8):

    | Setting | n | P | Evaluations | Source |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | unrestricted neighbors | 29 | 23.51 | 221,399 | Table 4, p. 19 |
    | neighbors restricted to Z | 20 | 24.85 | 18,310 | Table 5, p. 19 |
    | Z and n_max = 10 | 10 | 25.36 | 2,350 | Table 6, p. 21 |
    | cost term γ = 0.1% / 0.5% / 1% | 14 / 9 / 7 | 25.43 / 27.06 / 27.40 | 15,912 / 4,206 / 11,513 | Table 8, p. 23 |

  - *No competing solver.* The evaluation counts are to be read "in a qualitative and not quantitative manner" (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an application and a demonstration of the algorithm. Treating n and the insulator types as variables gives a 65% lower refrigeration power than the literature design (pp. 1, 17, 23), and the algorithm selects insulator sequences that match the conductivity ranking over temperature. The authors call this choice obvious in retrospect but absent from previous studies (pp. 3, 17–18, 24).
  - *Stated limits*:
    - local optima and dependence on the start (pp. 15, 18);
    - a better solution costs more evaluations (pp. 18, 24);
    - the implementation lacks a nontrivial Search, mesh coarsening and a cache (pp. 18–19);
    - once n is free, the model itself needed refining (pp. 20–23).
  - *Future directions*: treat the cross-section area as a variable (p. 23); use richer cost and weight terms (p. 23); terminate sooner with a good solution (p. 20); a filter version for general constraints and the use of derivative information ("work is underway", p. 24); a parallel implementation (p. 24).
  - *A claim without a run*: the conclusion says a higher extended-poll trigger was "demonstrated here" to cost more evaluations for a better solution (p. 24). Every §5 run uses ε = 1% (p. 17), and I found no run with another ε. Card S011 reports ξ = 1% and 5% runs in the companion paper.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: The Search/Poll split is stated in its classic form. The Search "determines the global quality of the solution", and the Poll "guarantees theoretical convergence" (p. 9; again pp. 23–24). The deliberately empty Search shows the Poll alone is enough (p. 10). The variant is that the paper's performance lever, domain knowledge, goes into the *Poll's neighbor set*, not the Search: "Problem specific information can lead to a less expensive POLL STEP" (p. 24). As with card S029, the Poll is changed, but here the change also redefines what "local" means.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (early form): Categorical variables get their own semantics because the model cannot be evaluated at relaxed values (p. 4). Constraints are split into explicit bounds and constraints handled by f = +∞ (extreme barrier, p. 7). The blackbox may return no value (pp. 23–24).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: A referee-found hole (add/remove oscillation off the mesh) is repaired by enlarging the mesh generator without changing the Poll (p. 13; acknowledgment p. 24). Spanning sets are cycled to widen the set of directions covered by the convergence result (p. 10), a small step toward the dense directions of MADS.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: A real engineering problem from the literature is used, with its physical data sources (pp. 10, 14), but it is not released as an artifact here. It was reused by the next thesis (card b2-02, S213, pp. 133–137).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: The currency is function evaluations, with objective-vs-evaluations plots (Figs. 6–8). The baseline is re-evaluated with a common routine (p. 14), and the literature baseline is faulted for reporting no evaluation counts, "making comparison impossible" (p. 15). But there is one run per setting, no solver from another family, and the counts are called qualitative (p. 19).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **user-defined neighborhood defines optimality** (b2-02 S213; c2-01 S011 as *user-defined optimality notion as a priced knob*). The user defines "local optimality" for the discrete variables (p. 9), and speed and quality "depends on the user-defined set of neighbors" (p. 13). The price is measured: neighbors restricted by knowledge cost about 90% fewer evaluations for about 5.7% worse f (p. 18).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **certify or improve the published solutions of literature instances** (c01 S015; c2-01). The published designs are re-evaluated with the authors' own routine, reproduced (pp. 14–15), then improved by 65% (p. 17).
- new-pattern candidate (new): **new decision freedom exposes the model's missing costs**. Once n is free and the mesh keeps shrinking, the optimizer would add ever more intercepts at marginal spacing, imitating a multilayer insulator (p. 20). The authors fix the *model* with a cap or a cost term, not the algorithm, and report the loss/evaluation trade-off (pp. 20–21, 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before claiming improvement over a published design, re-evaluate its reported solution with your own evaluator and report both values. S028 prints the originally reported values next to the recomputed ones (p. 14).
- Validate a new method by restricting it to the old method's setting (fixed insulator, capped n) and checking that it recovers the published solutions before freeing the new variables (pp. 14–15).
- For a variable-dimension categorical problem, write each neighbor move as a constructive rule that also sets the continuous values of the new component (split a layer, copy its type, halve its thickness; or redistribute the thickness on removal) (pp. 12–13).
- Encode domain knowledge as a restriction of the neighbor set and report its price in evaluations and objective (≈90% fewer evaluations for ≈5.7% worse f, p. 18).
- If a mesh-based argument needs heuristic trial points to lie on the mesh, add finitely many generator directions that are never polled (p. 13).
- Plot a low-dimensional slice of the objective and zoom in to find discontinuity-induced local optima before blaming the optimizer (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "A way to view this step is that the POLL on the categorical variables is an incomplete local parametric study controlled by the user." (p. 10)
- "In retrospect, it is to be expected that the model objective will have to be refined when used with additional optimization variables." (p. 23)

### S050 · Concavity cuts for disjoint bilinear programming (Mathematical Programming 2001, 10.1007/pl00011428)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Alarie, B. Jaumard, G. Savard (École Polytechnique / GERAD) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is GERAD G-99-38, dated September 1999, with Audet at Rice (p. 1) on an NSERC postdoctoral fellowship (p. 2). It builds directly on Audet et al. [4] (card S041, *Math. Program.* 85, 1999), whose branch and bound BB it reuses (pp. 4, 18). Card b2-01 records that the thesis listed adding concavity cuts to Al(BILD) as future work (thesis p. 174), and this paper carries that out. The concavity-cut side of the thesis is §2.4 of S070 (card b2-01) and GERAD G-94-52 (card S127). **Read**: all 27 pages (OCR), references included.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: The problem is disjoint bilinear programming, BILD (p. 3). The entry is a two-sided critique of the literature (pp. 3–4):
  - Cutting-plane methods: Konno's converges to an ε-optimal solution, and the similar Vaish–Shetty method "again" has no guarantee of finite convergence. Sherali–Shetty obtain finiteness only with costly disjunctive cuts.
  - Polyhedral annexation: one method meets numerical difficulties as the size grows, and Gallo–Ülkücü's has no finite convergence.
  - Every method except Thieu's and [4] assumes that X and U are bounded (p. 3).

  The paper's question is whether concavity cuts make the exact BB of [4] better: "to study the usefulness of incorporating concavity cuts in a branch and bound procedure" (p. 5). The difficulty is named in advance: cutting planes converge slowly with nearly parallel, shallow cuts, and on unbounded domains they may never cut enough (p. 11).
- **D2 Core idea**: Algorithm CBA is a budget-capped cutting phase CC followed by the unchanged BB of [4] (pp. 11–14).
  - *CC* alternates Konno's mountain climbing (p. 10) with concavity cuts in *both* X and U. The two sets of cuts come from BILD's two symmetric linear maxmin reformulations (abstract, p. 2; §2, pp. 5–6).
  - *Stopping rules* C1–C5 cover unboundedness found, an empty domain, a cap on cut attempts, κ consecutive shallow cuts, and no new cut (p. 11).
  - *Restarts* reflect the last vertex through the last cut, to start far away (p. 12).
  - *Cut retention before BB* (pp. 13–14): keep the farthest cut that removes the incumbent and the closest one that does not. Then add cuts only while their average cosine with the kept set stays below 1/2 ("half the cardinality", p. 13). Fix the new dual variables to 0 by complementary slackness, since the cuts may be treated as strict (p. 14). BB still branches only on the original constraints (p. 14).
  - *Unboundedness test*: for unbounded domains, CBA⁺ solves FORBIDDANCE auxiliary problems over *truncated* cones K_U = {u ≥ 0 : uᵗB ≤ 0, 1 ≤ uᵗ1 ≤ 2}. This replaces [4]'s highly degenerate auxiliary problem (Prop. 3.3, p. 9; Table 1, p. 15). The incumbent is set to 0, which gives deeper cuts, and the run stops at the first positive value (pp. 14–15).
  - *Degeneracy removal*: at a degenerate vertex, cut at a non-degenerate neighbor found by single pivots, instead of enumerating all neighbors, which is NP-complete (pp. 15–16).
  - A 2-D worked example runs the whole pipeline (pp. 16–18).
- **D3 Assumptions**: X and U are polyhedra with no boundedness assumption (pp. 3–4). Cuts are defined at non-degenerate vertices; the degenerate case is handled separately (pp. 5, 15). The analysis separates the two usual boundedness hypotheses: bounded X and U for BILD, versus a bounded LMM set containing the optimum. The paper shows they "are not equivalent" (pp. 6–7) and that Falk's single-polytope LMM is less general than LMM_xy (p. 7). Prop. 3.3 assumes X bounded (p. 9).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**:
  - *γ-extension as an LP* (Prop. 2.2, pp. 5–6). For fixed θ, the condition *the minimum of bᵗy over Y(x̄ + θλ) is at most a threshold* is replaced by *some y ∈ Y(x̄ + θλ) has bᵗy at most that threshold*. θ and y can then be optimized jointly, so each γ-extension is one linear program.
  - *Validity of the cut* follows from the convexity of f (p. 6).
  - *Cut depth depends on boundedness.* Prop. 3.2 shows that bounded U gives deep cuts in X, and vice versa. Three γ-extension types are distinguished, and type (c), finite but not deep, "occurs when the domain U is unbounded". A one-variable example shows it (p. 8).
  - *Unboundedness* is decided by the sign of an optimum over a truncated cone, via a ray argument that uses linearity in u (Prop. 3.3, p. 9). FORBIDDANCE is recast as the nonemptiness of a d.c. set (Prop. 3.4, pp. 10–11).
  - *Each cut removes a local optimum* (Lemma 4.1 and Prop. 4.2, pp. 12–13).
  - *Finite exactness* (Thm. 4.3, p. 14): the number of cuts is capped, the cuts never remove the true optimum (Horst–Tuy), and BB is finite by [4]. Thm. 4.4 extends this to the Table 1 sequence of problems (p. 15).
- **D5 Experiments** (pp. 18–26):
  - *Setup*: C with CPLEX 5.0 on a SUN Ultra 1/167, with [4]'s BB code and problem generator. Each series has 10 random instances, reported as mean and standard deviation of time, cuts and nodes (pp. 18–19).
  - *CBA vs BB alone*: CC alone solves 4 of 5 series, taking seconds where BB alone takes hours (Table 2, p. 19).
  - *Scale*: sparse instances up to 500 variables in each set with 100 constraints, dense ones up to 60 variables per set with 60 constraints (abstract, p. 2; Tables 3–5, pp. 20–21).
  - *Unbounded cases*: Tables 6–9 (pp. 22–26).
  - *Time-outs* are reported, at 2 h and 6 h (pp. 19, 21).
  - *Anomalies are re-run*: a cell looked unusual, so 15 more instances were generated and the revised figure reported (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and computational, with supporting propositions: a cut-plus-BB algorithm, an easier unboundedness test and a degeneracy-removal procedure (abstract, p. 2). Instances are "significantly larger than those solved by algorithm BB alone" (p. 21).
  - *Limits are stated in the results*:
    - once BB is called, time "increases very rapidly" (p. 20);
    - the kept cuts enlarge BB's subproblems, so the BB instances can be slower despite fewer nodes (pp. 19–20);
    - raising the cut cap to 1000 left three instances unsolved at about 42,500 s, which justifies the cap (p. 20);
    - degeneracy removal "fails systematically" on F_XU for D ≤ 30% (p. 24).
  - *No conclusion or future-work section*: the report ends after §5.3 and goes straight to the references (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (analogy outside DFO): The same division of labour appears in exact optimization. A free, heuristic, budget-capped phase (mountain climbing, reflection restarts, cuts) does most of the work, and a rigorous phase (BB) carries the guarantee. Finiteness follows from the cap on cuts plus BB's own finiteness, so the heuristic can never break it (Thm. 4.3, p. 14).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: Results are stated relative to explicit hypotheses. The two boundedness assumptions are shown not to be equivalent (pp. 6–7), cut depth is tied to boundedness, and a one-variable example exhibits the non-deep case (Prop. 3.2, p. 8). There is no counterexample to a published theorem.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: The ablation is disciplined: BB vs CBA on the same generator, mean ± standard deviation over 10 instances, time-outs and anomalies reported (pp. 19–23). The currency is CPU time (an exact method), however, and there is no competitor from another family.
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **proposer–certifier protocol** (c01 S015; b2-01 S070). "CC has however found the optimal solution and BB is only called to rigorously confirm its optimality" (p. 20).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **report cost-to-find vs cost-to-certify** (c2-01 S011). CC and BB times are tabulated separately (Tables 2, 4, 6, 8; pp. 19–25). Where CC found the optimum, the BB column is the price of certification (p. 20).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **reformulation as a lens (transfer tests, not just solutions)** (a2-01 S041, S127). The two symmetric maxmin reformulations each supply a family of concavity cuts (abstract, p. 2; p. 4), and FORBIDDANCE is re-read as a d.c.-set question (Prop. 3.4, pp. 10–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Put a heuristic phase with a hard cap (number of cuts or iterations) in front of an exact method. Prove finiteness from the cap plus the exact method's own finiteness, and show that the heuristic never removes the optimum (Thm. 4.3, p. 14).
- Select a *diverse* subset of generated cuts before passing them on. Keep the cut that removes the incumbent and lies farthest from it, plus the nearest cut that does not remove it, then add cuts only while their average cosine with the kept ones stays below 1/2 (pp. 13–14).
- In a decision version (*is there a positive value?*), set the incumbent to 0 instead of the best value found. The cuts get deeper, and the run can stop at the first positive point (pp. 14–15; example p. 18).
- If an auxiliary problem over a cone has a degenerate optimum at the apex, intersect the cone with the slab 1 ≤ 1ᵗu ≤ 2. The decision then reduces to the sign of a bounded problem's optimum (Prop. 3.3, p. 9).
- At a degenerate vertex, cut at a non-degenerate neighbor reached by single pivots instead of enumerating every neighbor (pp. 15–16).
- When a table cell looks anomalous, generate extra instances, say so, and report the revised statistic next to the original (p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "The aim of this paper is to study the usefulness of incorporating concavity cuts in a branch and bound procedure for the disjoint bilinear program." (p. 5)
- "For all instances, CC has however found the optimal solution and BB is only called to rigorously confirm its optimality." (p. 20)
