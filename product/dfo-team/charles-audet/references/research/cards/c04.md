# Paper cards · batch c04 (core)

Batch intent: See how Audet's group, in 2025, builds research infrastructure rather than a new algorithm: a public engineering benchmark (SOLAR), a benchmarking methodology with its software (RunnerPost), a modeling framework and metric for mixed-variable hierarchical inputs (meta distance), and an OR application to the group's own community (ISMP 2024 scheduling released to MIPLIB). ### D007 · Scheduling ISMP 2024 (Les Cahiers du GERAD G-2025-35, to appear in Optima 2025, 10.13140/rg.2.2.19281.03688)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M. Gervais-Dubé, A. Hertz, S. Le Digabel, A. Legrain (all GERAD and Polytechnique Montréal, p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: April 2025 technical report (p. 1) describing how the schedule of ISMP 2024 in Montréal (close to 1,600 participants, more than 1,300 talks, 5 days) was built by mixed-integer programming (abstract, p. 3). It is not a DFO paper: no blackbox, no direct search; the solver is Gurobi 11.0.0 (p. 13). The organizers' roles of the authors are not stated in the text. It continues the ISMP 2012/2015 model described in a bachelor's thesis (Tesch 2013, [6]) and replaces the 2018 tool "the Scheduler" (p. 3). **Read**: all 17 file pages, appendix (nomenclature, pp. 15–16) included. Text-file caveats: display formulas are partly garbled (Table 2.1 p. 8; ordering constraints (2.1)–(2.2) p. 9; room bonus (4.3)–(4.4) p. 11); Figures 1.1, 2.1, 4.1, 5.1–5.3 are not in the text (captions only).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is scale, stated in the abstract: "Building a reasonable schedule with these figures cannot be done by hand" (p. 3). The problem is first stated in words: regular sessions only (keynotes, plenaries, panels and meetings are placed by the organizers), 4 clusters, 29 streams, sessions of 3 or 4 talks with a chair, 13 time blocks, 32 rooms; each talk gets a (stream, room, time block) triplet (p. 4). Talks are invited (to a session or to a stream) or contributed; participants chose 2–6 keywords and up to two preferred streams (p. 4). Five general constraints are listed, the fifth hedged: "If possible, any pair of sessions belonging to the same stream should not be held during the same time block" (pp. 4–5). Soft goals: sessions with one research area, full sessions, talks in a matching stream, suitable rooms (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: write the requirement list in two tiers (hard constraints numbered 1–4, *if possible* wishes, then objectives) before any notation, so each later modeling device can be traced to one line of that list.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: A three-stage pipeline (p. 5).
  - *Candidate groups by a combinatorial necessary condition.* Build a graph on talks with an edge when two talks share a keyword; a "promising" group must share a keyword, so it must be a clique; enumerate cliques of size 1 to U_talk = 4, keep pre-assigned groups whole, and filter pairs by requiring at least two common keywords because pairs can be quadratic in number (§2.1, pp. 5–6).
  - *Variable reduction by keyword mining.* Each stream gets the union of keywords of talks that name it; a group's possible streams K_ℓ are the intersection of its talks' possible streams (§2.3, p. 7).
  - *Selection MIP.* Binary x_{ℓ,k,j} (group ℓ in stream k, block j), y_{k,j} (session exists), z_{k,j} (session full), s_k (stream used); maximize affinity bonuses c(ℓ,k), full sessions and a stream-copy penalty w(k), with each talk in exactly one selected group, session size between L_j = U_talk − 1 and U_j, at most |R| parallel sessions, no participant in two sessions of a block (§2.2–2.3, pp. 6–7).
  - *Soft constraint 5 through *stream copies*.* A stream whose blocks are full gets copies; the i-th copy costs w(k) = −i, so parallel sessions of a stream are allowed but discouraged (§2.5, p. 9).
  - *Participant requests as variable fixings or covering constraints* (unavailability, required blocks or streams), plus precedence constraints for ordered invited groups (§2.6, p. 9); talk order inside a session maximizes shared keywords between consecutive talks, and chairs follow five rules (§2.7, pp. 9–10).
  - *Stability in re-optimization.* After publication, the bonus becomes time-indexed: +6 if the group sits in the same stream and block as in the last published schedule, +5 if same stream and same day, otherwise unchanged (§3, p. 10).
  - *Room assignment MIP.* Rooms ranked by capacity, sessions of a block ranked by the size of their stream; the bonus decays exponentially with the rank difference (as extracted, from 2^7 for the best to 1 for the worst choice), plus α·y_{k,p,r} for three consecutive sessions of a stream ("without holes", Def. 4.1) in the same room (§4, pp. 10–12).
  - What is not changed: standard MIP solved by a commercial solver; which ideas come from the 2012/2015 model [6] is not stated (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when a set-partitioning model has too many columns, generate columns from a cheap necessary condition (cliques of a similarity graph) and let the MIP choose; state the filter's cost in the limits section, as done here on p. 15.
  - Transferable technique: for re-optimization of a published plan, reward staying close to the previous solution in the objective (+6 same slot, +5 same day) instead of hard-fixing it, so new requests can still move items.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Affinity is measured only by shared keywords and preferred streams (pp. 5, 7–8). Bonuses are hand-set: b_suggested = 5 for the first and 3 for the second preferred stream, "the stream bonuses were manually adjusted by analyzing assignments for different values of bsuggested" (p. 8); Table 2.1 gives internal-affinity bonuses per session configuration, e.g. {2,2} → 4 + b_group({1,2}) + b_group({3,4}); the {4} entry combines −8·b_group of the four talks with 3·Σ b_group over triples, a choice the text does not explain (p. 8). U_talk = 4; the last block of Monday–Thursday, J_short = {3, 6, 9, 12}, holds one talk fewer (p. 15). The two-stage split assumes room choice does not feed back into talk assignment; feasibility of stage 2 is guaranteed by the stage-1 bound on parallel sessions (p. 10). α = 200 and a rounded exponent b ≈ −0.2 are used (p. 13). The authors concede that cliques are "a strong condition" (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: when a later stage of a decomposition could be infeasible, add to the earlier stage the aggregate constraint that guarantees its feasibility (here: parallel sessions ≤ |R|) and say so in one sentence.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: No theorem or proof. Modeling devices only: the clique bound Σ_{i=1}^{U_talk} C(|I|, i) on candidate groups, with two reasons why far fewer appear in practice (p. 6); linking inequalities that make y and z indicators of non-empty and full sessions (p. 7); precedence written as: if group ℓ_i sits in block j′, then ℓ_{i′} sits in a later block, plus ℓ_i never in the last block (p. 9); total orders on rooms and sessions to express the goal of large rooms for large streams as a rank-matching bonus (p. 11); sets P(3, S^k) of consecutive session triples to reward room continuity (p. 12). Reader's notes: (4.3) is derived with |R| − 1 while (4.4) divides by |R| (p. 11), and the numerical section uses b = −7/31 rounded to −0.2 (p. 13), consistent with (4.3); one constraint indexes x_{ℓ,j,k} instead of x_{ℓ,k,j}, and the domain of x is written {0,1}^{|I|×|J|×|K|} although x is indexed by groups ℓ ∈ L (p. 7). These look like typos, not modeling errors.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: One real instance, reported as an initial optimization that "do not reflect the real schedule of ISMP 2024" because about seventeen re-optimizations followed; keynotes and special sessions removed (p. 13). Gurobi 11.0.0 on an i7-12700 with 20 threads (p. 13). Talk assignment: 3,000 s budget, gap 0.2351%, 32,111 rows, 1,927,811 columns, 8,366,329 nonzeros; 1,265 talks, 198 with a preferred stream, all 198 assigned to one of them (p. 13). Keyword counts are given (invited 1,056 talks: 378/321/212/145 with 2/3/4/5 keywords; contributed: 50/75/44/40) (p. 13). Room assignment: 20.89 s, gap 0.0039%, 11,240 rows, 23,456 columns, 58,400 nonzeros; most streams use one or two rooms, two use four and two use three; Nonlinear Optimization gets the largest rooms (p. 13). Re-optimization: 17 runs, 3 published, about 47 requests; between versions 1 and 2, 37 talks changed day, 30 changed block within a day, 1,173 did not move; between versions 2 and 3, 62, 26 and 1,163 (p. 14). Reader's note: these counts sum to 1,240 and 1,251, not 1,265; the text does not say why. No baseline (manual schedule, the 2018 Scheduler, or published conference-scheduling methods) is compared; the authors say quality is hard to quantify (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: for an applied MIP, report the instance size (rows, columns, nonzeros), time budget and final gap, and for re-optimizations report how many items moved between published versions as the stability metric.
- **D6 Positioning**: Minimal and placed in the abstract: prior ISMP tools (2018 Scheduler; 2012/2015 model [6]) and six conference-scheduling papers cited as a list, with no comparison or critique (p. 3). No related-work section.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Technical-report register. Every constraint of each model carries a one-line gloss ("a talk is assigned to a single session.", "is stream k ∈ K used?") (p. 7; also p. 12). Notation is moved to an appendix (pp. 15–16). The roadmap previews what each section does (p. 5). Hedging is reserved for quality claims: the quality "is difficult to quantify precisely as it relies on the experience of the participants" and some participants may not have been fully satisfied (pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable technique: annotate each constraint of a long MIP with a short plain-language gloss right under it, so readers who skip the algebra can follow the model.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution layers: applied modeling (three MIPs and a re-optimization rule), service (reduced manual work for the committee, p. 14) and data (anonymized instances close to the real models, with all participant constraints, submitted to MIPLIB 2024, p. 15). Limits are concrete: clique-only groups lose information, hard constraints may leave participants unsatisfied, quality not quantified (p. 15). Future work: similarity from abstracts, non-clique groups, soft co-authorship constraints to avoid clashes, and a user-friendly interface for future ISMP committees (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: when an applied project ends, submit anonymized instances of the real model to the standard library of the field (here MIPLIB), so the one-off application becomes a benchmark.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → public benchmark artifacts) — evidence ✅, outside DFO. The real ISMP instances, anonymized and including all participant constraints, go to MIPLIB 2024 (p. 15); the introduction announces "links to MIP formulations for benchmarking purposes" (p. 5).
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first) — variant ⚠. The same classification move applied to requirements: hard constraints 1–4, an *if possible* constraint 5 turned into penalized stream copies (pp. 4–5, 9), and wishes as bonuses (pp. 5, 7–8). No blackbox, so no EB/PB distinction.
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll) — variant ⚠ (weak). A heuristic generates candidates (clique enumeration, keyword filters, pp. 5–7) and an exact solver chooses among them, but here the heuristic does restrict what the exact stage can reach: optimality holds only for the restricted problem, which the authors acknowledge (p. 15). Method 1 requires the opposite (the heuristic can never break the guarantee).
- Methods 3, 5, 6: not exercised (no theory, no solver comparison, Gurobi rather than NOMAD).
- new-pattern candidate: **stability-weighted re-optimization** — objective bonuses tied to the last published solution (p. 10), with moved-item counts as the metric (p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate: **community-service OR → public instance** — optimize the field's own conference, then release the instance (pp. 3, 15).

**Quotes**:
- "After the initial schedule is published online for participants, it is crucial to minimize the number of changes in subsequent schedules to avoid disrupting their event planning." (p. 10)
- "Furthermore, only cliques were considered to form group of talks. Since this is a strong condition, some information is lost in the process." (p. 15)

### S071 · solar: A solar thermal power plant simulator for blackbox optimization benchmarking (Optimization and Engineering 2025, 10.1007/s11081-024-09952-x; arXiv 2406.00140)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. Andrés-Thió (University of Melbourne), M. Diago (Hydro-Québec), A. E. Gheribi (Centre for Research in Computational Thermochemistry, Polytechnique), S. Le Digabel, X. Lebeuf, M. Lemyre Garneau, C. Tribes (GERAD/Polytechnique) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: arXiv v1 of 4 June 2024 (p. 1). Supported by NSERC Alliance-Mitacs Accelerate grant ALLRP 571311-21 "Optimization of future energy systems" with Hydro-Québec (p. 1). The full plant model is in the 2015 MSc thesis of Lemyre Garneau [47] (pp. 3, 35), so the paper is the release of a nine-year-old model as a benchmark. It is one of SKILL.md's Signature Works; this card is the full-text check. **Read**: all 36 pages (references pp. 32–36 skimmed). The published version was not read; the text file is arXiv v1. Text-file caveats: Figures 2–5 and 7–15 are only captions and axis labels (e.g. pp. 25, 28–32); Tables 6–15 are extracted as run-on rows but readable.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Entry by a gap in benchmarking practice, not by an algorithm. Real engineering models are proprietary, so methods are assessed on analytic test problems (Rosenbrock, Hock–Schittkowski, Moré–Wild) that are "of crucial importance" but "not representative of difficulties encountered in real BBO applications" (p. 2). Published realistic blackboxes each show some desirable features, but none was developed for benchmarking and none has all of them (p. 3; Quote 1). The paper first lists the BBO difficulties the benchmark must carry: mixed and categorical variables, variables that change the dimension, expensive evaluations, noise, failing simulations (the helicopter rotor example with about 60% failures), Boolean constraints and multiple objectives (§1.1, p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: open a benchmark paper with the list of pathologies of real problems, then show a table in which every pathology is present in at least one instance (here §1.1 and Table 6).
- **D2 Core idea & artifact design**: One C++ simulator of a molten-salt CSP tower plant (heliostat field, cavity receiver, two-tank storage, steam generator, Rankine power block; §2, pp. 5–13) parametrized into ten instances solar1.1–solar10.1 (§3.2, pp. 13–16).
  - *Instances differ on purpose*: 5 to 29 variables, 1 or 2 objectives, 0 to 17 constraints, split into simulation vs a priori (linear or not), 0 to 6 stochastic outputs, fidelity available or not (Table 6, p. 15). solar2.1 has an analytic objective; solar10.1 is the unconstrained, penalized version of solar6.1 with "empirically" fixed weights (pp. 15–16).
  - *Command-line contract*: instance number and x are mandatory; seed, rep and fid are optional and default to deterministic, full fidelity (p. 14). Output is one vector y(x) = (F(x), C(x)) with constraints written c_i(x) ≤ 0 (eq. (3), p. 13).
  - *Constraints that exist only to prevent trivial optima*: without them, insulation would go to its maximum and the field would be filled with tiny heliostats (§2.10, p. 11; Quote 2). The cost model is rebuilt from material prices because the NREL SAM model predicts cost from scale and is "of little use as a limiting factor in optimization problems" (pp. 11–12).
  - *Features as switches*: mixed, categorical (turbine type, 8 values) and unbounded integer variables (pp. 17, 22–23); a priori, simulation and hidden constraints (§4.3, p. 23); stochastic outputs with seed and replications, including an adaptive stopping rule (§4.4, pp. 24–25); static surrogates by lower fidelity or fewer replications (§4.5, p. 26); fidelity φ ∈ [0,1] affecting constraints as well as objectives, "a unique feature among BBO benchmarking problems" (§4.6, p. 27).
  - *Reproducibility engineering*: release 1.0 is "frozen in time" and instances are named solarX.1, so any bug fix goes to release 2.0 (pp. 13–14); an in-house random number generator makes outputs platform and compiler independent, checked by `solar -check` (pp. 20–21, 24).
  - What is not changed: the physics sub-models come from the literature (receiver losses from Li et al., tank losses from Zaversky et al., shell-side pressure from Gaddis–Gnielinski, turbine efficiency from Bahadori–Vuthaluru; pp. 8–11).
  - Transferable technique: when you build a benchmark from a physical model, add constraint models whose only job is to make the optimum non-trivial, and replace descriptive sub-models (cost from plant scale) with ones that actually respond to the design variables.
  - Transferable technique: freeze a released benchmark and put the major version into the instance name (solarX.1), so results stay comparable after bug fixes.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Simplifications are stated with their direction of bias: point-source sun, 1 kW/m² irradiance, clear-day attenuation, flat heliostats without aiming or tracking errors, so "the model may overestimate the actual performance" (p. 8). Tanks share a diameter with cold height = 1.2 × hot height; internal natural convection and radiation neglected; constant 6 m/s wind (p. 9). Steam generator by the ε-NTU method, or an idealized 100% exchanger in some instances (pp. 9–10). Steady state, no turbine transients, 95% mechanical-to-electrical efficiency; turbine run at its minimum regime rather than stopped (p. 11). Pumps idealized at 90% efficiency, 55 W per heliostat (p. 12). Creep in the tubes ignored "For the sake of simplicity and time" (pp. 12–13). Problem class (2): min F: ℝⁿ → ℝ̄ᵖ subject to c(x) ≤ 0, integrality and bounds (p. 13). Parameter values (budgets, field areas, demand) are taken from existing plants and listed in the thesis (p. 18). Constraint semantics follow the Le Digabel–Wild taxonomy: a priori constraints are unrelaxable and checked before simulation, simulation constraints are relaxable and quantifiable (QRSK), hidden ones (NUSH) are code failures (pp. 18–20, 23).
  - Transferable technique: state each modeling simplification together with the direction of the error it introduces ("may overestimate the actual performance"), not just the simplification.
- **D4 Analysis (no proofs)**: The "analysis" is a set of diagnostics that certify the benchmark is hard and non-trivial.
  - *Feasibility rates* of 10,000 Latin hypercube points vs a NOMAD 3 run of 400n evaluations: e.g. solar4.1 has 0.112% a-priori-feasible and 0% feasible LH points, versus 84.8% and 28.0% along the NOMAD run; hidden-constraint violations stay below 0.8% (Table 13, p. 24).
  - *Noise level per output*: coefficient of variation at 100 random points × 100 seeds (Fig. 8, p. 25).
  - *Replication cost*: replications and CPU time needed under a Gauss-distribution stopping rule, far larger near x* than near x⁰; solar2.1 at x* with r = 0.5 took almost 12 days to reach the 5 × 10⁴ cap (Table 14, pp. 25–26).
  - *Fidelity*: time roughly linear in fidelity (Fig. 9, p. 27); low fidelity mislabels constraint satisfaction near best solutions, e.g. constraint 13 of solar4.1 is reliable only for fidelity ≥ 0.9 (Fig. 10, pp. 27–28); low fidelity mostly underestimates the solar10.1 objective (Fig. 11, pp. 27, 29).
  - *Non-triviality*: few active bounds and several saturated constraints at x*, with large improvements over x⁰ on solar1.1, 3.1 and 7.1 (Table 15, pp. 28–29).
  - *Local sensitivity*: proportion of feasible points in balls of radius 10⁻⁷ to 0.1 around x* (solar4.1: feasibility requires being very close to x*) and objective change around x* (solar10.1) (Figs. 14–15, pp. 31–32).
  - Transferable technique: certify a new benchmark with four cheap diagnostics: feasibility rate of random sampling vs one solver run, coefficient of variation per stochastic output, fidelity-vs-time/error plots, and active constraints and bounds at the best-known point.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Budget 400n evaluations per instance, 150n for the slow solar5.1; NOMAD 3 stopped on solar6.1 after 1,976 evaluations (Table 13, p. 24). Hardware for Table 14: Apple M2 Max (p. 26). Solver comparison is deliberately minimal: data profiles on solar6.1 from 30 LH starting points for NOMAD 3, NOMAD 4 and CMA-ES, which "do not systematically produce the best known solution"; the data are in the repository (pp. 29–30). Biobjective solar8.1: non-dominated points from several solvers of [19] with 5,000 evaluations, distinct across solvers (p. 30). Best-known values come from named beta-testers, including students of the MTH8418 DFO course (pp. 28, 32). Reader's notes: the figure contents are not in the text file; Table 13 applies LH sampling although some integer inputs are unbounded and LH needs bounds (p. 23), and the bounds used for sampling are not stated.
  - Transferable technique: when a benchmark instance can be solved by many methods, give the best-known value publicly and credit who found it, and invite logs by e-mail to update the table (pp. 28, 32).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continuity with the group's own benchmarking line: textbook, survey of hundreds of applications, NOMAD, the constraint taxonomy and the meta-variable framework are all self- or group citations (pp. 2–3, 17, 23). Other realistic problems are credited, not criticized: groundwater community problems (Fowler et al., with Audet as coauthor), STYRENE, heat intercepts (Kokkolaras–Audet–Dennis; Abramson), the Lockwood plume (pp. 3, 32–35). Other CSP optimization papers "do not provide benchmarking tools" (p. 3). Analytic test sets are praised before being called unrepresentative (p. 2). Benchmarking tools named: performance and data profiles as "now the standard", accuracy profiles, COCO (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: position a benchmark against both neighbors (analytic test sets, and domain papers that optimize the same system without releasing it), and give each neighbor its merit before stating what it lacks.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The main claim is hedged, then made strongly: "it seems that no work exhibits a realistic application specifically developed for BBO benchmarking" followed by "solar is the first realistic blackbox that gathers all of these characteristics in a single family of instances" (p. 3). §1.3 states one "main contribution" (p. 3). §4 switches to a user-manual register (`solar -h`, `solar -check`, `-seed=diff`, `-rep=r`, `-fid=φ`; pp. 21–27) and gives explicit recommendations ("the authors recommend solar3.1 with lower fidelities", STYRENE for frequent hidden-constraint violations, p. 23). The difficulty claim ends with "we believe" (p. 31). A footnote asks users to contact the authors if `solar -check` fails (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: in a benchmark paper, recommend which instance to use for which pathology (e.g. rare vs frequent hidden constraints), including instances from other benchmarks.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution layers: software and data artifact (LGPL code, starting points, best-known values, solver logs; p. 3), a documented engineering model (summarized here, full in the thesis; p. 3), and benchmark diagnostics (§4). Limits are specific and often reframed as features: model simplifications may overestimate performance (p. 8), creep ignored (p. 13), "Some bugs may still be present in the code, as is often the case in real blackbox problems" (p. 23), unbounded variables left to the user (pp. 17–18), coordinates of x* withheld, only values published (p. 28). Future work: new instances; invitation to send logs to nomad@gerad.ca (p. 32). Reader's notes: the Table 11 caption says "Nonlinear a priori constraints" but the table lists simulation constraints (p. 21); the per-instance descriptions of §3.2.1 count relaxable/unrelaxable constraints in ways that do not follow §3.2.3's rule (a priori = unrelaxable, simulation = relaxable) or Table 6, e.g. solar2.1 "14 relaxable and quantifiable" vs m = 12, solar9.1 described with 5 relaxable and 12 unrelaxable constraints vs 10 simulation + 7 a priori, solar6.1 with 2 relaxable and 4 unrelaxable vs 6 simulation + 0 a priori (pp. 14–16, 15, 18–19).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → public benchmark artifacts) — evidence ✅, the strongest in the corpus: a thesis model (p. 3) becomes a standalone executable with a fixed input/output contract (pp. 13–14), classified constraints (pp. 18–23), fidelity surrogates, seeds and replications (pp. 24–27), starting points, best-known values with credits (pp. 28, 32), and a public repository with solver logs (p. 3). Variant ⚠ on two SKILL.md steps: variables keep physical units, and some integer variables have no upper bound, left to the user (pp. 17–18, 23), rather than being scaled to [0,100] (Method 4 step 2). Only best-known values are released, not the coordinates of x* (p. 28), against Method 4 step 5, which asks for a best-known solution.
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first) — evidence ✅: a priori/unrelaxable constraints are checked before simulation and the evaluation is not counted (`cnt eval=false`, outputs set to 10²⁰), simulation constraints are relaxable (QRSK), hidden constraints are code failures (pp. 18–23). Most suggested x⁰ are infeasible (Table 15, p. 29), matching Method 2 step 5.
- Method 5 (Budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking) — evidence ✅: budgets in multiples of n (p. 24), data profiles over 30 starting points, CMA-ES as another-family competitor (pp. 29–30). Variant ⚠: the runs certify the benchmark's difficulty rather than rank solvers.
- Method 6 (The solver as research instrument) — evidence ✅: starting points given in NOMAD parameter syntax (p. 22), NOMAD logs shipped (p. 3), results collected at nomad@gerad.ca (p. 32).
- Research Taste 6 (repeatability) — evidence ✅: frozen release naming, platform-independent RNG, `solar -check` (pp. 13–14, 20–21, 24).
- new-pattern candidate: **benchmark non-triviality certification** — anti-triviality constraints by design (p. 11) plus diagnostics (LH vs solver feasibility, CoV, fidelity mislabeling, active constraints at x*, feasibility in balls) (pp. 24–32).
- new-pattern candidate: **frozen, versioned benchmark IDs** (solarX.1; p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate: **course-to-benchmark loop** — DFO course students as beta-testers and best-known contributors (p. 32).

**Quotes**:
- "In fact, it seems that no work exhibits a realistic application specifically developed for BBO benchmarking." (p. 3)
- "Without them, the solution to many of the problems would be trivial or impractical." (p. 11)

### S092 · A distance for mixed-variable and hierarchical domains with meta variables (Neurocomputing 2025, 10.1016/j.neucom.2025.131208; arXiv 2405.13073)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: E. Hallé-Hannan (PhD student; funded by NSERC, FRQNT and IET PhD scholarships), Y. Diouane, S. Le Digabel (Polytechnique/GERAD), P. Saves (IRIT/ONERA, EU COLOSSUS project) (pp. 1–2) · **Position in trajectory**: arXiv v3 of 31 March 2025 (p. 1). It generalizes the group's own framework [7] (Audet, Hallé-Hannan, Le Digabel, Operations Research Forum 2023), which had meta variables but a single level of hierarchy and no distance (pp. 5, 7, 10). It is the Hallé-Hannan thesis line named in SKILL.md Heuristic 10. A follow-up on incompatibilities between values is announced (p. 27). **Read**: all 33 pages, appendices A–C (pp. 27–29) included, references skimmed. The published Neurocomputing version was not read. Text-file caveats: Figures 1–8 and 12 are extracted as tables or loose labels, but readable; Figures 10–11 are axis ticks only.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Heterogeneous datasets are usually split into homogeneous subsets, which hurts when data is limited or expensive (p. 2; Quote 2). Scope: a domain that is mixed-variable (categorical, integer, continuous) and hierarchical (two points need not share variables or bounds), shown with an MLP hyperparameter example where the optimizer decides which hyperparameters and bounds exist (pp. 2–3). Motivating applications: hyperparameter optimization, a magnetic resonance device whose number of magnets adds variables, an aircraft engine with or without a fan (p. 4). The gap is stated as three defects of existing distances: randomness (hence not interpretable), missing metric properties such as the identity of indiscernibles, or a cost that depends on the dataset or on an optimization subproblem (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: state the research gap as a short list of defects that every existing method has at least one of (here randomness, missing metric axioms, dataset-dependent cost), then build a comparison table with one column per defect (Table 2, p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: Map every point to an *extended point* that also carries its excluded variables, set to a special value EXC, define a distance there, and induce it back through a bijection (pp. 4–5, 9–10, 15, 20).
  - *Roles via the decree property*: meta (decrees, has no decree dependency), meta-decreed (both), decreed (only depends), neutral (neither) (Defs. 1–2, p. 8). Allowing meta-decreed variables is what [7] did not allow (p. 8).
  - *Role graph*: nodes are all variables, arcs are decree dependencies; roles read off DAG positions (root, internal, leaf, isolated) (Def. 5, pp. 11–12). Parents, ancestors, universal sets and restricted sets formalize which values a variable may take given its parents (Defs. 6–10, pp. 12–15); the extended domain collects all extended points respecting them (Def. 11, p. 15).
  - *Included–excluded distance* per variable: d(u,v) if both included, 0 if both excluded, θ if exactly one is excluded, with θ ≥ sup d/2 (Thm. 3, p. 17).
  - *Meta distance*: the ℓ_p aggregate of the per-variable distances, p ≥ 1, and the max for p → ∞ (Thm. 4, p. 19); weights ω scale each 1D distance and hence θ's lower bound (pp. 19–20). The induced distance on the original domain follows from the bijection (Cor. 1, p. 20).
  - What is not changed: standard 1D distances (Euclidean, Hamming) inside the "both included" case (pp. 3, 18); no graph distance, no learned embedding (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: to compare objects that do not share coordinates, extend each to a common coordinate set with a sentinel value, define the metric per coordinate with an explicit rule for sentinel pairs, prove the extension is a bijection, and induce the metric back.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumption 1: the role graph is a DAG, justified by a two-variable example of circular decrees that leads to a contradiction (p. 11). θ ≥ sup{d(μ,ν)}/2 is required for the triangle inequality (pp. 17–18). With an unbounded restricted set the sup is infinite, so θ must be ∞, "a behaviour one would generally avoid"; bounded restricted sets give an ordinary real-valued distance (pp. 18–19). The authors admit that scaling categorical and excluded variables "is ambiguous" (p. 19). A variable with missing entries can be modeled as a decreed variable switched by an extra binary meta variable (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: when you need a structural assumption (acyclicity), put the smallest example that breaks without it right before the assumption.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: Four short results.
  - Thm. 1 (equivalent definitions of a hierarchical domain) is called "a direct consequence of Definitions 5 and 7" (p. 14).
  - Thm. 2 (the transfer mapping is a bijection): injectivity by the variable that differs, surjectivity by deleting the EXC nodes from the role graph and reading off a valid point (pp. 15–16).
  - Thm. 3 (included–excluded distance): case analysis on which of u, v, z are EXC; the only case that needs the lower bound on θ is u, v included and z excluded, d(u,v) ≤ sup d ≤ 2θ (pp. 17–18).
  - Thm. 4 (meta distance): reindex the per-variable distances into one vector a and identify dist_p with ‖a‖_p (p. 19).
  - Reader's notes: the proof of Thm. 3 relies on θ being strictly positive although the statement only asks θ ∈ ℝ₊ with θ ≥ sup d/2, which allows θ = 0 when the included set is a single value (p. 17). The proof of Thm. 4 stops at dist_p = ‖a‖_p; the step from there to the triangle inequality (monotonicity of ‖·‖_p on nonnegative vectors plus Minkowski) is left implicit (p. 19). The text calls the learning rate "a neutral categorical variable" while Fig. 3a lists it as continuous (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: when a new "distance" is built from pieces, isolate the one case in the triangle-inequality proof that forces a parameter bound (here z excluded, u and v included) and state the bound as the hypothesis of the theorem.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Three approaches with simple models so that the comparison is about the distance: Sub (one model per subproblem), Hybrid (the one-level variable-size framework of Pelamatti et al.) and Meta (the new distance, p = 2) (p. 20). Wasserstein-type distances are excluded because each evaluation needs an optimal transport solve (pp. 20–21). Data: 40 datasets = 5 variants of a hyperparameter domain (increasing difficulty) × 4 sizes × MLP (Fashion-MNIST) or CNN (CIFAR10), splits 50/25/25 (pp. 21–22); data generated by training networks on 25% of the image data, 25 epochs, batch 128 (App. C, p. 29). Classification (KNN, 5 labels, budget 100·n_{p,s}, 20 seeds): Meta is best only on variant #1, "Hybrid performed best overall", Sub worst, standard deviations 0.05–0.1 (Table 3, pp. 22–23). Regression (IDW): model parameters tuned by NOMAD on validation RMSE, 200·n_{p,s} evaluations with 33% LHS, 200 instances; data profiles at τ = 10%, 2.5%, 0.5% on validation and on test RMSE (test RMSE recorded at every validation improvement); Meta best, Hybrid worst; up to about 75 minutes per instance on an i7-11800H (pp. 23–25). Data-aggregation curves (medium datasets, 20 seeds): Meta best on MLP; on CNN Hybrid is best early and the three end close, except variant #5 (p. 25). Parameter counts (Table 4, p. 28) and dataset sizes (Table 5, p. 29) are given. Reader's notes: the regression paragraph says "Meta-KNN" where the models are IDW (p. 25); the discussion's claim that Meta performed globally better (pp. 25, 27) is stronger than the classification results of Table 3.
  - Transferable technique: when a DFO solver tunes a model on validation error, log the test error at each validation improvement and draw data profiles for both, which shows overfitting in the same picture.
- **D6 Positioning**: Two comparison tables. Table 1 separates the meta distance from graph distances (one graph for the domain vs many graphs; compares points vs graphs) and declares graph distances and data fusion out of scope (p. 3). Table 2 rates subproblems, the authors' own meta framework [7], variable-size kernels [34], the hierarchical kernel of Hutter–Osborne [23], EMI [19] and the present work on mixed variables, non-shared variables, determinism, levels of hierarchy, number of parameters and cost of d(x,y) (p. 7). Criticisms are mechanism-based: EMI costs O(N³ log N) (p. 7); Hutter–Osborne restrict parents to categorical variables and use pseudo-distances without the identity of indiscernibles (p. 7; Quote 1). The own prior framework is listed with its gaps (no distance, one level) (p. 7). The framework claims to generalize tree-structured, variable-size, conditional/NAS and hierarchical spaces (pp. 4, 25).
  - Transferable technique: include your own previous framework as a row of the comparison table, with its gaps marked, so the generalization is visible.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution sentence comes early: "This work has two main contributions" (p. 2). One running MLP example is carried through Figs. 1, 3, 4, 6 and 7 (pp. 3–18), extended with a dropout variant when ancestors are needed (p. 13). Every definition is followed by a paragraph of numbered comments (five comments numbered First to Fifth, p. 10; "Next, multiple comments on the included-excluded distance are provided.", p. 18). "Corollary 1 unpacks most of the contributions" and a four-step recap follows (p. 20). Hedging concentrates in the discussion: "Theoretically, the meta distance should provide better generaliziability" and the experiments "seems to confirm this" (p. 25).
  - Transferable technique: after the final result, recap in a numbered list the definitions and theorems that were needed to reach it (here the four steps after Cor. 1), so readers see the architecture of the paper.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Layers: modeling framework (roles, role graph, extended domain), theory (bijection, metric axioms), empirical evidence, and code and data at github.com/bbopt/graph_distance (p. 27). Limits stated in the body: θ = ∞ for unbounded sets (p. 19), ambiguous scaling of categorical and excluded variables (p. 19), only simple distance-based models tested (p. 20). Future: incompatibilities between values of different variables (a subsequent work in progress), Gaussian processes and Bayesian optimization, more problems and models (p. 27).

### S101 · Benchmarking constrained, multi-objective and surrogate-assisted derivative-free optimization methods (Les Cahiers du GERAD G-2025-36 2025, no DOI)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: W. Hare (UBC Okanagan), C. Tribes (GERAD/Polytechnique) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: May 2025 technical report (p. 1), funded by NSERC Discovery grants and the same NSERC/Mitacs Alliance grant with Hydro-Québec (#571311–21) as SOLAR (p. 3). Hare is Audet's textbook coauthor ([4], p. 16). It is the methodology and user guide for the group's RunnerPost post-processing tool (pp. 4, 16). SKILL.md lists a benchmarking summary (Opt. Lett. 2026); whether this report is that paper is not stated in the text and was not verified. **Read**: all 17 file pages, references skimmed. Text-file caveats: figures are axis ticks and legends only; some formulas are garbled but readable.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: In DFO the effort is counted in simulation calls, because evaluations are expensive (pp. 3–4). Existing tools implicitly assume no special effort to reach feasibility, a single objective, and no surrogate (p. 10). The entry is a formal extension of known tools to these three settings, not a new tool (abstract p. 3; p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: when a standard evaluation tool silently assumes something (feasible start, one objective, one cost per evaluation), write the extension paper by naming each hidden assumption and fixing the one quantity it breaks (baseline f⁰, the solution-quality measure, the effort count).
- **D2 Core idea & design**:
  - *Setup rules before plots* (§2): problem (1) with f and c possibly ∞; an "evaluation" computes f, c and membership in Ω (p. 4); test problem vs test problem instance (problem plus starting point or seed) (p. 4); representativeness beats size (road-construction example), only DFO algorithms, few algorithms (pp. 4–5); logs must hold at least the evaluation number, the objective value and a feasibility flag, with ∞ or NaN for failed (hidden-constraint) evaluations (p. 5).
  - *Accuracy value* f_acc^N = (f(x^N) − f⁰)/(f* − f⁰) with f⁰ and f* identical for all algorithms; three choices of f* (best final value on the instance, best across instances of the same problem, best in the literature), ordered by construction (Def. 2.1, pp. 5–6); if no algorithm improves f⁰, remove the instance and flag it as difficult (p. 6).
  - *Review* of convergence plots (as monotone staircases), accuracy profiles, performance profiles and data profiles, each with its definition, axis ranges and one worked reading of an example figure (§3, pp. 6–10).
  - *Constrained extension*: η_a = evaluations until the first feasible point; a convergence plot showing the violation h up to η_a and the best feasible f after it; f⁰ = min or max of the first feasible values over algorithms, the max keeping f_acc in [0,1] but pushing accuracies toward 1 (§4.1, pp. 10–12).
  - *Multi-objective extension*: normalized hypervolume inside the ideal–nadir box computed from the union of all fronts, and s_acc = (s_a − s⁰)/(s* − s⁰) with the same initial points for all algorithms (§4.2, pp. 12–14).
  - *Surrogate extension*: time-based profiles when dynamic models are costly to build; for static surrogates an effective count N = N_t + w·N_s with w ∈ [0,1) the relative cost; several weights for several surrogates; N = number of repetitions for Monte Carlo fidelity (§4.3, pp. 14–15).
  - What is not changed: the τ-solve test and the profile definitions of Dolan–Moré and Moré–Wild (pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable technique: when comparing surrogate-assisted methods, count effort as N = N_t + w·N_s with w set to the measured cost ratio, and show the profiles at w = 0 and at the realistic w.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: An evaluation dominates the cost of the algorithm (p. 4); all algorithms run with comparable stopping criteria and logs (p. 5); all algorithms get the same initial point(s) (p. 13); w reflects the true effort ratio (p. 15). Data profiles scale by n_p + 1 because a simplex gradient needs n_p + 1 evaluations (footnote 1, p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices (no theorems)**: Short derivations used as reading aids: −log₁₀(1 − f_acc) ≥ d ⇔ f_acc ≥ 1 − 10^(−d), the number of correct digits (p. 7); τ-solving means f(x) ≤ f* + τ(f⁰ − f*) (p. 8); reading ties off performance profiles (82% + 20% fastest ⇒ 2% ties, p. 8); the switching phenomenon of performance profiles (Gould–Scott), shown by re-plotting without the best algorithm: the ratio range drops from 40 to 12 and the order of the remaining two becomes visible (pp. 9, 13–14); closed-form 2D hypervolume as a sum of rectangles (p. 13). Reader's notes: the violation function is written h(x) = Σ(max{g_j(x),0})² if x ∈ Ω and ∞ otherwise, which as written is 0 on Ω and ∞ outside; the filter/PB convention is x ∈ X, and g should be c (p. 10; g also appears on p. 5). Def. 4.1 sets ι_F(u) = 1 when u ≻ v for some front point v, which, with the dominance convention of p. 12, is the region dominating the front, while the 2D formula computes the region dominated by it; the volume product is indexed up to N (the nadir symbol) instead of η (p. 13). The text refers to case i. while the cases are labeled a)–c) (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: to show that performance profiles depend on the set of algorithms, plot them twice, with and without the best solver, and point to the change of ratio range and ranking.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: None on real problems. The figures use anonymized data "designed to highlight key attributes that can arise in practice", and all profiles are produced by RunnerPost (pp. 4, 16). Illustrations: evaluations vs time (Algo 1 poor per evaluation, good per time) (p. 6); multiple τ reversing a ranking after k = 50 (pp. 9–10); two f⁰ choices giving the same conclusion (pp. 11–12); w = 0 vs w = 1/10, with Algo 1 reaching 0.56 at about 800 groups vs 0.52 near 1,100 (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: in a methodology paper, generate illustrative data that exhibits each pitfall (ranking reversal with τ, switching, baseline choice) and say so explicitly, instead of implying it came from a real study.
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on, never attacks: Dolan–Moré performance profiles are "a significant development" (p. 8), Moré–Wild data profiles "extend" them (p. 9), accuracy profiles are credited to Beiranvand–Hare–Lucet (p. 7), the switching phenomenon to Gould–Scott (p. 9), h to filter methods (p. 10), indicators to the group's own review [3] (p. 12), constraint types to Le Digabel–Wild (p. 10).
