### S160 · Blackbox optimization for origami-inspired bistable structures (Optimization Methods and Software 2025, 10.1080/10556788.2025.2541095; arXiv 2408.15147)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: L. Boisneault, D. Melancon · **Position in trajectory**: The text file is arXiv v1 (dated August 28, 2024, p. 1), not the OMS 2025 version; the published version was not read. It is a mechanical-engineering application run from Melancon's LM2 lab at Polytechnique Montréal. Audet co-supervised with Melancon, and the first author did the FEM, the prototypes and the optimization runs (author contributions, p. 12). Funding: Audet's NSERC Discovery Grant 2020–04448 and Melancon's start-up grant (p. 12). **Read**: all 15 pages; references (pp. 12–15) skimmed. Supplementary Materials S1–S3 (fabrication, test set-up, other patterns) are not in the file: not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem comes from the application. Physically built bistable origami has one dominant stable state because it is fabricated in one state where residual stresses are minimal (abstract, p. 1). The paper's entry is a gap in that literature: most work characterizes how geometry affects multistability, and optimization has been limited to beam-based structures or to maximizing geometrical incompatibility. Manufacturing parameters (panel thickness, hinge type) have not been included (p. 2). The problem is stated as maximizing the bistability ratio ϕ = ΔU/Umax subject to a stress limit and bounds (eqs. 1 and 3, pp. 3, 8). The blackbox argument is the failure rate: derivatives are hard to obtain "due to the numerous fails in the computation" (p. 8), each successful call takes about 110 s on average, and 30% of calls fail (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: justify DFO with measured blackbox statistics (mean time per successful call, failure rate) in one sentence, not with a generic claim that derivatives are unavailable.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper introduces no new algorithm. NOMAD's MADS is used through PyNOMAD coupled to Abaqus (pp. 8–9, Fig. 4a). What is designed is the model:
  - *Model.* A compliant-crease waterbomb replaces the ideal spring-hinge model. The folds are wider, softer regions and the central node is a hole (p. 3, Fig. 1c).
  - *Variables.* Five scaled design variables x = (θ1/α, θ2/α, θ3/α, ω = tc/tf, h/ro) (p. 4).
  - *Dimension reduction.* ri/ro = 1/6 and n = 4 are fixed, and only 1/2n of the pattern is simulated with cyclic boundary conditions (p. 4).
  - *Two-step FEM.* A forming step deploys the flat sheet to height h, and its geometry is taken as a stress-free base. An actuation step then pulls the hole down by 2h (pp. 4–5).
  - *MADS in the paper's own words.* N + 1 random points are drawn on a grid centred on the incumbent; the grid expands on success and shrinks on failure. A point above the yield stress "is discarded" and treated as a failed evaluation (p. 9). Reader's note: this is a simplified description for an engineering audience. Whether the stress constraint was declared EB or PB in NOMAD is not stated.
  - Transferable technique: when the simulator has a symmetric pattern, fix the symmetry order and one ratio, and simulate one fundamental sector with cyclic boundary conditions (p. 4). This trades design freedom for evaluations you can afford, so state it explicitly as a design choice.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Materials.* Linear elastic, with S4 shell elements in Abaqus Standard 2022 (p. 4) and the ratios Ef/Ec = 21.67 and νf/νc = 0.78 (p. 6). PLA faces and TPU creases are used in the prototypes (p. 6).
  - *Stress constraint.* The elastic regime is imposed as σmax < S_Y^i for the region (face or crease) where σmax occurs (p. 5). The optimization problem writes it as σmax ≤ SY (eq. 3, p. 8).
  - *Bounds.* The bounds are there so the optimization "will not diverge and deliver unrealistic results" (p. 8). The manufacturing bound is a minimum hinge width of 0.4 mm, the nozzle diameter (p. 10).
  - *Hidden constraints.* Failures come from "instabilities and nonlinearities" in the simulation (p. 2) and are tied to tall, narrow folds, i.e., large h/ro (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: when a physical limit such as the nozzle diameter sets a bound, name that limit next to the bound (p. 10). A reader can then tell a modelling bound from a technological one, and knows which bound is worth relaxing later.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: No convergence or complexity analysis; none is claimed. The analysis devices are modelling devices:
  - *A dimensionless performance ratio*, ϕ → 0 for marginal bistability and ϕ = 1 for equal energies (p. 3).
  - *Normalizations* of energy by Ef ro νf and of stress by the facet yield limit S_Y^f (p. 6).
  - *Experimental energy* obtained by integrating the measured force over displacement (eq. 2, p. 7).
  - *A qualitative mechanism* for the worst validation error. The larger soft creases of Design III near the bolt add compliance that the FEM does not model (p. 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Simulator validation before optimization.* Three contrasting designs (standard, wide-thin creases, alternating creases) give ϕ = 28.85%, 57.74% and 2.4% (p. 6). Five 3D-printed specimens per design are tested at 0.05 mm/s on a 100 N load cell, with standard-deviation bands (pp. 6–8). The Umax errors are 5.5%, 2.9% and 25.7% (p. 8). The worst case is reported with a proposed cause (p. 8).
  - *Optimization set-up.* Budget 1000 evaluations, x0 = Design I, one Intel i9-9900K (p. 9).
  - *Five scenarios* (p. 10):
    - (a) Manufacturing bounds: ϕ = 64.8%, about 30% failed evaluations.
    - (b) h/ro fixed at 0.6: ϕ = 58.4%, failures down to 1%.
    - (c)–(d) Relaxed bounds: ϕ = 77.7% and 76.8%. Umax collapses from 36.93 mJ to 0.21 and 1.57 mJ.
    - (e) An added constraint Umax ≥ U0max: ϕ = 62.3%, with a two-plateau convergence.
  - *Early convergence.* For (a) and (b), 99% of the final performance is reached after 25 evaluations (p. 10).
  - *Not reported*: seeds or repeated runs, competing solvers, wall-clock totals. Reader's note: Fig. 5 plots only the first 300 evaluations of the 1000-evaluation runs (axis ticks, p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: when a variable drives the crashes, rerun with it fixed and report both sides of the trade. Here a 6.4-point loss in ϕ bought a failure rate of 1% instead of 30% (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: when the optimizer maximizes a ratio, check the numerator and the denominator separately. If it wins by shrinking the denominator (Umax), add a floor constraint on it and rerun (p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Mechanics side.* The paper places itself in the origami-engineering literature: rigid vs deformable origami, bar-and-hinge vs FEM models, and actuation modes (p. 2).
  - *Optimization side.* Only Audet-group and classic DFO sources are cited: MADS, the textbook, NOMAD 4, Alarie et al. 2021 for applications, Audet–Orban 2006 for long evaluation times, and Chen–Kelley 2016 for hidden constraints (p. 8).
  - *Prior optimization work.* It is described factually as limited in scope (beam structures, geometric incompatibility; p. 2). There is no polemic and no comparison with other optimizers.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper is written in physical-science style: numbered Roman sections, Author contributions and Data availability statements (p. 12), and results stated as multiples ("more than doubled", "two-fold", pp. 10–11). MADS is explained to engineers with a 2D picture of polls after success and failure (Fig. 4b, p. 9). The core claim, a general framework for optimizing bistability, is already in the abstract (p. 1). The Discussion calls the method "general" and points to the Supplementary Materials, which were not read (p. 12). Hedging is light.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an application methodology: a validated FEM blackbox plus a NOMAD loop, with a two-fold bistability gain (p. 11). The code (OriMads) is on GitHub. Data are available on request only (p. 12). The blackbox requires commercial Abaqus (p. 4), so it cannot be a public benchmark as it stands.
  - Limits and future work are concrete and stated as extensions (p. 12): a bar-and-hinge model as a surrogate; a categorical variable for the material; curved creases; relaxed bounds via higher-resolution fabrication (e.g., composite laminates); and multistable structures. For the multistable case the authors say multiobjective optimization "tends to lack efficiency" and propose local–global optimization over building blocks instead.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first) — variant ⚠. Hidden constraints (30% crashes) are named and left to NOMAD (pp. 8–9). The stress constraint is quantifiable (σmax is output), yet the text describes a barrier-style discard (p. 9); PB is not discussed.
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → public benchmark artifacts) — variant ⚠. The optimization code is public (p. 12) and x0 plus all final designs are printed (pp. 9–10). But the blackbox depends on commercial Abaqus (p. 4) and the data are on request (p. 12), so this is the proprietary-simulator limitation that Method 4 names.
- Method 5 (Budget-aware benchmarking) — absent/variant ⚠. Evaluations are the currency (convergence against the evaluation count, p. 11), but there is a single run per scenario, no seeds and no competing solver (pp. 9–11).
- Workflow B (Solve-and-diagnose loop) — evidence ✅. The paper runs, diagnoses (crashes tied to h/ro; optimum on a bound; Umax collapse), reformulates (fix h/ro, relax bounds, add a Umax floor) and reruns (p. 10). This is also practice for the taste check on whether n can be reduced by fixing variables.
- new-pattern candidate: **validate the blackbox physically before optimizing it**. Three contrasting designs, five specimens each, per-design errors and a mechanism for the worst (pp. 6–8).
- new-pattern candidate: **price a constraint by relaxing it**. When the optimum hits bounds, rerun with relaxed bounds to show what better manufacturing would buy (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Report failure rate and mean time per successful evaluation as the justification for a DFO solver (pp. 8–9).
- Fix the crash-driving variable and report both the objective loss and the drop in failure rate (p. 10).
- After optimizing a ratio, audit its numerator and denominator, and add a floor constraint if the optimizer gains only by shrinking the denominator (p. 10).
- Validate the simulator on deliberately contrasting designs, including one that is expected to be poor (ϕ = 2.4%), before optimizing (pp. 6–8).

**Quotes**:
- "In the present case, derivatives are difficult to obtain due to the numerous fails in the computation, therefore derivative-free optimization techniques [5] are required." (p. 8)
- "To reduce the number of failed evaluations, a second optimization is launched with the initial height fixed to h/ro = 0.6 along the process." (p. 10)

### S174 · A novel sequential Benders-semidefinite framework with dual feasible proxy for multi-stage transmission expansion planning with contingencies (Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2025–83, 2025; no DOI)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: E. Fathipasandideh, H. Suprême (Hydro-Québec IREQ), H. Dagdougui, D. Asber · **Position in trajectory**: This is a power-systems working paper (December 2025, p. 1). It extends the first three authors' and Asber's PowerTech 2025 paper, which did not include Audet (ref. [5], p. 13). It sits outside the DFO core: exact decomposition plus convex relaxation. Audet is listed with Polytechnique and GERAD affiliations (p. 2); his specific role is not stated. There are no acknowledgments. It is another link to Hydro-Québec, the partner of SOLAR and Micro-PRIAD. **Read**: all 14 pages; references (pp. 13–14) skimmed. Figures 1–3 (runtime and iterations, rank-1 rate and gap, dual bound curves) are captions only in the text file, so only the numbers quoted in the text are available (pp. 9–11).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is multistage AC transmission network expansion planning (TNEP) combined with reactive power planning (RPP) under N–1 security (p. 4). The entry names three gaps:
  - TNEP and RPP are usually solved separately (p. 4).
  - Classical Benders assumes convex subproblems, which AC power flow violates, "leading to weak or invalid cuts" (p. 4).
  - The authors' prior Benders-then-SDP framework lacked explicit N–1 modelling and spent most of its time in repeated SDP solves (p. 4).
  The model is stated as min CapEx(x) + Σ π_{t,ω} θ_{t,ω}(x), with x binary and θ_{t,ω} the SDP-relaxed AC-OPF recourse cost for each stage and contingency (eq. 1, p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: open by naming the two concrete limitations of your own previous paper (here: no N–1 model; SDP cost dominates runtime, p. 4), then make each one a contribution.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key idea: "Any dual-feasible point of the slave SDP yields a valid lower bound and hence a valid Benders cut" (p. 7). A dual-feasible proxy (DFP) builds such a point cheaply in two steps (p. 7):
  - (i) a raw dual estimate, from K ∈ {3, 5} interior-point iterations or, optionally, a small neural network;
  - (ii) a closed-form repair: projection onto the nonnegative and SOC cones, and symmetrization plus an eigenvalue shift for the PSD block.
  Algorithm 1 uses DFP cuts when k mod r ≠ 0 and exact SDP solves with Magnanti–Wong strengthening otherwise (r = 3). It adds adaptive N–1 screening and stops at UB − LB ≤ 0.1% with full AC validation (pp. 6–8).
  - *What is not changed*: the generalized Benders master (a MILP with epigraph multi-cuts, eqs. 2–3) and the SDP relaxation (eq. 4) (pp. 5–6).
  - *Borrowed*: the proxy adapts the dual conic proxy of Qiu, Tanneau and Van Hentenryck (2025) (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: when an expensive subproblem only feeds a lower bound, replace most exact solves by a truncated solve plus a projection onto the dual-feasible set. Validity then holds by construction, and exact solves are kept on a fixed schedule (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Relaxation and discreteness.* The recourse θ_{t,ω}(x) is convex through the SDP relaxation (p. 5). Investments are binary; stagewise budget and buildability constraints live in X (p. 5).
  - *Low-rank term.* A penalty ε tr(W) with ε ∈ {0, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴} promotes low rank "without affecting investment decisions" (p. 6). This is asserted, not shown.
  - *Convergence claim.* "finite convergence under standard convex GBD assumptions" is claimed (p. 7). The assumptions are not listed.
  - *Case parameters* (p. 9): 15-year horizon in three 5-year stages; 10% discount rate; load factor 0.6; voltage limits 0.95–1.05 p.u.; a 10⁻⁵ p.u. resistance added to transformers for conditioning.
  - Reader's note: the outage mask sets the thermal limit to 0 "if line ℓ ⊈ ω" (p. 8). If ω is the outage set this reads inverted; it may be a notation slip.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: No theorem or proof. The correctness argument is informal (p. 7):
  - DFP cuts come from dual-feasible points, so they never overestimate θ, and the master's lower bound is nondecreasing.
  - Periodic exact solves "ensure the cut family approaches the true recourse function".
  Other devices:
  - *Magnanti–Wong strengthening.* The auxiliary problem maximizes cut value at a core point x̄ = αx^k + (1 − α)x̂, with ‖g‖₁ = 1 normalization (eq. 6, pp. 7–8).
  - *Cut acceptance.* A cut is kept only if its violation exceeds 10⁻⁵, to avoid "numerically flat planes" (p. 7).
  - *Termination.* It is certified by a full AC N–1 validation. Any new violator is added and the loop resumes (p. 8).
  - Reader's note: the abstract says "Optimality cuts are always derived from exact dual solutions" (p. 3), but §3.2 adds DFP cuts from repaired, inexact duals in most iterations (p. 7). The two statements do not match.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Set-up.* RTS–24, IEEE 118- and 300-bus systems (p. 9). Hardware: i7-4770 with 16 GB. MOSEK 8.1 with chordal decomposition and tolerance 10⁻⁶; Gurobi for the master (version not stated); PyTorch 2.0 with Adam for the proxy (p. 9).
  - *Baselines.* Three variants of the authors' own method: BDSDP, BDSDP + DFP, BDSDP + DFP + Pareto (pp. 9–10).
  - *Headline.* On IEEE–300, runtime falls from 231.4 to 38.9 minutes and iterations from 34 to 11 (p. 9). Exact SDP solves fall from 548 to 172, with 322 inexact ones (Table 1, p. 10). The text claims "approximately 75%" fewer exact solves (p. 12).
  - *Reader's arithmetic from Table 1 (p. 10)*, full variant: exact solves fall by 100% (RTS–24), 63% (IEEE–118) and 69% (IEEE–300). Total subproblem evaluations fall on IEEE–300 (548 → 494) and IEEE–118 (209 → 193) but rise on RTS–24 (18 → 52). The RTS–24 rows show 0 exact solves, which the schedule of Algorithm 1 (exact solves when k mod r = 0) would not produce unless screening emptied the active set.
  - *Quality.* The rank-1 recovery rate reaches 80.6% on IEEE–300, and the "final master gap" stays within 5–12% (p. 10). Reader's note: this is not reconciled with the 0.1% stopping test of Algorithm 1 (p. 6), and the gap is not defined.
  - *Labels.* Table 1 is captioned "SDP constraint violations", while the text says SDPi "are not violations" (pp. 9–10).
  - *Validation.* Final AC N–1 validation shows 0 violating contingencies and line loading ≤ 99.2% (Table 4, p. 12). The investment tables (Tables 2–3, p. 12) are planning outputs.
  - *Not reported*: training data, features and training time of the learned proxy; a MILP-based baseline, which contribution 4 promises (p. 5); run-to-run variability.
- **D6 Positioning**: The method families are sorted by their weakness (p. 4):
  - heuristics "sacrifice optimality";
  - classical Benders produces invalid cuts on nonconvex subproblems;
  - metaheuristics rely on simplified models;
  - multistage replication enlarges the problem.
  SDP is credited with strong bounds and near-global AC solutions (p. 4). The paper positions itself as the first to combine TNEP+RPP, multistage and N–1 in a Benders–SDP loop ("Unlike classical approaches", p. 5). The acceleration idea is credited to Qiu et al. (p. 5). The self-citations are to the prior PowerTech paper and to Suprême's Hydro-Québec tools (refs. [5], [17]–[19], pp. 13–14).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Structure.* Four numbered contributions (p. 5), then a re-grouping into "two main contributions" in §2.1 (p. 5). Algorithm steps get bold run-in labels ("Goal.", "Key idea.", "Raw dual estimate.", "Feasibility repair.", "Scheduling and guarantees.", p. 7) and a rhetorical subheading, "Why screening is needed?" (p. 8).
  - *Claim language.* Adjectives stand in for evidence ("excellent warm start", "tight margin", p. 7, p. 11), and guarantees are asserted, not proved (pp. 3, 7). This is far from the hedged, proof-carrying style of Audet-led papers.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic engineering: an integrated framework plus accelerations, with no theory and no code release. No limitation is stated in the conclusion. The one future direction is renewable and demand uncertainty via stochastic or robust formulations (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll) — variant ⚠, outside DFO. There is a cheap, always-valid step (DFP cuts) and a periodic exact step (exact SDP + MW) that carries convergence (p. 7). This mirrors the Method 1 rule that a speed-up idea must not be able to break the guarantee. But the paper does not show that the guarantee survives when the cheap step is poor.
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder) — contradiction ✗. Convergence is claimed "under standard convex GBD assumptions" with no hypotheses listed and no proof (p. 7). The abstract's exact-duals claim conflicts with §3.2 (pp. 3, 7). In a paper Audet co-authors but does not lead, the Method 3 discipline is absent.
- Method 5 (Budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking) — contradiction ✗. The comparison is only against ablations of the authors' own method, in CPU minutes (pp. 9–10). The promised MILP baseline is missing (p. 5). There are no external competitors and no variability.
- new-pattern candidate: **cheap valid bound + scheduled exact refresh**. Replace most exact subproblem solves by certified-valid approximations and keep exact solves every r iterations (p. 7). This is the Method 1 idea transplanted to decomposition. It needs evidence from Audet-led work before it counts.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Get a valid Benders cut without solving the slave: take a few IPM iterations, project the duals onto the dual cone (max{0,·} for inequality duals, an eigenvalue shift for PSD blocks), and evaluate the dual objective (p. 7).
- Screen contingencies (or scenarios) with a cheap relaxation (DC/SOC), promote only the top-M violators, and certify at termination with the full model, re-entering the loop on any new violator (p. 8).
- Reject cuts whose violation at the current point is below a threshold (10⁻⁵) to avoid numerically flat cuts (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "Any dual-feasible point of the slave SDP yields a valid lower bound and hence a valid Benders cut." (p. 7)
- "Optimality cuts are always derived from exact dual solutions, preserving convergence guarantees." (p. 3)

### S175 · Benchmarking derivative-free optimization solvers for CSP plant design using the solar simulator (Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2025–70, 2025; no DOI)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: X. Lebeuf, S. Le Digabel, P. Brillon, M. Diago (Hydro-Québec), C. Tribes · **Position in trajectory**: This is the companion usage paper to SOLAR (Optim. Eng. 2025, ref. [2], p. 12), written by the NOMAD team with a Hydro-Québec researcher (p. 2). It is cited by Micro-PRIAD (D006) as the template (D006 ref. [7], p. 31). Solver versions: NOMAD 4.5.0, HEXALY 14.0, PRIMA 0.7.3 (COBYLA), CMA-ES 4.2.0 (pp. 7–8). **Read**: all 13 pages, including the French abstract (p. 3); references skimmed. Figures 3–8 (field layouts, data profiles, h-profiles) are captions only in the text file, so profile shapes are not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap in benchmarking, not an algorithm. Real engineering blackboxes rely on proprietary or hard-to-interface codes, so "DFO solvers are too often benchmarked on problems that do not fully capture the challenges of the field" (p. 4). The CSP design community lacks standardized benchmarks (abstract, p. 3). Two stated objectives (pp. 4–5): (1) show how a CSP designer, or anyone with a blackbox, can use SOLAR to choose a solver; (2) give "the first large-scale comparison" of four solvers on SOLAR. The problem class is written generically: f, c: X → ℝ ∪ {∞}, with infinite values for failed evaluations (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: No new algorithm. The design object is the benchmarking procedure (pp. 7–9):
  - *Problem selection* keyed to the property the solver must handle (p. 7): crashes and narrow feasibility → solar3 at fidelity 0.5; biobjective → solar8/solar9; continuous-only → solar6 (constrained) or solar10 (unconstrained); Monte Carlo noise → solar1.
  - *Instances* = (problem, starting point) pairs, with optional seeds (p. 7).
  - *Starting points* from Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) (p. 7).
  - *Competitors* chosen because they handle inequality constraints, accept infeasible starts and assume no smoothness (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: publish a selection guide that maps a solver requirement to the benchmark instance that tests it (p. 7). This turns a suite into a decision tool for practitioners.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *The suite* (Table 1, p. 6). Ten problems with 5–29 variables, continuous and discrete (a categorical turbine choice, p. 6). Constraints are split into a priori ones (explicit, checked before the simulator runs; ref. Le Digabel–Wild) and simulated ones (p. 6). Replication and fidelity parameters are available for the stochastic problems (p. 7).
  - *Solver set-up* (p. 8). Budget 100·n_p evaluations. An artificial upper bound of 10⁵ where a variable has none, because HEXALY, PRIMA and CMA-ES require bounds. Known linear constraints are passed to HEXALY and PRIMA. Otherwise default parameters for all solvers.
  - *Not stated*: the replication and fidelity values and the seeds used in the runs, including CMA-ES's own randomness.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: No theory. The measurement devices are these:
  - *τ-solved test.* (f⁰ − f^N)/(f⁰ − f*) ≥ 1 − τ, with f* the best value found by any solver (p. 8).
  - *Profile axis.* Profiles are plotted against κ, the number of groups of n_p + 1 evaluations, so problems of different dimension share an axis (p. 8). The text recommends at least 20 instances per profile (p. 8).
  - *Infeasible starts.* The same profile is computed on the violation function h(x) = Σ max{c_j(x), 0}² (∞ outside X) instead of f, because f-based profiles are "not informative" when few instances are solved (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: when feasible points are rare, build data profiles on h, with h⁰ = h(x⁰) and h* the best h found by any solver (p. 11). This measures progress towards feasibility with the same machinery.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *LHS census.* 10⁴ LHS points per problem are classed as a priori infeasible, simulated infeasible or feasible (Table 2, p. 9). Feasible points: solar2 1, solar3 1, solar4 0 (9,990 a priori infeasible), solar7 1,354, solar10 all.
  - *Feasible-start instances* (pp. 9–10): solar10, 30 starts, τ = 10⁻², 10⁻⁴; solar7, 30 of the 1,354 feasible points, τ = 0.1, 10⁻²; solar2/3/4 together, 21 starts (2 from LHS plus 19 known to the authors), τ = 0.1, 10⁻³ (Fig. 7 caption, p. 11).
  - *Infeasible-start instances.* solar3 (20) and solar4 (10), all chosen to satisfy the a priori constraints so that the simulator runs (p. 10).
  - *Feasibility counts* (Table 3, p. 11). solar3: NOMAD 3/20, PRIMA 0, HEXALY 1, CMA-ES 5. solar4: NOMAD 4/10, the others 0. Reader's note: the text's "Only the NOMAD solver finds feasible points for both solar problems" (p. 10) is true, but on solar3 CMA-ES finds feasible points in more instances than NOMAD.
  - *Scope limit.* Tuned parameters and larger budgets are left out: "this exceeds the scope of the present work" (p. 11).
  - *Not reported*: profile curves in text (figures only), repeated runs, wall-clock time.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - *Against analytic suites.* SOLAR is set against "traditional optimization benchmarks, which often oversimplify engineering problems" (p. 4).
  - *Competitors described fairly.* Each is presented with the class it is best suited to: HEXALY "particularly efficient on smooth problems"; PRIMA "most effective on smooth problems"; CMA-ES for non-convex, noisy, ill-conditioned problems (p. 8).
  - *Citations.* Benchmarking methodology is cited to Moré–Wild, Beiranvand–Hare–Lucet and Audet–Hare–Tribes (pp. 8–9). The remaining citations are Audet-group papers plus the Le Digabel–Wild constraint taxonomy (pp. 12–13).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Teaching the method.* Fig. 4 aligns a data profile above the convergence graphs of three instances, marking where each bump comes from (p. 9). The profile is taught through the instances it summarizes.
  - *Hedging is uneven.* Results are hedged ("The figure suggests that NOMAD outperforms", p. 11), but the Discussion is strong: the mesh-based method "reliably performs better", and SOLAR is "the first freely available and open source suite of benchmarking problems specifically designed for BBO" (pp. 11–12).
  - *An interpretive leap.* NOMAD's win is read as "a sign that solar successfully captures the difficulties encountered with real BBO problems" (p. 12). A suite built by the NOMAD team is validated by NOMAD winning on it.
  - Reader's note: the Discussion calls CMA-ES "model-based" (p. 11), although §3 describes it as an evolutionary sampler (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a benchmarking methodology plus a comparison, i.e., a usage layer on a public artifact. Three claims go untested here:
  - *Smooth-problem claim.* That model-based solvers "tend to perform better on smooth problems" (p. 11); no smooth analytic set is included.
  - *Diversity of the suite.* It is argued from the fact that each model-based solver wins at least one high-precision profile (p. 12).
  - *Future work* is a wish: "We hope that this contribution will encourage the use of realistic benchmark problems" (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → public benchmark artifacts) — evidence ✅. The paper is the use-case layer of an artifact: public repo and version (v1.0, p. 7), a selection guide (p. 7), LHS starting points, seeds, and replication and fidelity parameters (pp. 7, 9).
- Method 5 (Budget-aware, cross-community benchmarking) — evidence ✅ and variant ⚠. Evidence: an evaluation currency (100·n_p budget; κ in groups of n_p + 1), data profiles, four solver families including a commercial one, defaults, and h-profiles for infeasible starts (pp. 8, 11). Variant: the competitors' home ground (smooth problems) is described but not tested (p. 11), and seeds and replications are not reported.
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first) — evidence ✅. The a priori vs simulated split is used both in the census (Table 2, p. 9) and in choosing infeasible starts that let the simulator run (p. 10). Linear a priori constraints are passed to solvers that can exploit them (p. 8).

- new-pattern candidate: **Cheap-first evaluation gating** (name shared with an earlier batch). A priori constraints are checked by the benchmark code, and the simulation is skipped when one fails (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before benchmarking, run an LHS census of the domain and publish the three-way feasibility table (p. 9).
- For infeasible-start instances, draw starts that satisfy the cheap constraints so the benchmark exercises the simulator, not the bound handling (p. 10).
- Use h-based data profiles when f-based ones are uninformative (p. 11).
- Pair a data profile with the per-instance convergence plots behind it to teach readers how to read it (Fig. 4, p. 9).

### S177 · Optimal sorting with comparisons involving three elements (Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2025–54, 2025; no DOI)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (solo) · **Position in trajectory**: This is a solo working paper (August 2025), "Dedicated to the memory of Pierre Hansen" (p. 2), Audet's former GERAD colleague. It generalizes Audet's 2014 *Mathematical Gazette* paper on ordering 15 marbles with a three-tray balance (ref. [1], p. 13; recalled on p. 4) from n = 15 to all n. It is a recreational or combinatorial side-line with no link to DFO. **Read**: all 13 pages. Text-file caveats: the base case of α_n reads "1 if n ≤ 32" (p. 6). The table (α_4 = 2 with a = 1, p. 12) implies the base case is n ≤ 3, so the "2" is an extraction artifact. The proof figures (Figs. 1–9) are partly lost.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Pairwise sorting has a known lower bound ⌈log₂ n!⌉, which is attained by Ford–Johnson for small n, and the optimum is open for n ∈ {16, 17, 18} and n ≥ 23 (p. 4). The paper changes the oracle: a comparison of three elements returns their full order, i.e., six outcomes (p. 4). The information-theoretic bound becomes ℓ_n = ⌈log₆ n!⌉, but it "only appears to be tight for the trivial cases where n = 3" (p. 4). The goal is to minimize the worst-case number of comparisons (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: take a classical problem with a well-studied oracle, change only the oracle (2 outcomes → 6), and restate the trivial lower bound for the new oracle as the first yardstick (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The algorithm is a portfolio recurrence: t_n = min over six heuristics, each using t_1, …, t_{n−1} (p. 5).
  - s_n, single insertion: sort n − 1 elements, then insert one using a five-group split around two pivots (α_n recursion, p. 6).
  - p_n, pair insertion: sort n − 2, then insert a pair (β_n = 1 + 2α_{a+1}) (pp. 6–7).
  - f_n, "4chains": sort four groups, then merge in n − 1 comparisons (Thm 2.2(ii); Alg. 3.1, p. 7).
  - v_n, "1v-2chains" (Thm 2.2(i); Alg. 3.2, p. 7).
  - m_n, "1mono-3chains" (Thm 2.2(iii); Alg. 3.3, p. 8).
  - g_n, special constructions for n = 7, 9, 13, 15, 21 (p. 8).
  Each chain-based heuristic enumerates all group-size partitions and adds the merge cost from a lemma (pp. 7–8).
  - Transferable technique: when you have several structural lemmas, each saying that some configuration can be finished in a known number of steps, turn each one into a heuristic by enumerating the partitions that lead to C. Then take the pointwise minimum and compute bottom-up (dynamic programming over n) (pp. 5–8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The elements are distinct reals, and each comparison returns the full order of its three elements (p. 4). A "chain" is a totally ordered set, and a "v-chain" is two chains sharing the least element; there is a symmetric "reversed v-chain" (pp. 4–5). No probabilistic model: the analysis is worst-case only (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**:
  - *Theorem 2.2* (pp. 4–5): (i) two chains plus a v-chain are sorted in n − 2 comparisons; (ii) four chains in n − 1; (iii) four chains with one singleton in n − 2. Part (i) is proved by induction: compare the three least elements, merge the two chains into a v-chain, remove the overall minimum. Parts (ii) and (iii) reduce to (i) by a first one or two comparisons, with a case split in (iii) on where the singleton a falls (p. 5). Reader's check: in the second case of (iii), "remaining n − 3 elements" presupposes that min(s, m) is also removed as the known third-smallest element; the text leaves this implicit (p. 5).
  - *Propositions 3.1 and 3.2*: 13 elements are sorted in 16 comparisons (pp. 8–9) and 21 elements in 34 (pp. 9–11). Both are proved by an explicit adversary-style case analysis: build chains, find a pivot, split by the number of elements on each side of the pivot (4 cases each), invoke Theorem 2.2 in each case, and use symmetry to halve the cases (pp. 8–11).
  - Reader's note: the last sentence of Prop. 3.1's proof says 16 comparisons suffice to sort 14 elements (p. 9); the proposition is about 13, so this is a typo.
  - *Lower bounds*: only ⌈log₆ n!⌉ (p. 4). No lower-bound argument beyond counting.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The computations are Table 1 (p. 12): α_n, β_n, the six heuristic values, ℓ_n and t_n for n ≤ 40 and n ∈ {50, 100, 200, 500, 1000}. Bold marks best values and boxes mark unique bests (p. 11). Three accounting rows close the table (p. 12):
  - best occurrences for n ≤ 40: s 9, p 5, f 21, v 11, m 3, g 5;
  - unique best: 3, 2, 10, 1, 2, 5;
  - the largest n ≤ 1000 at which each heuristic is best: 243, 19, 1000, 83, 10, 21.
  For 23 ≤ n ≤ 1000, f_n is always best (p. 11). Reader's arithmetic from Table 1: t_n − ℓ_n grows from 1 (n = 4) to 20 (n = 40) and 1053 (n = 1000). The ratio t_n/ℓ_n stays about 1.32–1.34 for n = 40…1000 (p. 12).
  - Transferable technique: when a result is the best of k methods, add accounting rows (times best, times uniquely best, largest instance where best). They show which methods are redundant and which matter asymptotically (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: The literature is the pairwise minimum-comparison line: Ford–Johnson, Peczarski's series, Cheng et al. on s(15) and s(19) (p. 4). The three-way setting is claimed as new: "a new class of sorting problems" (p. 12). The only three-way predecessor is the author's own 2014 paper (p. 4). No competing three-way algorithm is cited.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Claim strength.* The abstract says "optimal or near-optimal results for all values up to n = 40" (p. 3). The Discussion is more careful: "many of the reported values of tn are likely not optimal", and optimality is "challenging even for relatively small n" (pp. 12–13). The strength of the claim differs between abstract and discussion.
  - *Form.* The paper is short and proof-driven; each heuristic gets a name, a symbol and a pseudo-code block (pp. 6–8). The dedication plays on Hansen as "the world’s finest octagonist" (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory and computation: constructive upper bounds t_n for n ≤ 1000 and two hand-built certificates (n = 13, 21). The limits are concrete (pp. 12–13):
  - only n = 6…15 were studied in detail;
  - enumeration is practical up to n = 8 and impractical from n = 9 on;
  - optimality of t_n is unconfirmed.
  Future work: confirm or disprove the optimality of the t_n; generalize to comparisons that rank p elements at once (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder, bounded by counterexamples) — variant ⚠. Each upper bound comes with a constructive proof (Thm 2.2, Props 3.1–3.2, pp. 4–11), and the Discussion states plainly that optimality is unproven (pp. 12–13). But there is no tightness evidence beyond n = 3, and the abstract's "optimal or near-optimal" (p. 3) is stronger than anything proved. This is the counterpart of Method 3's demand to show that the hypotheses are needed: here the matching lower bound is missing and is named as future work.
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll) — no link. The portfolio-of-heuristics idea (min over six constructions) is a design move, but it carries no convergence-type guarantee.
- new-pattern candidate: **min-over-constructions recurrence**. Several lemma-backed constructions, each enumerated over partitions, combined by a pointwise minimum computed bottom-up, with accounting rows for which construction wins (pp. 5–8, 12).
- new-pattern candidate: **puzzle-to-family generalization**. A single recreational instance (15 marbles, 2014) is generalized a decade later into a parametric family with a table up to n = 1000 and an open generalization to p-way comparisons (pp. 4, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- State the information-theoretic lower bound for your oracle first and print it as a column next to your upper bounds (p. 12).
- Prove a small set of *finishing lemmas* for partial orders (v-chains, chains with a singleton) and reuse them as the closing step of every case in larger hand-built certificates (pp. 8–11).
- Use symmetry (assume at least as many elements above the pivot as below it) to halve the case analysis (pp. 8–9).

**Quotes**:
- "We report optimal or near-optimal results for all values up to n = 40 elements, and for selected instances up to n = 1000." (p. 3)
- "Larger instances have not been studied as thoroughly, and many of the reported values of tn are likely not optimal." (p. 12)

### D006 · Micro-PRIAD: A power utility simulator for blackbox optimization benchmarking (Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2026–45, 2026; no DOI)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: F. Gentile, X. Lebeuf, S. Le Digabel, M. Diago (Hydro-Québec) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the second Hydro-Québec benchmark after SOLAR (August 2026, pp. 1–2). It is built to exercise the group's own algorithms that use intermediate outputs, Inter-DS and multi-fidelity constraints (refs. [1], [2], p. 5). The data profiles use the group's RunnerPost tool (p. 21). Code: github.com/bbopt/Micro-PRIAD (p. 6). **Read**: all 33 pages, including the appendix (RTM table and the full user guide, pp. 23–31); references skimmed. Figures 3, 5, 8–10 are captions or axis labels only (pp. 10, 14, 19–21).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is a benchmark gap. Realistic BBO problems are expensive, restricted, hard to install or proprietary. The largest DFO comparisons (Rios–Sahinidis, Ploskas–Sahinidis) use analytical problems only (pp. 4–5). The existing realistic problems (STYRENE, Browne et al., the simplified wing, groundwater, hydraulic capture) offer "only a single blackbox instance" (p. 5). SOLAR fills both gaps, but for CSP (p. 5). The trigger is Hydro-Québec's PRIAD asset-management blackbox, which needs up to 145 days per evaluation on one CPU (p. 4). The problem is stated as (P): min f(x) subject to c_i(x) ≤ 0, with mixed integer/real bounded x and f, c ∈ ℝ ∪ {∞}, all constraints "quantifiable and relaxable" and all outputs stochastic (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: when an industrial blackbox is too expensive to benchmark on, build a scaled-down public twin that keeps its structural features (stochastic blocks, intermediate outputs, sub-sampling) and drops its cost driver (here the power-flow simulation, pp. 4, 6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The design object is the benchmark and its interface.
  - *Pipeline* (Fig. 1, pp. 6–7): degradation model → power-grid generation → unavailability simulator, a Monte Carlo loop of η groups of s draws → electrical cost computation, with N = η·s draws in all.
  - *Nine realistic instances* (pp. 8–10, Table 1): three grids (urban u, suburban s, rural r) × three input sizes. n = 28 is the complete strategy. n = 15 is partial: each relative periodicity is fixed at 2, which makes the problem continuous. n = 13 is within-city reach only.
  - *Tenth instance.* A simplified unconstrained one, "t", with n = 4, η = 1, m = 0 (p. 16).
  - *Encoding of variables.* One absolute periodicity per equipment type (real) plus integer relative multipliers for further degradation mechanisms (pp. 9–10).
  - *Solver levers* (§3, pp. 16–18). Three interrupters (power grid, availability, energy) receive intermediate outputs and can stop the evaluation. A user-supplied subsampler passes ŝ < s draws to the cost block.
  - Transferable technique: expose the blackbox's internal stages as hooks the solver can call (interrupt, subsample, fidelity). The benchmark then supports research on algorithms that exploit partial evaluations, not only on algorithms that consume final outputs (pp. 16–18).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Randomness* (p. 11). Failures follow a geometric law Geo(p) with p from analytic degradation curves; failure times are uniform within the year; repair and maintenance durations are lognormal with variance 0.25 and instance-specific means. Reader's note: as extracted, σ² = ln(V[T]/E²[T]), without the usual "1 +" inside the logarithm; this may be an extraction loss.
  - *Seed.* The seed determines every stochastic output (p. 12). Default seed 0 makes outputs repeatable; seed −1 draws from OS entropy (pp. 13, 25).
  - *Simplifications, stated as such.* Analytic degradation curves replace the historical-data model (p. 10). Prices are static (Table 2, p. 12). Power flow is removed (p. 6).
  - *Constraints* (Table 4, p. 15). They are classified by the Le Digabel–Wild taxonomy: c1–c2 QRAK (a priori analytical), c3–c5 deterministic QRSK, c6–c9 stochastic QRSK. All are rescaled to a common lower bound −500 and threshold 0, "this similarity helps for the utilization of the constraints violation function" (p. 15).
  - *Bounds* (p. 15). Integers in [1, 9] and reals in [0.1, 10]. Reader's note: the simplified instance uses a lower bound 0.0 on periodicities (Table 5, p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: No theorems. The quantitative devices are these:
  - *Horn plot for the default N* (pp. 13–14). At the best-known x of each instance, f is evaluated 100 times for each N. Assuming normality, the smallest N with 3σ_f/f̄ ≤ 0.1% is taken; N = 10⁴ meets it for all nine instances. N = 10³ and 10² give 0.5% and 1%.
  - *A second vector with each intermediate output.* It gives the number of draws N_i behind each component. f, c8 and c9 carry N_{i−1} at the availability interrupter because the cost block has not yet run in that iteration (p. 18).
  - *Linear fidelity map.* A fidelity increment of 10⁻⁴ adds one draw (p. 29).
  - Transferable technique: choose the default Monte Carlo size from repeated evaluations at the best-known point, with an explicit relative-precision threshold, and publish the lower-N alternatives with their precision. Users can then trade time for accuracy knowingly (p. 13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Hardware: an i9-14900K workstation with 128 GB (p. 19).
  - *§4.1, landscape illustrations.*
    - 2D slices on 200 × 200 grids at N = 500, each taking more than 10 hours. They show a large feasible region (r, n = 15) and a small one (u, n = 13, called the hardest instance for feasibility) (p. 19).
    - The same slice at N = 1, 10, 100 and 10⁴; the N = 10⁴ plot "required a week". Time scales roughly linearly in N (p. 20).
    - 1D cuts showing that at low N points switch between feasible and infeasible, and that f at N = 1 lies above or below f at N = 10⁴ depending on the seed (p. 20).
  - *§4.2, benchmarking use.*
    - Setup: 32 problem instances (start point, seed, instance), five anonymized solvers, a 32-hour budget each, τ = 0.005, and N = 10⁴ (pp. 20–21).
    - Profiles are drawn against both groups of n + 1 evaluations and hours. Solvers τ-solve 3–50% of instances (p. 21).
    - Rankings shift between the two axes. Algo B looks better and Algo D worse on the time axis, because B sampled fast-to-evaluate regions and D slow ones (pp. 21–22). Reader's note: one sentence names "Algo A" where the surrounding text discusses Algo B (p. 22).
    - The same solver runs with and without a static surrogate (f at N = 10³), counting 10 surrogate calls as one evaluation; the surrogate version solves "significantly" more instances (p. 22).
  - *Not reported*: solver identities, which instances make up the 32, starting points, and the best-known values.
  - Transferable technique: draw every data profile twice, once against the evaluation count and once against wall-clock time, and explain any ranking change by where each solver sampled (pp. 21–22).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself against analytic suites (CUTEst, the BBOB functions), which "severely lack the inherent difficulties" (p. 5), and against single-instance realistic problems (p. 5). SOLAR is credited as the one collection that addresses both gaps (p. 5). The claimed novelty is narrow and hedged: "to the author’s knowledge, no BBO benchmarking problem currently offers these last two features" (intermediate outputs and sub-sampling, p. 5). Hydro-Québec internal sources describe PRIAD itself (refs. [12]–[14], [18], [21], p. 4).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Structure.* Contributions are "twofold" in §1.3: a collection, and grid-specific features (p. 5).
  - *Honest about fidelity.* The paper says where the mini-simulator does not reproduce the real one: sub-sampling gains are "negligible" in Micro-PRIAD and exist only "to mimic" PRIAD (p. 18).
  - *A report doubling as a user manual.* About a third of it is the manual: three call modes, parameters, output line format and example files (pp. 23–31).
  - *An inference overreaches.* The static-surrogate experiment is taken to suggest "that the use of intermediate outputs is advantageous" (p. 22), but interruption itself was not tested.
  - *Inconsistencies in the text.* §3.1 says intermediate outputs are disabled by default (p. 17), while the guide gives availInterrupter default true (p. 26). The guide announces four implemented interrupter functions but describes three (p. 26). The example ARGS file names "printInterReturnConinueEval" (p. 31), not the documented function. The simplified instance is said to be at the end of §2.5, but it is in §2.6 (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a public benchmark artifact: ten instances, stochastic, with solver levers, a user guide and code. The limits are stated as model simplifications, not in a limitations section: no power flow (p. 6), analytic degradation (p. 10), static prices (p. 12), negligible sub-sampling gain (p. 18). The Discussion restates the goal and names no future work (p. 22). The reported results are "illustrative" (abstract, p. 3), and the solvers are anonymized (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (Real blackboxes → public benchmark artifacts) — evidence ✅ (strong). It follows the release contract step by step (pp. 13, 23–31): executable with an x.txt input and a one-line output (countEvalFlag, f, c1…c9); constraint classification; seeds; a fidelity parameter; several instances; open code. - Method 6 (The solver as research instrument) — evidence ✅. The benchmark is co-designed with the group's algorithm line (Inter-DS and multi-fidelity constraints use intermediate outputs, p. 5) and uses the group's RunnerPost (p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: **scaled-down public twin of a proprietary blackbox**. Keep the structure and pathologies, drop the cost driver, and say which features are kept only "to mimic" (pp. 4, 18, 22).
- new-pattern candidate: **Cheap-first evaluation gating** (name shared with an earlier batch). countEvalFlag = 0 when the a priori constraints fail (p. 30); an N = 0 mode (p. 13); the power-grid interrupter after c1–c5 (p. 17).
