# Paper cards · batch s2-03 · Charles Audet · 2009–2013, MADS inside other people's pipelines, plus three exact side lines (supplement)

**Batch intent**: The eight entries fall into two groups. Three use MADS/NOMAD as the engine of someone else's pipeline: a stochastic surrogate framework with Marsden's cardiovascular/UQ group (S039), a parameter-tuning layer around the DFO trust-region code (Opal, S057), and a monitor-placement blackbox from Hydro-Québec (S074). The other five sit outside DFO: two extremal-polygon pieces with Hansen and Messine (S104, S146), one game-theory paper on proper Nash equilibria that is listed twice (S067 = D002), and one signal-processing paper that designs orthonormal bases by optimization (S118). Three questions:
- In the MADS-based papers, where does the new idea sit relative to the Search/Poll backbone, and is the guarantee kept or openly given up (Method 1, Method 3)?
- How do these wrapper and application papers choose a constraint treatment and an evaluation currency (Methods 2, 5)?
- Outside DFO, how is a numerical observation turned into a theorem or a certificate?

**Focus dimensions**: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line), D8. These are supplement short cards. The emphasis is on D2 (placement of the idea relative to the MADS backbone) and D4 (the proof device).

### S104 · Ranking small regular polygons by area and by perimeter (Journal of Applied and Industrial Mathematics 2009, 10.1134/s1990478909010037; text read: Russian original, Diskretnyi Analiz i Issledovanie Operatsii 15(3), 2008)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen, F. Messine · **Position in trajectory**: This belongs to the extremal-small-polygon side line (Research Trajectory, 2002–2013). The paper cites the group's earlier numeric results: the largest small octagon, the octagon with longest perimeter, and the 2007 JOGO survey (refs 2–4, p. 8). Received 10 October 2007, revised 3 March 2008 (p. 9). Audet's funding line names NSERC, AFOSR and ExxonMobil (p. 1). **Read**: all 9 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Three classical questions are stated: max area for a fixed perimeter (P1), max area for a fixed diameter (P2), and max perimeter for a fixed diameter (P3) (p. 1). For P2 and P3, regular n-gons are optimal for odd n (Reinhardt 1922) but not for even n. The known even-n optima come from geometry plus global optimization (octagons +2.79% area, +1.96% perimeter) or from Mossinghoff's constructions (pp. 1–2). The entry is a **figure**: area against perimeter for regular and for known optimal small polygons, n ≥ 5 (p. 3). By the authors' account, the figure intuitively leads to two observations: from pentagons on, the regular odd n-gon has more area than the regular (n+1)-gon, and from heptagons on the successive differences shrink (p. 4). The stated aim is to prove these, their perimeter analogues, and hence to give a new, very simple proof of Reinhardt's theorem (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Work only with closed forms for regular small polygons: A_n = (n/8) sin(2π/n) for even n and (n/8) sin(2π/n) cos²(π/2n) for odd n (p. 4); P_n = n sin(π/n) or 2n sin(π/2n) (p. 7). The results are:
  - Theorem 1: A_{2k−1} > A_{2k} for k ≥ 3 (p. 5).
  - Theorem 2: A_{2k−1} − A_{2k} > A_{2k+1} − A_{2k+2} for k ≥ 4 (p. 6).
  - Proposition 4: P_{2k−1} > P_{2k}, read off directly from the identity P_k = P_{2k} for odd k (p. 7).
  - Theorem 3: perimeter differences decrease for k ≥ 3 (p. 7).
  - Theorem 4 (Reinhardt) follows: add one vertex, within the diameter bound, to the regular odd (n−1)-gon and take the convex hull. It beats A_{n−1}, which beats A_n by Theorem 1 (p. 8). The authors contrast this with Reinhardt and later authors, who add a vertex to the even regular polygon, and with Mossinghoff, who starts from the (n−1)-gon by a more laborious route (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Convex polygons of unit diameter ("small", following Graham) that are regular (equal sides, equal angles) (p. 4). No other assumption is needed, because everything is explicit.
- **D4 Proof device (brief)**: This is a *Taylor sandwich → polynomial certificate*, in four steps:
  1. Extend the area functions to real x (eq. 1).
  2. Bound sin and cos² between Taylor polynomials, s_ℓ < sin < s_u and c_ℓ < cos² < c_u (eq. 2). This gives strict lower and upper bounds on A^e and A^o (eq. 3).
  3. Multiply f_ℓ(x) = A^o_ℓ(x) − A^e_u(x+1) by a positive factor μ(x) to get a degree-6 polynomial. Maple puts its largest real root at ≈ 5.6029 < 6, so the claim holds for x ≥ 6 (pp. 5–6).
  4. Settle the small cases from a table of exact values for n = 3…14 (p. 4, p. 5).
  Theorem 2 uses a degree-11 polynomial (largest root ≈ 11.0747 < 14, p. 6). Theorem 3 uses a degree-9 polynomial (only real root ≈ 3.3876 < 4, p. 8). The text does not discuss how the root locations were certified beyond the use of Maple (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The only numbers are the table of A_n and P_n (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A theoretical note: two monotonicity theorems for areas, one for perimeters, and a short new proof of a classical result (pp. 4–8). There is no conclusion section, no stated limitation and no future work (pp. 8–9).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (outside DFO): the same taste for results stated exactly relative to a threshold (k ≥ 3, k ≥ 4), with small cases checked explicitly (pp. 5–6). There is no ladder and no counterexample.
- new-pattern candidate (evidence, second occurrence): **extremal-geometry side line: numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later** (s03). Here the numerically found optima feed the plot (p. 3), and the paper's output is a purely analytic result (pp. 5–8).
- new-pattern candidate: **plot the known optima, prove what the plot shows**. A figure of the solved instances against a baseline family supplies the conjectures that become the theorems (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To compare terms of a sequence given by trigonometric closed forms, extend it to real x, sandwich each trigonometric factor between Taylor polynomials, clear positive denominators to get one polynomial, show its largest real root lies below a threshold, and close the finitely many remaining cases from a table (pp. 5–6, 8).
- (D2) Get the classical theorem as a two-line corollary of your new inequality. Here that is done by adding one vertex to the neighbouring odd regular polygon, rather than by a separate construction (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Этот рисунок интуитивно приводит к следующим наблюдениям" (p. 4) [This figure intuitively leads to the following observations]
- "Цель статьи — доказать эти свойства вместе с аналогичными для периметров." (p. 4) [The aim of the paper is to prove these properties together with the analogous ones for perimeters.]

### S039 · A method for stochastic constrained optimization using derivative-free surrogate pattern search and collocation (Journal of Computational Physics 2010, 10.1016/j.jcp.2010.03.005)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Sankaran (first author), A.L. Marsden (corresponding author), both at UC San Diego (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Audet is the second author on an application-side extension of the Surrogate Management Framework (SMF) of Booker et al. The acknowledgments thank J.E. Dennis Jr. (p. 18), and the funding is a Burroughs Wellcome Fund award (p. 18). Received February 2009, revised March 2010 (p. 1). The paper is cited as a MADS application in S074 (ref. 40, p. 25). **Read**: all 19 pages; references skimmed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: PDE-based design where one cost evaluation may take "hours or even several days", and where parameters, and possibly the design variables themselves, are uncertain (p. 1, p. 3). Gradients are ruled out one by one: noisy mesh derivatives, adjoints' storage cost in unsteady flows, finite differences, automatic differentiation, complex step (p. 2). Monte Carlo UQ is dismissed as too slow (p. 2). The gap stated is that stochastic gradient-based tools exist, but "a large class" of expensive problems remains for which they are impractical (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Couple three existing pieces without changing any of them (p. 2): SMF (Kriging surrogate in the SEARCH), MADS (POLL directions), and sparse-grid stochastic collocation (Smolyak) for the random dimensions.
  - The collocation layer is packed into a **stochastic black box**: a wrapper that takes an input PDF, runs the deterministic solver at collocation points, and returns moments or statistics (Fig. 2, p. 6).
  - The SEARCH/POLL loop is the deterministic one. Only step (b), the evaluation, changes (p. 6).
  - Stochastic design variables get one mesh per collocation point (p. 6).
  - A hierarchical variant raises the depth of interpolation d level by level. Each level stops when the solution repeats, and the next level restarts from the previous filter points (p. 7).
  - Constraints: a filter on the violation H is used, although EB and PB are described (p. 5). The code is MATLAB with spinterp and DACE (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions**: For convergence of the interpolated cost, J ∈ C⁰ for piecewise-linear interpolants or J ∈ C^{Ny+Nz} for Lagrange interpolants (Theorem 5.1, p. 8). The GPS part assumes r(ξ_i) ∈ C¹ "as was assumed by Torczon", on a neighbourhood of a compact level set (p. 9). Probabilistic constraints are written as ∫ I(σ − σ_c) p dξ ≤ p_f and aggregated as H = Σ I(c_i)|c_i|. "strong constraint conditions" require H = 0 (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: 
  - Theorem 5.1 splits the interpolation error by the triangle inequality into a sparse-grid term (rate from [24]) and a Kriging term (pp. 8–9).
  - Theorems 5.3 and 5.5 apply Torczon's Theorems 3.5/3.6 at each collocation point, then recombine through the non-negative quadrature weights (eq. 12): ‖∇r^m‖ ≤ Σ w_i ‖∇r^m(ξ_i)‖ (pp. 9–10). Variance ≥ 0 then ties the first and second moments together (eq. 13, p. 9). As written, the proof treats the lim inf as attained at an iterate k′ (p. 9).
  - The MADS/Clarke extension is only sketched: "we outline a proof for stochastic MADS polling" (p. 11).
  - For the constrained case the authors state that "no convergence proof has been published yet for the MADS technique with the filter approach". They justify the filter by the filter plots (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: Four problems, one run each, with total simulator calls against a Monte Carlo baseline:
  - Beam with two chance constraints and 4 random inputs: level 3 reaches cost 8.03 in 8,905 calls; MC reaches 8.01 in 8.4M calls (Table 2, p. 13).
  - Lognormal beam: no feasible point at levels 2/4/6; level 10 needs 1.86M calls against 25M for MC (Table 3, p. 15). The level-10 point "violated the constraint by less than 0.3%" (p. 13), yet it is listed under "Best feas. pt." (p. 15).
  - Inverse heat PDF, uni- and bimodal (pp. 15–16), and porous-media reconstruction with the CTRW toolbox as blackbox (pp. 16–18).
  - The authors warn that the evaluation counts "are very large" for practice (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Claimed gains over MC are "15 to 20" with strict probabilistic constraints, and more than two orders of magnitude for unconstrained smooth PDFs (p. 18). The number of design variables is limited (p. 18). The collocation depth needed depends on the PDF, and a bimodal flux needs level ≈ 8 (p. 16). Future work: improve the POLL with OrthoMADS, move to the progressive barrier, and adapt collocation points (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the surrogate lives in the SEARCH, and the POLL "provides the convergence framework". The LHS initial design "does not affect the convergence properties" (p. 5).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the ladder is imported (Torczon's GPS theorems, then Audet–Dennis Clarke results) rather than built. The constrained filter-MADS case is run with an openly acknowledged missing proof (p. 11). Research Taste mark 3 (*practical freedom never costs the theory*) ✗ contradiction for the constrained runs: the filter is chosen on empirical grounds (p. 11), and PB, which has theory, is deferred to future work (p. 18).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: chance constraints are quantifiable and relaxable, but they are aggregated into one H and handled by a filter rather than PB (pp. 5, 12).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the currency is simulator calls, the right one, but the only baseline is MC UQ under the same optimizer. There are no replications and no competitor from another optimizer family (Tables 2–3, pp. 13, 15).
- new-pattern candidate: **non-intrusive stochastic wrapper**. Put the UQ layer inside the blackbox so that the deterministic optimizer and its analysis are reused unchanged (Fig. 2, pp. 6–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Wrap uncertainty inside the blackbox: return moments or chance-constraint values computed on a quadrature grid, so that any deterministic DFO solver applies as is (pp. 6–7).
- (D2) Run a coarse-to-fine continuation over a fidelity parameter. Stop a level when its solution repeats, and seed the next level with the previous filter points (p. 7).
- (D5) Report, per fidelity level, the number of collocation points, the best point, the cost and the total simulator calls, next to a Monte Carlo row that reaches the same design (Tables 2–3, pp. 13, 15).

**Quotes**:
- "The POLL step consists of a local exploration in the space of d.v.s, and provides the convergence framework for the algorithm." (p. 5)
- "However, no convergence proof has been published yet for the MADS technique with the filter approach. An attempt for such a proof is beyond the scope of this article." (p. 11)

### S057 · Algorithmic parameter optimization of the DFO method with the OPAL framework (Software automatic tuning: from concepts to state-of-the-art results, Springer 2010, 10.1007/978-1-4419-6935-4_15)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: C.-K. Dang, D. Orban · **Position in trajectory**: This is the first Opal paper. It implements and extends Audet & Orban (SIAM J. Optim. 2006) (p. 3, p. 23). It is the precursor of the 2014 MPC Opal paper (S059, carded in s01). **Read**: all 23 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Parameter tuning of computational tasks. Performance is nonsmooth, often not analytic, and "may not even be a function" because CPU time varies between runs (p. 2). Users keep the defaults, and codes have 5–20 parameters (p. 3). Two questions define a tuning problem: which parameter values are acceptable, and in what sense one setting is better (p. 3). The related work named is calibra (at most five parameters, no guarantee), ParamILS, ATLAS (coordinate search), PHiPAC and OSKI (pp. 4–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Model tuning as a blackbox: minimize ψ(p) subject to p ∈ P and φ(p) ∈ M. ψ and φ are **compound measures** built from **atomic measures** read from the solver's output (eq. 4.2, p. 7). This extends the 2006 formulation by allowing constraints on measures (p. 7). The environment is Python and non-intrusive: model structure plus model data, and a solver component whose only instance is NOMAD (pp. 9–10). An 18-line listing tunes DFO on small unconstrained HS problems (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Real parameters only; integer and categorical ones are future work (p. 9). The case study uses DFO (Conn et al.), with 5 real parameters p = (delmin, stpthr, cnstol, delta, pp) under the bounds of the user's manual. The default pp = 1000 is taken from the example driver (pp. 14–15). The two formulations (4.3) and (4.4) minimize the average normalized evaluation change φ_EVAL, subject to φ_SOLVED = 1 and φ_QUALITY ≤ 0 (pp. 15–17). They fix stpthr (and cnstol in 4.4), because a stopping tolerance would be gamed, and they fix pp on unconstrained problems, where it has no effect (p. 17). How NOMAD treats these constraints (EB or PB) is not stated. §4.2.2 mentions both rejection and PB (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: None new. The MADS ladder is recalled: lim inf Δ_k = 0 for bounded iterates; nonnegative Clarke derivatives for Lipschitz ψ; KKT for strictly differentiable ψ on a regular domain (pp. 8–9).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: 82 unconstrained CUTEr problems and 125 HS problems (pp. 14, 16). The authors note that HS26 is misclassified in Conn et al. (p. 14). Training uses 50/25/10% subsets, taking every 2nd/4th/10th problem alphabetically, with cross-validation on the rest (p. 18). NOMAD runs with defaults, a 500-evaluation cap and diagonal scaling, using 302–474 blackbox evaluations (Table 4.4, p. 18). The results are reported as tables plus performance profiles in evaluations (pp. 19–22):
  - Unconstrained, 10–90 split: the tuned setting is strictly better on only 15% of the problems and the default on 40% (p. 21).
  - Constrained: more than 70% of the problems strictly improve, with pp lowered by three orders of magnitude (pp. 19, 22).
  - One run per setting.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Software plus a modelling layer (p. 23). The study "confirms" that DFO's unconstrained defaults are well chosen, and finds better constrained settings (p. 23). The authors warn that a small or ill-chosen training set may not generalize (p. 23), and that 300–500 blackbox evaluations "may be expensive" (p. 23). Future work: better initial guesses, integer and categorical parameters, surrogates, and robust parameters, which remain "an open question" (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Research Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence: the algorithm itself is treated as a blackbox tuned by MADS, "in contrast with heuristic methods" (pp. 3, 8).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: Opal is a modelling front end over NOMAD, with an object-oriented extension point for other solvers (p. 10).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: evaluation-based profiles on held-out problems (pp. 20–22). There are no competing tuners (calibra and ParamILS are only discussed, p. 4) and no replications.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (by absence): φ_SOLVED = 1 is effectively unrelaxable and φ_QUALITY ≤ 0 is quantifiable, but no EB/PB choice is reported (pp. 8, 17).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence, second occurrence): *train/held-out split for tuned algorithmic parameters* (s02). The split here is deterministic, taking every k-th problem alphabetically (p. 18).
- new-pattern candidate (evidence): **audit the literature instances**. The paper notes that HS26 is misclassified in the DFO paper's test set (p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate: **freeze gameable parameters**. Fix any parameter the objective could win by trivially, such as a stopping tolerance when minimizing evaluations (p. 17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3) Before tuning, list the parameters the objective can game (stopping and feasibility tolerances when you minimize work) and those with no effect on the instance class (penalty parameters on unconstrained problems), and fix both (p. 17).
- (D2) Use a bounded, symmetric relative change (a − b)/(a + b) per problem, averaged over problems solved by both settings, both as the objective (evaluations) and as a no-worse constraint (solution quality) (pp. 15–17).
- (D5) Tune on every k-th problem and report on the rest with evaluation-based performance profiles, including a profile of the per-problem ratios themselves (Fig. 4.2(d), pp. 18–21).

**Quotes**:
- "Besides only requiring the evaluation of ψ at a number of different values of p, a well-chosen direct method will offer certain optimality guarantees upon termination, which is in contrast with heuristic methods." (p. 3)
- "Allowing it to vary while minimizing the number of evaluations would not make sense, since the optimizer would simply increase its value." (p. 17)

### S146 · Optimization problems in planar geometry (Global Optimization Workshop (TOGO) 2010, pp. 3–6; no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the extended abstract of a talk surveying the extremal-polygon side line. Most of the cited solutions are Audet–Hansen–Messine papers, 2002–2010 (refs 1–9, p. 3). NSERC grant (p. 1). **Read**: all 4 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For a convex n-gon, five attributes are considered: area A, perimeter P, diameter D, sum of vertex distances S, and width W. Fixing one and optimizing another gives "ten pairs of extremal problems" (p. 1). The entry is a survey. The geometric approach, "some since the Greeks", has been complemented by global optimization methods in the last four decades (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: A **status grid**:
  - Minimizing X for fixed Y is equivalent to maximizing Y for fixed X, so only maximization is tabulated (p. 2).
  - Table 1 lists, for each (fixed, maximized) pair, whether the problem is trivial, solved for which n and by whom, or open (p. 2). Table 2 repeats this for equilateral polygons (pp. 2–3).
  - Closed-form vertex formulas for A, P, D, S and W make each cell an explicit nonconvex program (pp. 1–2).
  - Three global solvers are named: QP (branch and cut for nonconvex QCQP), IBBA (interval branch and bound), and GloptiPoly (SDP moments) (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Planar convex polygons with vertices v_i = (x_i, y_i) in cyclic order (p. 1).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: None in the abstract. Results are cited, not proved (pp. 2–3).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a map of the field. Most solved non-trivial cells cover very small n, odd n, or n with an odd factor (p. 2). Open cells include max W for A = 1, max A for S = 1 and max W for S = 1 (Table 1, p. 2), and more in the equilateral table (p. 3). For equilateral unit-width polygons with odd n, the optima are "arbitrarily close to symmetrical trapezoids" (pp. 2–3, citing Audet & Ninin, in preparation). The research directions are promised for the talk and not written down (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- new-pattern candidate (evidence, third occurrence): **extremal-geometry side line: numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later** (s03; S104 above). This abstract states the pattern in one sentence (p. 1).
- new-pattern candidate: **status grid as agenda**. Enumerate a problem family as a grid, reduce it by symmetry, and mark each cell trivial / solved (for which n, by whom) / open (pp. 2–3). It is the geometry analogue of Method 4's public best-known-solution lists (analogy, not evidence).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D1) Map your problem family as a grid of (fixed quantity × optimized quantity). Collapse it with the min/max duality. Fill each cell with trivial / solved-for-which-cases (with reference) / open, and pick your next paper from the open cells (pp. 2–3).

**Quotes**:
- "This combination allowed solution of instances than could be solved by any one of these two approaches alone." (p. 1)
- "Minimizing a first attribute while fixing a second one is equivalent to maximizing the second one while fixing the first one." (p. 2)

### D002 · On proper refinement of Nash equilibria for bimatrix games (Automatica 2012, 10.1016/j.automatica.2011.07.013)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Belhaiza (first author; KFUPM, PhD Polytechnique 2008), P. Hansen (p. 1, p. 7) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the last step of the game-equilibrium line: enumeration (SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 2001; JOTA 2006), then refinement (p. 1, refs p. 7). The paper was partially presented at ROADEF 2011. Received January 2008 and revised March 2011 (p. 1), a three-year revision. Audet's bio lists structured global optimization and blackbox nonsmooth optimization as his interests (p. 7). **Read**: all 7 pages, including the appendix on refinement concepts.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Games with many equilibria need refinements. Perfect and proper equilibria are "rarely used in practice" for "Lack of analytical and numerical tools" (p. 1). The question is posed verbatim as "How can we automatically detect proper extreme Nash equilibria?" (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: 
  - Define ε-proper profiles (Def. 2.1, p. 2).
  - Encode them as a set Ω^σ_ε with big-M binaries u_ih, v_jl. The binaries express the implication *if row i pays less than row h, then x_1i ≤ ε x_1h*, with u_ih + u_hi ≤ 1 and a floor σ on all probabilities (p. 2).
  - A parametric 0–1 mixed QP (3.5) then minimizes ε over Ω^σ_ε near a given perfect equilibrium. The equilibrium is proper iff (3.5) is feasible for all small σ and f(σ) → 0 (Prop. 3.1, pp. 2–3).
  - The QP is solved by NEW-QP, with binaries written as u − u² = 0 (p. 3).
  - The numerical output is used only as an *indication*: the ε-sequence is then built by hand (pp. 3–5).
  - A second program (3.6) maximizes σ with ε ≤ ε̄/2. A zero optimum certifies non-properness (Thm 3.5, p. 5).
  - A six-step procedure (enumerate extreme equilibria → Nash maximal subsets → perfect extreme equilibria and Selten subsets → (3.5) → analytic sequence → randomize within a Selten subset) finds a proper equilibrium for any bimatrix game (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Finite bimatrix games. L is "sufficiently large" (p. 2). The existence of a proper equilibrium is taken from Myerson 1978 (p. 2), and extreme perfect equilibria from Borm et al. (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: 
  - Props. 2.2–2.3 prove the encoding sound, and complete for small σ, by case analysis on the binaries (p. 2).
  - Corollary 3.2 gets monotonicity of ε(σ) from nested feasible sets, Ω^{σ''} ⊆ Ω^{σ'} for σ'' > σ' (p. 3).
  - The proof of Theorem 3.5 is two sentences (p. 5).
  - Whether f(σ) "converges to zero" is judged numerically. Table 2 labels equilibria with ε̄ ≈ 0.054–0.078 at σ̄ ≈ 10⁻⁵ as proper, and those with ε̄ = 0.20–0.73 as not (p. 4). The authors themselves warn that numerical noise makes certification "risky" (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: Three small worked games:
  - A 5×5 game with 7 extreme equilibria enumerated by EχMIP (Tables 1–2, pp. 3–4).
  - A 2×3 zero-sum game with no extreme proper equilibrium, where ε̄ → 1/2 (p. 5).
  - Myerson's 4×2 example, where ε̄ ≈ 0.618 for the non-proper equilibria (Fig. 1, pp. 5–6).
  - No timing is reported and no larger games are tried.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A detection tool, plus an analytic procedure that always yields a proper equilibrium (p. 6). The authors suggest it "could be useful" for subgame-perfect equilibria of extensive games (p. 6). The limits are implicit: numerical judgement of convergence (p. 3), and hand construction of sequences in Steps 5–6 (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (outside DFO): the paper states an iff characterization plus a one-sided certificate for the negative case (pp. 2–5), and it is explicit that the numerics cannot certify the positive case (p. 3). There is no counterexample to hypotheses.
- new-pattern candidate: **numeric indication, analytic certificate**. Let the global solver point to candidates and prove the property by construction. This generalizes the s03 geometry candidate (*numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later*) to game theory (pp. 3–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Encode a conditional ordering constraint (*if a_i < a_h, then x_i ≤ ε x_h*) with two binaries per pair, big-M on the payoff comparison, and an exclusivity constraint u_ih + u_hi ≤ 1 (p. 2).
- (D4) Prove monotonicity of a parametric optimal value by showing the feasible sets are nested in the parameter (Corollary 3.2, p. 3).
- (D2) To certify a *negative* limit property, solve a companion program that maximizes the parameter under half the observed bound and show that its optimum is zero (Theorem 3.5, p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "How can we automatically detect proper extreme Nash equilibria?" (p. 2)
- "Therefore, it would be risky to use the result provided by the optimization to certify that an equilibrium is proper." (p. 3)

### S118 · Construction of sparse signal representations with adaptive multiscale orthogonal bases (Signal Processing 2012, 10.1016/j.sigpro.2011.12.001; text read: Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2010–42, July 2010)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. Saucier (first author; Polytechnique Montréal) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: This is an interdisciplinary paper in which optimization designs the basis. It extends Saucier's single-layer multiscale-PCA construction (ref. 15, p. 8, p. 11). Audet's contribution is not stated. **Read**: abstract, §1, §2.1–2.2, §3–6 in full; §2.4–2.5 skimmed; §2.3 (pp. 9–10) not read.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Choosing a wavelet "has consequently become a significant problem" (p. 7). The usual approach is to try standard bases and keep the best, or to match wavelets to a signal (p. 7). The entry is to leave the multiresolution framework, and its self-similarity and fast transform, and build an adaptive multiscale orthonormal (AMO) basis of R^N "from an optimization perspective" (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**: 
  - Build the basis from small to large scales, level by level and layer by layer. Within a layer the vectors are shifted copies. Scales double per level, and each layer keeps at least α degrees of freedom after the orthogonality and normalization constraints (pp. 8–9).
  - The multilayer design gives Pssv = 1 − α/(2^{nmax−2} ℓ1) → 1, against at most 2/3 for the earlier single-layer design (p. 11).
  - Each layer vector minimizes the smoothed sparsity count f(y) = Σ|σ_i^T y|^p over the unit sphere, with p ∈ ]0,1] (eqs. 14–15, p. 12).
  - Subsample the windows (I_k, step 50–200) to tame the enumeration (pp. 14–15).
  - Choose p per layer from {1, 1/2, …, 1/2^10} by the true count of coefficients below 10⁻¹² on the full reference signal (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Sampled signals in R^N; a reference signal of length N_s = 1.5N (p. 14); sparsity threshold τ = 10⁻¹² (p. 14). Theorem 3.1 needs S to span R^d, with p ∈ ]0,1] (p. 13). (In the step-function definition, u(x) = 1 for x ≥ 0 would also count zero products; it reads as a slip, p. 12.)
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 3.1 says every optimum of (15) is orthogonal to at least d − 1 linearly independent σ_i (p. 13). The proof is by contradiction:
  1. If not, there is a direction v ⟂ ŷ and ⟂ all active σ_i.
  2. On the chord y(t) = (1 − ε)ŷ + t v, the function g(t) is a sum of concave powers, hence concave, and g(0) = (1 − ε)^p f(ŷ) < f(ŷ).
  3. Concavity gives g(0) ≥ ½(f(y_a) + f(y_b)) ≥ f(ŷ), a contradiction (pp. 13–14).
  Corollary 3.2 then reduces the continuous problem to enumerating (d − 1)-subsets and taking kernels (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: Seven families of piecewise signals (polynomial, sinusoid, exponential and their mixtures), with piece lengths U[100, 200] (pp. 16–17). Each AMO basis is built on one reference realization and tested on 100 independent test signals, with minimum and median sparsity ratio (SR) reported (p. 16). The Daubechies baseline gets its **best order per test signal**, chosen from 1–40 (p. 16). AMO wins in every family by 6 to 37 points (Table 1, p. 17). Timings run from 0.26 to 15 min in Table 1 (p. 17), while §4.2 says "between 15 seconds and 13 minutes" (p. 15). Variability is given as ± over realizations (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The claimed advantages are automatic basis selection, higher SR, and zero coefficients for a larger family of signals (p. 20). The drawbacks are stated concretely: minutes of computation for N ≈ 1000, O(N²) storage, and no fast transform (O(N²) against O(N)) (pp. 20–21). The authors are "not certain that the maximum possible SR was obtained", because step size k = 1 was never run (p. 15). Future work: use surplus degrees of freedom (ℓ1 > q + 1) for other objectives, such as correlation with structured noise (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (outside DFO): the baseline gets its best configuration per test instance, and results are on held-out realizations, but the currency is sparsity, not evaluations (p. 16). It is evidence for the s04 candidate *compare each model class with its own best configuration*.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: a structural theorem about optimal solutions (a vertex-type optimum) replaces convergence analysis, and there are no counterexamples (pp. 13–14).
- new-pattern candidate: **concavity on chords ⇒ finite candidate set**. Prove that optima sit where d − 1 constraints are active, then enumerate (pp. 13–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) For min Σ|a_iᵀy|^p over the unit sphere, or any objective that is concave along chords once the active terms are fixed, prove that optima have d − 1 active zero products by the chord-midpoint contradiction. Then solve by enumerating (d − 1)-subsets and kernels (pp. 13–14).
- (D5) Build your method on one realization, test on 100 independent realizations, and give the baseline its best configuration per test instance. Report minimum and median, not just the mean (p. 16).
- (D2) When a smoothing exponent is a nuisance parameter, solve for a geometric grid of values and select by the unsmoothed target metric on the full data (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "We emphasize that this orthogonality is a consequence of the optimization, as it is not built into the construction algorithm." (p. 18)
- "In that sense, we are not certain that the maximum possible SR was obtained, which is not necessarily a drawback if the SR obtained for k > 1 is very high." (p. 15)

### S074 · Snow water equivalent estimation using blackbox optimization (Pacific Journal of Optimization 2013; no DOI/arXiv; text read: preprint of February 23, 2011)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Alarie and L.-A. Leclaire (Hydro-Québec research institute IREQ), V. Garnier, S. Le Digabel (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is an industrial MADS application with Hydro-Québec. It grows out of the tsunami-buoy and hydraulic-capture positioning work (p. 22), and more strategies are in Garnier's MSc thesis of June 2010 (ref. 24, p. 22, p. 24). Audet's funding line names NSERC, AFOSR and ExxonMobil (p. 1). **Read**: all 26 pages; references skimmed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: About 30% of Hydro-Québec's hydraulic reserves come from snowmelt (p. 2). The new GMON gamma monitors give daily snow water equivalent (SWE) readings, so the question is where to place m monitors to minimize the kriging interpolation error over a catchment (pp. 3–4). The paper names two pathologies: a **fragmented feasible domain** (3.7%, 11.7% and 33.5% feasible pixels on GAT, STM and LG, p. 4) and a **proprietary blackbox** that "cannot be executed outside of HQ" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: 
  - (i) A deterministic **spiral repair** inside the blackbox moves an infeasible location to the nearest feasible pixel within ‖·‖∞ ≤ 15, or else declares a failure (p. 5).
  - (ii) A cheap **potential-well surrogate** of the error map, whose constants were chosen by HQ experts' visual comparison with true error maps (pp. 6–8).
  - (iii) **Groups of variables**: the mesh matrix D must positively span each group's subspace, and D = [I; −I] trivially does (p. 10). Coordinate search is the all-singleton case and MADS the single-group case (p. 10).
  - Groups start as INDIV, PAIRS or ALL and are rebuilt by five rules (STATIC, DIST, CLUSTER, MVT, REGRES). Each non-static rule ends with one group holding all variables after finitely many calls (pp. 12–14).
  - Regrouping is triggered after n_r consecutive failed iterations, with n_r increasing by one each time (p. 12).
  - Trial points are ordered by the error map (surrogate phase) or by surrogate value (true phase), exploiting opportunism (pp. 15–16, 18).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f may be +∞, since evaluations can fail (p. 8). Convergence is inherited from MADS Theorem 3.12, which needs f Lipschitz near a refining point (p. 11). The true blackbox is ordinary kriging (ISATIS) on a reference SWE map of March 15, 1990, taking 1–2 s per evaluation (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 3.1: with MADS directions inside each group, the refining directions are dense only in the *union of the group subspaces* (p. 11). The proof is three sentences. The authors then show what is lost: with overlapping groups {1,2,3}, {3,4}, {5}, a direction such as d = (0,0,0,1,1) may have a negative Clarke derivative (p. 11). The fix is structural: merge until one group is all of N, after which "the entire convergence analysis of MADS applies" (pp. 12, 14, 22).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: 3 basins × m = 5…10 monitors gives 18 instances. NOMAD runs with defaults, a 1000-evaluation budget, and reports at 250/500/1000 (TEB), averaged as relative improvement over x0 (pp. 14–15). The surrogate phase has 12 grouping combinations (Table 3, p. 17). Six strategies go on to the true function, started from the best surrogate solution (pp. 17–18). Results: per-instance Table 5 and a performance profile (pp. 19–21). The differences are small: means 4.98–5.13% (Table 6, p. 21). Baselines are CS and MADS only, with one run per instance.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic (grouping with a guarantee restored by regrouping) and applied (p. 22). The key reversal is reported openly. INDIV STATIC was best on the surrogate (Tables 2, 4, pp. 16, 18) but never best on the true function, and worst on 7 of 18 instances (p. 20). The authors conclude the surrogate "was possibly too simple" (p. 20). The winning MVT rule is generic, not specific to positioning (p. 22). Kriging with external drift is planned (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: grouping and trial-point ordering are practical freedoms, and the regrouping rules are designed so that the MADS guarantee is recovered after finitely many regroupings (pp. 12, 14, 22). Ordering by surrogate value uses the surrogate without touching the Poll (p. 18).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: Theorem 3.1 states exactly what a fixed grouping guarantees, and the example direction shows which hypothesis (a group containing all variables) cannot be dropped (p. 11).
- Method 2 ✅ variant: the unrelaxable location constraint is enforced by a repair operator inside the blackbox, with failure beyond radius 15, instead of EB or PB (p. 5).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: a real partner blackbox that cannot leave HQ gets a public-testable surrogate stand-in (pp. 3–4, 6). Release of the surrogate is not mentioned. It is evidence for the c07 candidate **scaled-down public twin of a proprietary blackbox**, with a caution: rankings did not transfer (p. 20).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: evaluation budgets at three levels and a performance profile (pp. 15, 21), but no competitor from another family and no replications.
- Research Heuristic 10 ✅ evidence: the MSc thesis (Garnier 2010) holds the fuller set of strategies (p. 22).
- new-pattern candidates (evidence): **surrogates judged and used as rankers** (s03; ordering trial points by surrogate, p. 18); *eventually-static adaptation* (s02; here *eventually-full grouping*: finitely many regroupings, then plain MADS, p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate: **surrogate-to-truth reversal report**. When strategies are chosen on a surrogate, re-run a short list on the true function and publish where the ranking flips (pp. 20, 22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Restrict polls to structured groups of variables for speed, but make every regrouping rule monotone (merge only), so that after finitely many calls one group contains all variables and the standard convergence analysis applies (pp. 12, 14).
- (D2) For fragmented or pixelated feasible sets, put a deterministic nearest-feasible repair (spiral over ‖·‖∞ = 1, …, 15) inside the blackbox, and return a failure only when the repair radius is exhausted (p. 5).
- (D5) Select strategies by their mean over several budgets (250/500/1000) rather than by the best final value, to favour strategies whose performance fluctuates less (p. 17).
- (D5) Develop strategies on the surrogate, then test a short list on the true function from the surrogate's best point, and report the reversals (pp. 14, 18, 20).

**Quotes**:
- "However restricting the exploration to these groups weakens the theoretical convergence analysis and may lead to practical difficulties by making it impossible to escape from an undesirable region by varying only a subset of variables." (p. 11)
- "The usefulness of the regrouping strategies is not apparent from the numerical tests on the surrogate function." (p. 22)
