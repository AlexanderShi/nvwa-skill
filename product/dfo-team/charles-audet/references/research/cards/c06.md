### S130 · Cat-Suite: A collection of optimization problems with categorical and quantitative variables for benchmarking (Les Cahiers du GERAD G–2025–39, 2025, rev. Jan 2026; no DOI)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: E. Hallé-Hannan, Y. Diouane, S. Le Digabel, C. Tribes (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: benchmark companion to the Hallé-Hannan mixed-variable line (cites the 2023 categorical/meta-variable framework [5] and the 2024 graph-structured distance [10], p. 45). CatMADS is not mentioned in the text (grep: no hit).
**Reading note**: §1–2 (pp. 4–7) read in full. The catalogue §3–4 (pp. 7–44) is formulas only. I checked all 60 entry headings against Tables 1–2 and ran a prose scan (no narrative text found). I read about 9 entries in detail (Cat-1, Cat-2, Cat-13, Cat-17, Cat-24 fragment, Cat-27, Cat-cstrs-12, Cat-cstrs-22, Cat-cstrs-30). The other entries were skimmed. References read (p. 45). There is no conclusion section.

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The entry is a gap in the infrastructure, not a gap in a theorem. Categorical variables are notoriously hard because their categories lack metrics and may be unordered, and they arise in engineering (e.g., material choices). The literature offers few test problems that are mixed-variable, which makes new methods hard to test (p. 4). The explicit model is CUTEst for continuous optimization (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea / design**: 60 analytic problems, 30 unconstrained and 30 constrained. Each has at least one categorical and one continuous variable, most have integers, and about half are nonsmooth for fixed discrete variables (p. 4; Tables 1–2, pp. 5–6). The problems are built by named transformations of classical continuous or minimax functions. The labels are `cont-to-cat` (a continuous variable is replaced by a category-dependent map), `fct-to-cases` (a function is split into category-indexed variants), `penalty` (a category-indexed penalty term), `minimax-to-cat` (each minimax piece becomes a category; taken from [16]), `con-to-int`, and `cat table` (category pairs map to tabulated values; taken from [17]) (pp. 5–6). Several constrained problems are Cat-X with added constraints, i.e., supersets of unconstrained ones (Table 2, p. 6). Some problems have a user-set dimension (⋆) (p. 5). The code is released in Python at github.com/bbopt/Cat-Suite, and the repository also provides the best known feasible values for the problems (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The problem is min f(x) s.t. g_j(x) ≤ 0, with x partitioned by type (x^cat, x^int, x^cont) and X a Cartesian product (p. 4). It is explicitly **non-hierarchical**: there are no meta variables, dimension is fixed, and bounds are predefined (p. 4). Constraints are either taken from the source problem or built so that a feasible point always exists (p. 7). Constraints are not classified (EB/PB/hidden): all are analytic inequality constraints (pp. 4, 7).
- **D4 Analysis & proof**: Not applicable (no theorems). The only construction lemma is the feasibility-by-construction recipe: start from a reference point, add constraints that it satisfies, and flip a constraint's sign if it is violated (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**: None in the manuscript. There are no solver runs, profiles or budgets. The best-known values are said to be in the repository (p. 5); the repository was not checked.
- **D6 Positioning**: The collection sits between communities. The introduction cites MADS for mixed variables [1, 3], Bayesian optimization with categorical kernels [7, 8, 20] and metaheuristics [22, 23] as the users (p. 4). The problems are drawn from Jamil & Yang's global-optimization survey [13], Lukšan–Vlček nonsmooth minimax problems [14], Munoz Zuniga & Sinoquet [16], Pelamatti et al. [17], Crélot et al. [6], the Surjanovic–Bingham VLSE library [21], Moré–Garbow–Hillstrom [15] and Roustant et al. [19] (Tables 1–2 and p. 45). There is no criticism of prior work: the framing is purely constructive.
- **D7 Writing**: The introduction is short (about one page, p. 4) and ends with an open invitation to contribute problems (p. 4). The provenance tables have `Ref.`, `Original problem` and `Modifications` columns, so every problem is traceable (pp. 5–6). I noticed some editorial inconsistencies. p. 6 names the Cat-23 constrained problem of Table 2 as the only unmodified problem taken from [17]. But Table 1 lists Cat-23 as Hal-04 from [11] (p. 5), and it is the §4.7 heading, Cat-cstrs-7 (Goldstein), that is marked as taken directly from [17] (p. 26). Table 2 says `HS-114` while the §4.9 heading says `HS144` (pp. 6, 27). `Cat-17 4.17` refers to a problem that is in §3.17 (p. 6). The paragraph that announces four techniques goes on to name six labels (pp. 5–6).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is at the software/data level: a benchmark artifact with no algorithm or theory. The limits are stated only implicitly: the problems are analytic only, not hierarchical (p. 4), and the collection is an ongoing project to be extended with more diverse problems (pp. 3–4). There is no experiment showing that the set discriminates between solvers.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the release habit is confirmed (GitHub bbopt repo, best-known values, citable report, pp. 3, 5). But the problems are synthetic analytic transformations, not real blackboxes from partners. Method 4's requirement of a real pathology is not met here.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (infrastructure): it supplies the target-pathology layer of Method 5 step 2 for categorical variables. The abstract opens by calling benchmarking on test problems essential both for assessing performance and for tuning parameters (p. 3). There is no budget-based comparison in this paper.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: it keeps the g_j(x) ≤ 0 convention (p. 4) but does not type constraints (unrelaxable, relaxable or hidden). This is a gap relative to the constraint-semantics practice.
- Heuristic 10 / Workflow C step 6 ✅: a benchmark released alongside the Hallé-Hannan categorical framework line (refs [5], [10], p. 45).
- New-pattern candidate: **benchmark by systematic transformation**: a named catalogue of operators (cont-to-cat, fct-to-cases, penalty, minimax-to-cat, cat table) that turns classical continuous problems into mixed-variable ones (pp. 5–6).
- New-pattern candidate: **feasible-by-construction constraints** around a reference point (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To build a test set for a new variable class, apply named, documented operators to well-known functions (e.g., map a continuous variable through a category-dependent function; assign each minimax piece to a category; index a coefficient by a category-pair table). Record the operator used for each problem in a `Modifications` column (pp. 5–6).
- Make constrained problems as unconstrained problem + added constraints. Guarantee feasibility by choosing a reference point and orienting each constraint so that the point satisfies it (flip the sign if violated) (pp. 6–7).
- Give each problem a provenance row (source reference, original problem, modifications, n per type, number of category combinations ℓ, m, smooth yes/no) so reviewers can audit the collection at a glance (Tables 1–2, pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "However, the current literature offers few test problems that are mixed-variable, making it difficult to thoroughly test the performance of novel solution methods." (p. 4)
- "To this end, one may start from a reference point and add constraints in such a way that this point remains feasible." (p. 7)

### S137 · Convergence towards a local minimum by direct search methods with a covering step (Optimization Letters 19(1):211–231, 2025, 10.1007/s11590-024-02165-2; arXiv 2401.07097)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: P.-Y. Bouchet (first author in the arXiv text), L. Bourdin (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the third step of a discontinuous-DFO chain. It follows DiscoMads' revealing step (SIOPT 2022, [2]) and the Math. Program. 2024 counterexample + revealing poll step ([3]), and it comes from Bouchet's PhD thesis, ch. 4 (p. 2). The acknowledgement credits S. Kojtych for foreseeing Theorem 1 (p. 1).
**Reading note**: the whole paper was read, including Appendix A (algorithm variant, experiments) and Appendix B (topological proofs). The text is arXiv v4 (13 Nov 2024).

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The problem is min f(x) with f: Rⁿ → R∪{+∞} possibly discontinuous. Few DFO methods are studied in this setting (p. 2). The entry is the gap between what the existing DSM theory proves and what users observe. Existing results give a necessary condition (a Rockafellar derivative) at refined points, provided that f(x^k) → f(x*) [25]. The authors' own previous work [3] obtains this only when all refining subsequences share one refined point (p. 2). The goal is to show that **all** refined points are local solutions (p. 2). The paper's origin is the attempt to understand why the revealing step helped. The authors noticed that it gives density of trial points near a unique refined point, but could not deduce local optimality (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea / design**: A **covering step** runs before search and poll. It evaluates (at least) one point in the ball B_r(x^k) that is as far as possible from all previously evaluated points: Oracle 1, D_C^k = round(argmax_{d∈cl(B_r)} dist(x^k+d, H^k), mesh) (Algorithm 1, p. 3; Eq. (1), p. 8). What changed: this one extra step, plus the use of the smallest poll radius so far, δ̄^k = min_{ℓ≤k} δ^ℓ, which the proof needs (Remark 1, p. 4; p. 10). What did not change: mesh-based and sufficient-decrease DSM with a positive-basis poll. The paper states that the covering step fits in the framework of the search step, so cDSM inherits all DSM properties (Remark 1, p. 4). The design also relaxes: an α-approximate oracle (any point achieving a fraction α of the maximal distance) is enough, which allows a grid or Halton search (Eq. (2), p. 8). Restricting the history to a ball of radius r+δr also works (Eq. (3), p. 8). The cost is 1 evaluation per iteration versus at least n+1 for the poll (p. 9). Section 5 lifts the step into a generic DFO framework (Algorithm 2: covering step + optional step, no poll, no mesh), so the guarantee applies to any algorithm it is fitted into (pp. 9–10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumption 1 (p. 4) has three parts: (a) f is bounded below with bounded sublevel sets; (b) f restricted to X = dom f is lower semicontinuous; (c) X admits a partition into **ample continuity sets** (S ⊆ cl(int S), i.e., semi-open) on which f is continuous. The paper calls (a) and (b) usual and (c) unusual, and gives (c) its own section (p. 4, §6). Lipschitz continuity on each piece, which [3] required, is dropped because generalized derivatives are no longer used (p. 11). The starting point must satisfy x⁰ ∈ X; the authors say this could be relaxed via the progressive barrier or a two-phase method (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Analysis & proof**: The proof has two stages. (i) **Property 1** (B_r(x*) ⊆ cl(H) for every refined point) plus Assumption 1 give Theorem 1 (p. 4). Proposition 2 carries it (pp. 4–5): for any x in B_r(x*) inside a continuity set X_i, a topological lemma (Prop. 8.d, p. 16) provides evaluated points y_ℓ ∈ H ∩ X_i → x. With κ(ℓ) the first iteration that evaluated y_ℓ, the failure/acceptance rule gives f(y_ℓ) ≥ f(x^{κ(ℓ)+1}) − ρ(δ̄^{κ(ℓ)}), and the limit uses continuity on X_i and δ̄ → 0. Theorem 1 then follows by contradiction plus lower semicontinuity (p. 5). (ii) An **a priori** sufficient condition: any `covering oracle` with ratio β ∈ (0,1] (Definition 1, p. 6) ensures Property 1 (Prop. 3, p. 7). The proof uses Bolzano–Weierstrass to show that consecutive covering points cluster, then a chain of distance inequalities (p. 7). The counterexamples are small and explicit. Example 1 (pp. 5–6) runs the revealing step on f = ‖x‖∞ in 2D with a crafted search step: it produces two refined points ±1, neither a local minimizer, which shows the predecessor device fails with more than one refined point. Proposition 6 (p. 12) shows Assumption 1.c is **tight**: a 2D f whose continuity set R*₊×{0} is locally thin makes an algorithm stall at a non-minimizer. Propositions 4–5 (p. 11) prove that Assumption 1.c is **strictly weaker** than the assumptions of [2] and [3], and each strictness claim comes with an explicit 1D or 2D example (pp. 11, 17). A cost remark: an ε-cover of B_r needs at least (r/ε)ⁿ covering points, so each ball must be visited during at least (r/ε)ⁿ iterations (p. 9). There are no rates.
- **D5 Experiments**: These are in Appendix A.2 only (pp. 14–15). There are two 2D discontinuous test problems. In the second one, on the half-plane x₁ ≤ 0, f is finite only on a thin cusp-shaped region around a line and +∞ elsewhere (p. 14; Fig. 3, p. 15). The setup is x⁰ = (98.77…, 12.35…), δ⁰ = 1, r = δ⁰/10, a momentum search, an OrthoMADS poll, and a stop at δ < 10⁻⁸ or k ≥ 300. The comparison is 10 executions of cDSM against 10 of DSM without the covering step (p. 14). The plots show the best f against the number of calls to f, plus the **ratio of iteration outcomes** (search, poll or covering success, or failure) over the iterations (Figs. 4–5, p. 15). On the easy problem, cDSM costs 5–10% more evaluations. On the thin-set problem, DSM stays in its starting continuity set in 4/10 runs, while cDSM always reaches the right set (p. 15). The authors suspect that the difficulty comes from the missing interior cone property (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper extends Vicente & Custódio 2012 [25] and the authors' own [2], [3]. It says the covering step generalizes the revealing step and that [2, 3] fail to deduce the local optimality (p. 2). The comparison is a formal ordering of assumptions (strictly weaker, Props. 4–5) rather than prose criticism (p. 11). Theorem 1 is said to be stronger in three aspects: local optimality instead of necessary conditions; an assumption on the problem only, not on the algorithm's execution; and validity for all refining subsequences, not only those with dense refined directions (p. 12). Space-filling-curve and Lipschitz global methods are cited as alternatives that need more structure (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: The contribution paragraph is organised as two main goals and two related auxiliary ones (p. 2). The paper explains the name change (revealing → covering, p. 2). Remark 2 states plainly that no existing DSM ensures Property 1 (p. 4). A closing Table 1 compares DSM, cDSM and a hypothetical DFO method with involved covering step design row by row (search/poll/covering) (p. 13). Hedging is honest: the step lacks efficiency in practice (Table 1, p. 13), and a poor instance may be inefficient in finite time (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical (a new guarantee level) plus an algorithmic step that transfers to other methods. The limits are specific. The covering step is asymptotic and slow in finite time (p. 13). The (r/ε)ⁿ curse means the choice of r trades escaping local minima against covering speed (p. 9). There is no automatic choice of r beyond the defaults δ⁰/10 or δ⁰ (p. 9). The experiments are toy problems. Future directions (pp. 12–13): covering steps inside Bayesian and model-based methods; oracles driven by expected improvement or models instead of pure distance; an explicit partition of X in the assumption; relaxing x⁰ ∈ X via PB; and replacing the revealing step with the covering step in DiscoMads.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (strong): the guarantee ladder moves up from necessary conditions to local minima. The new assumption is proved strictly weaker than the predecessors' (Props. 4–5, p. 11) and tight by counterexample (Prop. 6, p. 12). Example 1 audits the group's own earlier device (pp. 5–6). Method 3 step 3 is practiced: Lipschitz continuity is dropped because the proof does not use it (p. 11).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence + ⚠ variant: the covering step is admitted as a search-type step, so DSM properties are inherited (Remark 1, p. 4). But the new guarantee is carried by this mandatory search-like step, and in Algorithm 2 the poll becomes optional (Table 1, p. 13; Theorem 2, p. 10). This inverts the split in which a rigid Poll carries the proof.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the currency is evaluations (plots against calls to f, p. 15), but the test is two 2D toy problems in an appendix with no other solver family.
- New-pattern candidate: **a priori checkable conditions**. Prefer sufficient conditions on the oracle or the problem that can be checked before running over properties of the run. Proposition 3 is checked a priori whereas Property 1 must be checked a posteriori (p. 8). A local relaxation of Assumption 1.c is rejected as a main theorem because it is only checkable a posteriori (p. 12). Theorem 1 is praised for assuming only the problem (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: **algorithm-agnostic guarantee step**. Isolate a step whose guarantee holds inside a generic framework, so it can be plugged into other DFO families (Algorithm 2, Theorem 2, pp. 9–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Farthest-point covering oracle: each iteration, spend one evaluation at the point of B_r(x^k) farthest from the evaluation history (or any point within a fixed fraction α of that maximum). This yields density of the history around every accumulation point. The oracle can be computed with a distance transform on the mesh, a smooth inverse-distance surrogate, or a Halton/grid search (pp. 7–8).
- Split a convergence proof through a named property of the evaluation history (e.g., `B_r(x*) ⊆ cl(H)`). Prove (a) property ⇒ theorem and (b) an a priori oracle condition ⇒ property separately, so the property can be reused for other algorithms (pp. 3–8, 9–10).
- In proofs about evaluated points, use the first-evaluation index κ(ℓ) = min{k : y_ℓ ∈ T^k}. It links any evaluated point to the acceptance test of the iteration that produced it, and κ(ℓ) → ∞ because each T^k is finite (pp. 4–5).
- Use the running minimum δ̄^k = min_{ℓ≤k} δ^ℓ of the step size when you need a monotone sequence that tends to 0 (lim inf δ^k = 0 only gives this for the running minimum) (Remark 1, p. 4; p. 10).
- Prove tightness of a regularity assumption with a 2D function whose bad continuity set is a half-line (locally thin), and an algorithm instance that never samples it (p. 12).
- Diagnostic plot for multi-step algorithms: the cumulative ratio of iteration outcomes (search success, poll success, new-step success, failure) against the iteration index. It shows which step drives progress at each phase (Figs. 4–5, p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "Theorem 2 shows that the covering step is, roughly speaking, a self-sufficient algorithmic step that ensures the local optimality of all accumulation points returned by any algorithm it is fitted into." (p. 10)
- "Hence our framework cannot be broadened using only information available a priori." (p. 12)

### S150 · Risk averse constrained blackbox optimization under mixed aleatory/epistemic uncertainties (Computational Optimization and Applications 92:375–435, 2025, 10.1007/s10589-025-00704-w; arXiv 2310.11380)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. Bigeon, R. Couderc, M. Kokkolaras (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the second of two Couderc papers. It builds on the same team's zeroth-order sequential stochastic BBO paper (AIMS Math 2023, [7], p. 27). This is a non-direct-search branch of the stochastic/noisy BBO line (alongside StoMADS) with a reliability-engineering flavour (Kokkolaras).
**Reading note**: the text is arXiv v1 (18 Oct 2023). The page numbers refer to it, and the published COAP version may differ. The main text (pp. 1–26) was read in full. Appendix A (proof of Thm 5.1, pp. 32–40) was skimmed: the lemma statements, Steps 1–4 and the final saddle-point argument were read, not every inequality. Appendix B (test problems, pp. 40–43) was skimmed (SCD and WBD read). Appendix C (boxplots, pp. 43–46) was skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The problem is constrained BBO where objective and constraints are blackbox outputs subject to unknown uncertainties ξ, written with generic measures Ξ_j (Eq. (1), p. 2). The entry is a **criteria audit of the RBDO literature**. FORM/SORA-type methods linearise and need a known distribution (Nataf transformation). Sampling methods do only reliability analysis. Epistemic uncertainty (interval or point data) is poorly handled. The recent non-probabilistic, Bayesian and scenario approaches mostly do reliability analysis and lack a convergence proof to an optimal point (pp. 2–4). Table 1 scores 11 method families on 5 criteria (nonsmooth constraints, nonsmooth objective, noisy objective, unknown aleatory distribution, lack of data), and `This work` ticks all of them (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea / design**: The design is a pipeline. First, reformulate with **CVaR** for objective and constraints (Eq. (5), p. 6), using the Rockafellar–Uryasev auxiliary variable t (Eq. (6), Lemma 2.1, p. 7). Second, smooth both sources of nonsmoothness (the blackbox and the max in CVaR) by convolution with a **truncated Gaussian** kernel, so samples never leave the unrelaxable bounds (Lemma 3.1, p. 8; §3.2, p. 11). Third, relax the constraints with a **Lagrangian**, following Chow et al. [15] (Eq. (15), p. 13). Fourth, solve with **RAMSA**, a four-timescale stochastic approximation (Algorithm 1, p. 15). The fastest timescale holds Adam-like moving averages M, V of the gradient estimate. The next holds t (the VaR estimate), then x, and the slowest holds λ (pp. 14–16). What changed relative to [15, 42]: those use MDP structure to estimate gradients, which is impossible to use here. It is replaced by a two-point zeroth-order estimator that needs two blackbox outputs regardless of dimension (pp. 1, 5). Multi-timescale updates replace the nested inner loops (VaR estimate, chance constraints) (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumption 1 (pp. 5–6) comes in three increasingly strong levels on C_j(x,ξ). They are an integrable envelope κ₁; Lipschitz in x with integrable κ₂; and an almost-surely bounded Lipschitz constant κ₃ plus a continuous CDF. The authors stress that nothing is assumed about differentiability (p. 6). Assumption 2 holds the four step sizes: non-summable, square-summable, with ratios tending to 0 (p. 14). Theorem 5.1 additionally assumes that the smoothed problem (14) is strictly feasible and that the iterates eventually enter the domain of attraction of (x*, λ*), with λ* in the interior of the multiplier box (p. 16). These are strong, convenience-type assumptions and are not discussed. The bounds are treated as **unrelaxable** in the sense of the Le Digabel–Wild taxonomy [26] (p. 8). The CVaR formulation is admitted to be conservative and possibly infeasible (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof**: (a) The estimator: differentiate the truncated-Gaussian convolution. The mean µ of the truncated Gaussian gives the correction term (u − µ). Subtracting c(x) keeps the estimator unbiased because E[(u−µ)] = 0 and controls the variance as β → 0 (pp. 9–10). E‖u‖ ≤ √n for a truncated Gaussian is obtained by differentiating the log of the normalising constant with respect to β (pp. 10–11). (b) Approximation quality: |CVaR^β − CVaR| ≤ (L₂β₁√n + β₂)/(1−α_j) (Theorem 3.2.1, p. 11). For α_j close enough to 1, a solution of the smoothed problem is feasible for the original CVaR problem, by continuity of CVaR in α up to the essential supremum (Theorem 3.2.3, pp. 12–13). (c) Convergence: the standard ODE method for multi-timescale SA (Borkar ch. 6), applied timescale by timescale. Faster updates are treated as quasi-equilibrated and slower ones as quasi-static (pp. 16–17). Each timescale gets its own Lyapunov function (LaSalle/Khalil) (App. A, pp. 33–39). The KKT conditions of the local saddle point come from projection arguments by contradiction (pp. 39–40). Corollary 5.2 combines (b) and (c): almost-sure convergence to a feasible point whose value is within (L₂β₁√n+β₂)/(1−max α_j) of a local solution (p. 17). There are no rates.
- **D5 Experiments**: There are four analytic RBDO engineering problems from the literature: Steel Column, Welded Beam, Vehicle Side Impact and Speed Reducer (p. 18; App. B). The protocol: 100 runs per hyperparameter set, a budget of 5000 evaluations per run, and each final point re-evaluated by 10,000 Monte Carlo samples. A run counts as successful if every chance constraint is ≥ 0.99. The report gives the mean solution point ± std to show consistent convergence (pp. 18–19). The reference is SORA's published solution. The paper explicitly says the aim is not a direct comparison, because SORA uses analytic expressions and known distributions (p. 19). SORA's evaluation counts (216, 505, 8054, 2486) appear next to RAMSA's 5000 (Tables 3, 6, pp. 20–22). The **hyperparameter-rule protocol** splits the problems into experimental ones (SCD/WBD/VSI) and a validation one (SRD) (pp. 19–21). β₁ is chosen as the minimiser of the gradient-estimator variance at x⁰, then halved (Table 4, p. 21). s₂⁰ is set so that s₂⁰ × (normalised gradient norm) ≈ 10⁻³ (Table 5, p. 21). The negative result is reported: the truncated estimator needs about 3× more evaluations (15,000), which the authors say cannot be attributed to the chosen hyperparameter values (p. 23). The epistemic test on VSI treats uncertain means as points (Bernoulli sampling) or an interval (uniform sampling) (pp. 23–24). A worst-case feasibility audit (Problem (27)) shows the SORA solution is infeasible under epistemic uncertainty (P ≈ 0.88 on constraint 7) (p. 24). Boxplots are in App. C.
- **D6 Positioning**: The main community is RBDO and reliability engineering (FORM/SORM, PMA/RIA, SLA, SORA, SAP, sampling, surrogates, evidence/possibility/fuzzy theory, Bayesian, scenario optimization) (pp. 2–4). The algorithmic lineage is CVaR + multi-timescale SA from constrained RL (Chow et al. [15], Prashanth [42]) and Gaussian smoothing from Nesterov–Spokoiny [39] and Ghadimi–Lan [22] (pp. 5, 8). MADS/NOMAD are not used, and neither are the group's StoMADS papers. The novelty claim is hedged with a to-the-best-of-our-knowledge clause (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract states that the experiments serve three purposes (p. 1), and §6.2 and §7 follow those three purposes (pp. 19, 25). The contributions come as `First / Second / Finally`, each with a section pointer (p. 5). A high-level proof overview in the main text precedes the appendix proof (p. 16), including a two-timescale toy illustration (Eqs. (22)–(25), pp. 16–17). The comparison table uses ✓/✗ marks with footnotes that qualify the authors' own tick: for interval data the method only gives a worst-case solution (p. 4). Minor slips remain, e.g., `VCD` for SCD (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a formulation, an algorithm, a theorem, and practical hyperparameter rules. The limits are written concretely. The rules come from few problems and must be used with caution (p. 22). The truncated estimator is about 3× slower, for reasons still unexplained (pp. 23, 25). CVaR constraints are conservative (p. 5). Only worst-case solutions are possible for interval data (p. 4). Choosing β₁ is hard, and a decreasing β₁ as in [7] is left out (p. 22). Future work: real industrial test cases and comparison with state-of-the-art algorithms (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: constraint semantics drives the algorithm design. Because the bounds are unrelaxable [26], the smoothing kernel is truncated so no evaluation falls outside them (pp. 8, 22, 25).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: general inequality constraints go into a Lagrangian relaxation (p. 13), not a progressive barrier, and there is no treatment of hidden constraints.
- Method 1 / Heuristic 9 ✗ contradiction: there is no direct-search backbone (no mesh, no poll), and convergence comes from a zeroth-order SA/ODE argument (pp. 14–17). SKILL.md should not present keeping the direct-search backbone as universal for Audet's coauthored work.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the guarantee is stated as a two-level ladder (smoothed problem → original CVaR problem → feasibility for α near 1; Thm 3.2, Cor. 5.2, pp. 11–17). The hypotheses (e.g., domain of attraction, strict feasibility) are not tested for necessity by counterexamples.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the budget is fixed (5000/15000 evaluations) and there are 100 replications with spread (pp. 18–19, App. C). But there are no data or performance profiles and no head-to-head competitor on the same information; the comparison is deferred to future work (pp. 19, 26).
- New-pattern candidate: **hyperparameter rules via experimental/validation problem split**, tying problem-dependent parameters to quantities measurable at x⁰ (pp. 19–22).
- New-pattern candidate: **input/output transformations to cut hyperparameters**: map x to [0,1]ⁿ and outputs through arctan(∛·) so that one step size serves all coordinates and constraints (pp. 17–18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Bound-respecting zeroth-order gradient: sample u from a Gaussian truncated to [(b_ℓ−x)/β, (b_u−x)/β] and use ĝ = (u−µ)(c(x+βu) − c(x))/β, with µ the truncated mean. It is unbiased for the smoothed function and never evaluates outside unrelaxable bounds (Lemma 3.1, pp. 8–10).
- Replace nested loops (inner quantile/VaR estimation, multiplier updates) with multi-timescale stochastic approximation. Order the step-size decays from fastest to slowest (the paper uses τ = (0.8, 0.7, 0.6, 0.501)). Prove convergence one timescale at a time, treating faster variables as equilibrated and slower ones as frozen (pp. 14–17, 19).
- Risk-level continuation: start α at 0 and update α^{k+1} = α* + γ(α^k − α*). Choose γ from K_max so that α reaches about 0.9 at the end, which avoids over-conservative early iterations (pp. 18–19).
- Protocol for stochastic constrained solvers: many runs (100) at a fixed evaluation budget. Re-evaluate each final point with an independent Monte Carlo sample (10⁴), define success as all chance constraints above target, and report the mean final point ± std to show that runs converge to one neighbourhood (pp. 18–19).
- To set a smoothing radius, pick the β that minimises the empirical variance of the gradient estimator at x⁰ over a small grid, then halve it. Reuse the same samples to estimate the gradient norm that sets the initial step (pp. 20–21).
- Epistemic feasibility audit of any candidate (including a competitor's): solve a deterministic worst-case problem over the epistemic parameters at the means, then check the chance constraints by Monte Carlo at those worst parameters (pp. 23–24).
