**Batch intent**: These eight entries come from the years when Audet was moving from exact global optimization (the GERAD school) to derivative-free pattern search with Dennis. Five are exact-method side lines:

- In the GPS papers, where does the new idea sit relative to the Search/Poll split, and does the convergence ladder survive (Methods 1 and 3)?
- What does the grant report show about the solver-and-industry channel (Method 6) at the moment MADS was introduced?

### S037 · The largest small octagon (Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series A 2002, 10.1006/jcta.2001.3225)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen, F. Messine, J. Xiong (Salomon Brothers, New York) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the first paper of the extremal-small-polygon side line (SKILL.md Research Trajectory, 2002–2013). The optimum of the conjectured configuration comes from Audet's 1997 thesis (ref. [1], pp. 6, 15). It was computed with the QCQP branch-and-cut of Audet, Hansen, Jaumard and Savard (*Math. Program.* 2000, ref. [2]; card S010). The published version is JCTA 98(1):46–59, 2002 (D016 p. 7; S108 ref. [7], p. 20). **Read**: all 15 pages. The drawings of the 31 configurations are reduced to stray characters (pp. 4–5, 8–9, 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Which unit-diameter n-gon has maximum area? The history is set out on p. 3:
  - Reinhardt (1922) solved odd n.
  - For n = 4 the square is optimal, but so are infinitely many other quadrilaterals.
  - Graham (1975) found the unique optimal hexagon, about 4% larger than the regular one, and conjectured the shape of the optimum for even n ≥ 8.

  The entry is the first open case of that conjecture, the octagon, attacked with linear thrackleations of the diameter graph plus a global optimization algorithm (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: The paper extends Graham's hexagon method in three steps (p. 3; conclusion p. 14).
  1. Graham's argument that the diameter graph of the optimal polygon has a linear thrackleation extends to any n. Woodall's theorem then leaves 31 possible configurations for the octagon (p. 3; Fig. 1, pp. 4–5).
  2. Solve the conjectured configuration (31) exactly, as a QCQP in 10 variables with the symmetry cut x2 ≥ x3. The result is z* ≈ 0.726867, accurate to about 1e−5 (pp. 6–7; vertex coordinates in Table 1, p. 7).
  3. Show that none of the other 30 configurations can exceed 0.7268, using three elimination devices (§4, pp. 8–14; see D4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: The polygons are convex octagons of unit diameter (p. 3). The certificate rests on two things. The first is the numerical solver of [2], accurate to about 1e−5 (p. 7). The second is a set of rounded thresholds: 0.7268 for the octagon, and the heptagon and hexagon optima 0.7198 and 0.6750, both rounded up (pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: The proof is exhaustion over the 31 configurations, with three elimination tools.
  - *Analytic bound in one angle.* If a unit-length segment splits the other six vertices into a group of five and a single vertex, the area is at most 2B∞(θ) + cos θ sin θ + (1 − sin θ)/2 (inequality (2), p. 9). Figure 5 shows that this bound never exceeds z*. That eliminates 23 configurations (§4.1, pp. 9–10). The check is a plot, not an interval certificate (p. 10).
  - *Incumbent-cutoff infeasibility.* For configurations (10), (16) and (29), the known optimal heptagon and hexagon areas give lower bounds on two angles, and hence x3 ≥ 0.465 and x5 ≥ 0.684 (pp. 10–11). QP (3) with the extra constraint area ≥ 0.7268 is then shown infeasible by [2] (p. 11).
  - *Optimality conditions as constraints.* Configurations (17)–(20) are relaxations of (31). Solving (31) had already taken over 100 hours, so solving these relaxations directly was judged unrealistic (p. 12). Instead, move one vertex at a time while it stays at unit distance from another. Either the configuration reduces to (31), or perpendicularity conditions must hold (pp. 12–13). With these conditions added (Table 2, p. 14) and area > 0.7268 imposed, each QP is infeasible (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: none beyond the solver runs. The only timing given is the more than 100 hours for configuration (31) (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The paper confirms Graham's conjecture for the octagon. The optimum is 2.78% larger than the regular octagon, a smaller gain than the hexagon's 4% (p. 14). S108 gives 2.79% for the same values (S108 p. 12). The ratio 0.726867/0.707107 rounds to 2.79%, so the 2.78% here is a truncation. No limitation or future work is stated (p. 14). The authors also leave unstated that §4.1 rests on a plot and the whole proof on a solver tolerance of about 1e−5 (pp. 7, 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: a proposer/certifier split, here in exact optimization. The conjecture proposes the configuration, and its exact optimum becomes the incumbent. Every other case must then be certified unable to beat it (pp. 6–14).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: the thesis-era QCQP branch-and-cut code [2] is the instrument that settles a 1975 conjecture (pp. 6, 11, 14). It is a reused research code, not a maintained product.
- New-pattern candidate: *incumbent-cutoff infeasibility certificate* (pp. 11, 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *necessary optimality conditions as extra constraints*, which make an otherwise too-expensive relaxation solvable (pp. 12–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove an incumbent optimal across a finite case split, add the constraint *objective ≥ incumbent* to each case's formulation and show that the result is infeasible with an exact solver (pp. 11, 14).
- Use the known optima of smaller instances (here the heptagon and hexagon areas) to bound variables of the larger instance before the exact solve (pp. 10–11).
- When a case is a relaxation of the incumbent's case, perturb one variable at a time. Either the missing constraint becomes active, which returns you to the incumbent case, or a first-order condition must hold. Add that condition as a constraint (pp. 12–13).

**Quotes** (the extraction drops c and fi):
- "This proves the rst open ase of a onje ture of Graham (1975)." (p. 2)

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J.E. Dennis Jr. (PI, Rice) and C. Audet (CoPI, Polytechnique/GERAD) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the AFOSR final report for grant F49620-01-1-0013, covering 1 December 2000 to 30 November 2003 and dated 10 February 2004 (p. 1). It sits at the hinge between two periods of SKILL.md's trajectory: the move to derivative-free pattern search (~2000–2002) and theory building (2003–2009). It lists the MADS paper as submitted to *SIAM J. Optim.* (p. 8), and it names Le Digabel and Zghal as new PhD students under Audet who will work on MADS extensions (p. 4). It is not a research paper: it has no model, theorem or experiment section. **Read**: all 19 pages.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The grant's subject is engineering design with expensive simulations and surrogates (title, p. 1). The report states the research problem as the main limitation of GPS: the algorithm class relies on a fixed finite set of directions, and its convergence results are tied to those directions (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: MADS lifts that restriction by letting the algorithm explore a set of directions that is dense in the whole space. It generalizes GPS (p. 4). The software of the same period is NOMAD C++ cycle 5, written by Gilles Couture (pp. 3, 5). It offers:
  - GPS or MADS;
  - a graphical interface;
  - a cache that avoids repeated evaluations;
  - a filter method for general constraints;
  - a barrier for bounds, with directions conforming to the box;
  - user-defined surrogates;
  - Latin hypercube searches.
- **D3 Assumptions**: not applicable (no model or theorem).
- **D4 Proof devices**: not applicable. The report only asserts that MADS yields convergence results that did not hold for GPS (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: anecdotal only, with no protocol:
  - an ExxonMobil tanker-composition problem, where NOMAD never needed more than 15 minutes against over a day before (p. 3);
  - a four-variable catalytic-reactor kinetics fit, where GPS beat a gradient-based method and MADS was faster than GPS (p. 5);
  - C.T. Kelley's public well-location problems, where NOMAD often improved the initial point fastest and often found the best solution (pp. 5–6);
  - Boeing planform design with response-surface surrogates (pp. 16–17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The listed outputs are:
  - papers (pp. 6–9);
  - NOMAD cycle 5 and its industrial users, UTRC and ExxonMobil Upstream, with a DAKOTA integration under negotiation (pp. 3, 5);
  - a textbook in preparation with Abramson (p. 4);
  - an industrial pipeline. It includes ExxonMobil well casing, a mixed problem in which the number of sleeves is a categorical variable (pp. 14–15), and Boeing nozzle design with 100 parameters, 90 of them fixed, at about 3 hours per evaluation (pp. 15–16).

  Delays are given concrete causes: internal funding at ExxonMobil (p. 15) and Boeing's priorities (p. 16). The stated plan is that MADS will replace GPS and that all earlier papers will be recast in the new framework in the book (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: research features already reach industrial users through NOMAD C++ (pp. 3, 5, 16). A user problem, ExxonMobil's well-bore trajectory, is logged as an application for further algorithm development (p. 3). Dennis's talk *Optimization Tech Transfer is a Two-Way Street* (p. 10) names the same loop.

- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the public benchmark here comes from another group, Kelley's well-location problems (p. 5). The group's own industrial problems (Boeing nozzle, ExxonMobil casing) are not released (pp. 14–16).

### S029 · Generalized pattern searches with derivative information (Mathematical Programming 2004, 10.1007/s10107-003-0484-5)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M.A. Abramson (AFIT), J.E. Dennis Jr. (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The preprint is dedicated to Roger Fletcher on his 65th birthday (p. 1). It is GERAD G-2002-28, listed as to appear in *Math. Program.* Series B (D016 p. 7). It builds on the Audet–Dennis GPS analysis [8] (card S002) and the tightness report [5] (card S025), and it belongs to the ~2000–2003 pattern-search period. The experiments run in Abramson's NOMADm (p. 14). **Read**: all 19 pages; references skimmed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper starts from a question users ask: how can derivative information be used at iterates where it is available (abstract, p. 1)? The target is the worst-case cost of the Poll, O(n) evaluations (p. 1). This continues Lewis and Torczon's reduction of the poll set to n + 1 directions (p. 2). Two motivations are given (p. 2):
  - users pick direct search even when gradients exist, hoping for better local minima;
  - many engineering codes supply cheap but inexact gradients.
- **D2 Core idea**: Only the Poll changes. The algorithm prunes every direction d with dᵀ∇f(x_k) > 0 (or with a known positive directional derivative), because such a poll point will fail once the mesh is small enough (Def. 4, p. 5; p. 11).
  - *The key design move* is to poll from a richer set, D = {−1, 0, 1}ⁿ, which is never constructed, instead of a positive basis. For every nonzero v, D contains a positive spanning subset that v prunes to a single direction (Thm 2, p. 9). One function evaluation per Poll therefore suffices when the gradient is known (Thm 3, p. 9).
  - *The surviving direction* can be chosen three ways (eqs. (9)–(11), p. 8). They are the negatives of the normalized ℓ1 and ℓ∞ gradients, and the element of D closest in angle to −∇f (p. 9).
  - *Variants*: approximate gradients (Thm 4, p. 10); a subset J of partial derivatives, giving n + 1 − |J| poll directions (Cor. 1, p. 11); and linear constraints, giving at most |T_ε(x_k)| + 1 directions (Prop. 6, p. 12).
  - *The Search is untouched.* The paper places the global quality of the final solution there (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions** (p. 13):
  - A1: f_X = f + ψ_X is available. f may be nonsmooth, discontinuous or extended-valued.
  - A2: the constraint matrix is rational. This is needed for the mesh argument.
  - A3: the iterates lie in a compact set.

  Gradients need not exist, or be available, at every iterate (p. 13). For approximate gradients, the only requirement is sign agreement on the largest components (ε-approximation, Def. 6, p. 10).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**:
  - Davis's characterization drives the pruning counts: D positively spans iff every nonzero v has some d ∈ D with vᵀd > 0 (Thm 1, p. 3). Prop. 1 shows that positive bases prune poorly, with ρ(D) ≥ |D|/2 (p. 7).
  - Thm 2 is proved by completing the surviving direction d̂ with all directions that make a negative inner product with v. A two-case construction then checks positive spanning (pp. 8–9).
  - Convergence (Thm 6, pp. 13–14) reuses the Clarke-quotient argument of [8] with one new case. If the gradient is used infinitely often along the refining subsequence, a pruned direction has dᵀ∇f(x_k) > 0, which bounds the Clarke quotient below by zero. A direction therefore counts if it was polled *or pruned* infinitely often.
  - Thms 7–8 are cited from [8]: ∇f = 0 under strict differentiability, and KKT under linear constraints with conforming directions (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments** (pp. 14–17):
  - *Setup*: NOMADm (Matlab), Poll-only runs with an empty Search, so that Polls are compared fairly. Eight poll strategies are run on CUTE problems, although the text announces seven strategies and 20 problems. Table 1 has 21 problem rows; ALLINIT and ALLINITU are the constrained and unconstrained versions of one problem (p. 15), which may explain the count. Runs stop when Δ ≤ 1e−4 or after 50,000 evaluations. Function evaluations, gradient evaluations and final f are reported per problem.
  - *Fairness*: starting points were perturbed when a run hit an exact stationary point by chance.
  - *Toy example*: on a 2-D problem, a 2-point random Search recovers the global minimum (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and theoretical. With the full gradient, the worst-case Poll cost falls from n + 1 evaluations to 1, with convergence unchanged (pp. 1, 9, 13–14). Stated limits:
  - no time saving when the gradient costs about n evaluations, as with finite differences (p. 18); with such gradients only the linearly constrained case is likely to save anything (p. 2);
  - the two approaches often converge to different minimizers (p. 18). With a single pruned direction, runs sometimes end at a worse point, as on PALMER1 (p. 18).

  Future work: use derivatives in the Search, e.g. through Hermite interpolants (pp. 2, 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the Search/Poll division of labour is explicit. The Search decides global quality (pp. 4–5; Example 2, p. 15). Here, though, the performance idea goes into the Poll. It is admitted because the convergence proof is redone so that pruned directions count as polled (Thm 6, pp. 13–14). This refines Method 1, step 3: a Poll change is acceptable when the proof survives with the skipped point accounted for.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the guarantee is a ladder relative to local smoothness at the limit (pp. 13–14). Lipschitz continuity gives a Clarke condition, strict differentiability gives ∇f = 0, and linear constraints with conforming directions give KKT. Assumption A2 is flagged as needed (p. 13). The paper notes that liminf Δ_k = 0 cannot be strengthened, citing the tightness example [5] (p. 14).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the currency is evaluations, with f and ∇f counted separately. Starts are perturbed for fairness, and an unflattering case is reported (pp. 15, 18). But only same-family variants are compared, and the paper explicitly disclaims any comparison with other methods (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: *component ablation with the other step switched off*: the Search is left empty so that Polls can be compared (pp. 2, 14–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use side information only to *skip* poll points whose failure it predicts. In the proof, treat a skipped direction as polled, using the sign of its directional derivative (Def. 4, p. 5; Thm 6, pp. 13–14).
- Poll from a large implicit direction set such as {−1, 0, 1}ⁿ. Complete the chosen direction to a positive spanning set only inside the proof; the completion is never built (pp. 8–9).
- State the accuracy needed from an approximate gradient as sign agreement on its largest components, not as a norm bound (Def. 6 and Thm 4, p. 10).
- Test a component with the rest of the algorithm switched off. Count each oracle (f, ∇f) separately, and perturb the start when a run lands on a stationary point by luck (pp. 14–15).

**Quotes**:
- "We prove that using these less expensive POLL steps does not weaken the known convergence properties of the method, all of which depend only on the POLL step." (p. 1)
- "These results should not be interpreted as saying anything about the efficiency or robustness of GPS algorithms in comparison with other direct search methods." (p. 2)

### S098 · An exact method for fractional goal programming (Journal of Global Optimization 2004, 10.1023/b:jogo.0000035028.40292.36)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: E. Carrizosa (Sevilla), P. Hansen (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is an exact global-optimization note (GERAD G-2000-64, D016 p. 7). It reuses the QCQP branch-and-cut of Audet, Hansen, Jaumard and Savard (ref. [1]; card S010). Received 13 June 2001, revised 13 October 2003, accepted 4 January 2004 (p. 1). **Read**: all 8 pages. Formulas were read from the surrounding prose, because most symbols are lost in the extraction.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Fractional goal programming minimizes the weighted sum of the positive and negative deviations of m linear-fractional objectives from their targets. The feasible set is a bounded polyhedron, and the denominators are positive on it (problem (1), p. 1). The stated applications are finance, resource allocation and education (p. 1). The entry is a gap: all methods proposed so far for the general case are heuristic, either local NLP or implicit vertex enumeration (pp. 1–2). A two-variable example, whose optimum (2, 2) is not a vertex, shows that vertex enumeration can miss the optimum (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Reduce (1) to a QCQP with a linear objective (p. 3):
  - introduce deviation variables d⁺, d⁻ and a variable v_j for each denominator, so that each fraction becomes bilinear constraints (formulation (3));
  - the complementarity d⁺_j d⁻_j = 0 (condition (4)) then holds automatically at an optimum;
  - finite bounds on all variables come from LPs.

  The group's branch-and-cut code qp [1] then applies. A variant, frac, exploits the complementarity: when branching forces d⁻_j ≥ C > 0 it imposes d⁺_j = 0, and conversely (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions**: X is a bounded polyhedron and every denominator is strictly positive on X (p. 1). Variable bounds are obtained by solving LPs (p. 3).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: The main result is a localization theorem (Prop. 1, p. 2): some optimum lies on a face F of X with at least dim F + 1 − m fractional objectives exactly at their targets. The proof has three steps (pp. 2–3):
  1. Freeze the sign pattern of the deviations at an optimal point y as constraints (auxiliary problem (2)).
  2. Apply a Carrizosa–Plastria corollary to get an optimal face of dimension at most m − 1.
  3. Count the fractional objectives at target.

  The result replaces the false *optimum at a vertex* assumption with a weaker statement that is correct.
- **D5 Experiments**: Random instances on [0,1]ⁿ with two-decimal coefficients (p. 4). They are generated so that each target is attainable on its own and the positivity assumption holds, and instances with optimal value 0 are discarded. The grid is m ∈ {6, 10, 14} and n ∈ {5, 10, 15}, with 30 instances per cell. The mean and standard deviation of CPU seconds are reported (C++ with CPLEX 7.5 on a Sun Blade 100) (p. 4). Three factors are crossed (p. 4; Tables 1–2, p. 5):
  - constraint (4) added or not;
  - a simulated heuristic warm start or not. The simulation runs the method once to get z*, then again only to prove optimality;
  - qp versus frac.

  Findings: the warm start cuts the time for m = 10 and 14; constraint (4) slightly slows warm-started runs; time grows with m and falls with n; the variance is high (pp. 4, 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A computational note offering, by the title's claim, an exact method where only heuristics existed (pp. 1–2). It is tested with up to 14 fractional objectives (p. 1). Remarks sketch four extensions (p. 6):
  - m = 1 reduces to two LPs;
  - convex piecewise deviation penalties;
  - quadratic numerators or denominators, as in chance-constrained goal programming;
  - posynomial or signomial ratios, only in principle, because the number of quadratic terms may grow.

  No limitation is stated beyond the high variance (p. 6). The heuristics from the literature are not run as baselines (pp. 4–6).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the exact method serves as certifier of a heuristic's solution, and the simulated warm-start experiment measures the cost of certification (p. 4).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: a minimal two-variable counterexample to a shortcut used by the heuristic literature (optimum at a vertex), followed by the correct, weaker localization result (p. 2). The target is a solution-method assumption, not a convergence theorem.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: a factorial experiment on random instances with means and standard deviations. The currency is CPU time, and there is no external baseline (pp. 4–6).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: the group's qp code is reused, slightly modified to exploit complementarity (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Linearize a sum of ratio-deviation terms: introduce each denominator as a variable, split each deviation into d⁺ and d⁻, and obtain a linear objective with bilinear constraints (p. 3).
- Derive a branching rule from implied complementarity: when branching forces one member of a complementary pair above zero, fix the other at zero (p. 3).
- Simulate a good heuristic in experiments by re-running the exact method with the known optimum as incumbent. The difference measures the cost of proving optimality (p. 4).
- Generate random instances with a planted, separately attainable target for each objective, and discard trivial instances with optimal value 0 (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "However, only heuristic procedures are suggested in the literature on the field." (p. 1)
- "Given a solution produced by a heuristic method, the algorithm can be used to either confirm its global optimality or to show that it is not optimal by finding the true global solution." (p. 4)

### S108 · Quatre petits octogones (Groupe d'études et de recherche en analyse des décisions 2005, no DOI/arXiv; text read: MATAPLI no. 80, 2006)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. Hansen, F. Messine (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is a French expository account, written for the SMAI bulletin *MATAPLI*, of the extremal-octagon side line (SKILL.md trajectory, 2002–2013). It summarizes (pp. 9–10, 17, 20):
- three published or submitted papers: the largest small octagon [7] (= S037), the longest-perimeter octagon [4], and the octagon of Vincze's wife [6];
- the unpublished equilateral-area result;
- the submitted ranking paper [5], which matches the title of card S104.

The width problems left open here (pp. 18–19) match the titles of later papers in the corpus (S117, S142). **Read**: all 21 pages. Accents are displaced in the extraction, and figures are reduced to dots and captions.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Four questions about small (unit-diameter) n-gons: maximum area and maximum perimeter, each for general and for equilateral polygons (p. 2). The known results:
  - odd n was solved by Reinhardt (p. 2);
  - n = 4 was solved analytically (p. 3);
  - maximum perimeter for even n that is not a power of 2 was solved by Reuleaux-polygon constructions that reach Reinhardt's bound 2n sin(π/2n) (pp. 5–6);
  - maximum area for n = 6 was solved by Graham (p. 7).

  So all four first open cases are octagons (pp. 7, 9). The stated aim is to show how efficient the authors' global optimization algorithms are on long-open problems of Euclidean geometry (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Geometric reasoning reduces each problem to a finite set of diameter-graph configurations (31 for octagons, p. 10), and global optimization settles each configuration.
  - *Two solvers, chosen by problem*: the RLT branch-and-cut for QCQP [3], used for the area and the equilateral perimeter (pp. 12, 15), and Messine's interval branch-and-bound IBBA, with admissible-simplex bounds and constraint propagation, used for the perimeter (p. 14).
  - *The branch-and-cut* (p. 12) replaces products by new variables with linear constraints. It branches so as to minimize the worst-case local error, rather than splitting at the midpoint. Cuts are reused across nodes, and bounds are tightened before the search starts.
  - *The equilateral perimeter*: first prove that the four main diagonals equal 1, then solve a small QP in 45 s with error below 1e−7 (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions**: The polygons are convex with unit diameter (p. 2). Closed-form solutions are obtained with Maple under an assumed symmetry axis, and the text says so each time (pp. 15, 19).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**:
  - *Relaxation-saturation*: configuration 10 is a relaxation of 29. Solve it and observe that the extra constraint is active at the optimum, so the optimum belongs to 29 (p. 14).
  - *Optimality conditions as cuts*: first-order conditions plus the bounds 0.688 ≤ α_i ≤ 0.881, added as constraints, eliminate configuration 10 in 0.12 s (p. 14). Configuration 18 resists this device, because the conditions hold at its optimum; it needs 44 h of interval branch-and-bound (p. 14).
  - *Verified arithmetic*: rounding-controlled interval arithmetic guarantees every digit of the maximal perimeter 3.1211 (p. 15).
  - *Closed forms*: the formulas for regular small polygons give non-monotone area and perimeter sequences (p. 17). They also give another proof of Reinhardt's even-n result, by adding one vertex to the regular (n−1)-gon (pp. 17–18).
- **D5 Experiments**: computational records only:
  - the area of configuration 31 took over 100 hours on a SUN-SPARC 20 in 1997 (p. 12);
  - the perimeter took 3 hours on a 30-PC cluster, then a 12-hour elimination stage, then 44 hours for configuration 18 (p. 14);
  - the equilateral-perimeter QP took 45 s (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Four problems, open since 1922, 1950 or 1975, are solved (p. 18). The octagon of Vincze's wife (1950) is shown suboptimal after 54 years (p. 15). The calendar cost is stated: four more years to eliminate the other 30 area configurations, with acceptance in 2001 (p. 12), and about one more year for the perimeter proof (p. 14). The open problems are listed (pp. 18–19):
  - the decagon for area;
  - the 16-gon for perimeter;
  - the octagon of maximal width. A conjectured numerical solution of width 0.9776 exists, but it is not yet proven to be one of the 31 connected configurations;
  - the equilateral width problem, which is entirely open.

  That the regular polygon maximizes area among equilateral small polygons is verified only for n = 4, 6 and 8 (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: a hypothesis audit applied to the geometry literature (pp. 6–8). Graham omitted the proof of symmetry. Bieri had the hexagon earlier, under an unproven symmetry assumption. Reinhardt's even-n result was hidden in an epilogue. The authors label their own symmetry-assuming analytic solutions as such (pp. 15, 19).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: two exact solvers that come from the coauthors' theses and papers (RLT branch-and-cut [2, 3]; IBBA [17, 18]) are reused as instruments across the four problems (pp. 12, 14, 15, 20).
- New-pattern candidate (already opened in batch s2-03): *extremal-geometry side line: numeric global optimization first, exact algebraic certificate later*. The width octagon was found numerically in 2003 and solved analytically under symmetry with Maple in 2004, and its proof is still missing (pp. 18–19).
- New-pattern candidate: *optimality conditions as cuts* (p. 14; see also S037).
- New-pattern candidate: *close with numbered open problems, each with its best conjecture and what blocks the proof* (pp. 18–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To find which case holds the optimum, solve the relaxed case and check whether the constraint that distinguishes the two cases is active at its optimum (p. 14).
- When elimination by optimality conditions fails, state why (the conditions hold at that case's optimum) and switch to pure bounding (p. 14).
- End with explicit open problems, each with the best known conjecture and what stops the proof (pp. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "Which octagon with unit diameter (or small octagon) has the largest area or the longest perimeter ? Could it be the regular octagon ? Well, no, this is not the case." (p. 1)
- "C’est bien ce qui s’est passe... plus de 10 ans plus tard." (p. 9). Context: in the late 1980s, after a talk by a sceptical Graham at Rutgers, Hansen suggested that global optimization could find the largest small octagon (p. 9).

### S048 · Enumeration of all the extreme equilibria in game theory: bimatrix and polymatrix games (Journal of Optimization Theory and Applications 2006, 10.1007/s10957-006-9070-3)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Belhaïza (PhD student, Polytechnique), P. Hansen (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the middle step of the game-equilibrium line:
- EEE for bimatrix games (*SIAM J. Sci. Comput.* 2001, ref. 3; thesis ch. 4, card b2-01);
- this paper;
- proper refinement (D002, *Automatica* 2012). This paper says refinements are to be pursued in further research (p. 1).

It was submitted in 2003 as GERAD G-2003-63 (D016 p. 8) and appeared in *JOTA* 129(3):349–372, June 2006 (p. 1). **Read**: all 24 pages; references skimmed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A game can have infinitely many equilibria, forming a union of polytopes (maximal Nash subsets), so the goal is to enumerate their vertices, the extreme equilibria (pp. 1, 3). The state-of-the-art algorithm EEE (Audet et al. 2001) handles bimatrix games up to 29×29 and 700×5 (p. 4). It relies on bilinear programming, however, and does not extend to more players (p. 22). For polymatrix games with more than two players, no complete enumeration had been done (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea**: Each player's best response is an LP. The complementary-slackness conditions of these LPs are linearized with binary variables and big-L constants. The equilibria are then exactly the feasible points of a mixed 0–1 LP with any objective (Props 2.2 and 3.2, pp. 6, 10–11). The extreme equilibria are the extreme feasible solutions for each feasible binary vector (Cors 2.1 and 3.1, pp. 6, 12). The algorithm, Eχ-MIP, has three parts (pp. 16–17):
  - a principal tree branches on the binaries, choosing the one whose continuous partner is closest to 0.5;
  - at each node that yields an equilibrium, a secondary tree fixes the binary vector and branches on x_i^q = 0 to reach every vertex;
  - a no-good constraint prevents revisiting a binary combination.

  Preprocessing removes strongly dominated strategies iteratively (§4.1, pp. 13–15). Weakly dominated strategies are kept, because removing them may lose equilibria (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Normal-form games with finite strategy sets. Polymatrix payoffs are sums of pairwise terms (pp. 2, 8). No nondegeneracy assumption is stated for the formulation or the algorithm (pp. 4–18).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**:
  - *Valid big-L from data ranges*: L1 = max a_ij − min a_ij, and similarly for B. For polymatrix games, L_i is the sum of the pairwise ranges. Validity follows from bounding α and A_i·y between the smallest and largest entries (Props 2.1 and 3.1, pp. 5, 10–11). This answers the difficulty, admitted on p. 5, of choosing L.
  - *Completeness of Eχ-MIP* (Thm 5.1, pp. 17–18): principal branching covers every binary combination used by an extreme equilibrium, and secondary branching enumerates the vertices for each fixed combination. The proof is a sketch, and it cites a Proposition 5.2 that does not appear in the paper (p. 17).
  - *Termination of dominance elimination*: at most M² iterations, where M is the total number of pure strategies (Prop. 4.1, p. 15). The proof's own count is M(M+1)/2.
  - Reader's notes: Cor. 2.1 cites Proposition 3.2 where 2.2 is meant, and places the algorithm in Section 6 where it is in Section 5 (p. 6). Constraint (6b) writes u for v (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments** (pp. 20–21): C++ with CPLEX 6.0 on a SPARC Ultra 2. There are 10 random games per cell, with payoffs uniform on [0, 10], solved after removing strongly dominated strategies. The mean and standard deviation of node count, CPU time and number of extreme equilibria are reported (p. 21).
  - Bimatrix games from 5×5 to 14×14 are compared with EEE at densities 0.12, 0.25, 0.5 and 1 (Table 2, p. 20).
  - 3-player polymatrix games run from 3×3×3 to 13×13×13 (Table 3, p. 21).
  - The text mentions only densities 1 and 0.5 (p. 21), and the density parameter is not defined in the pages read.
  - Both algorithms find the same number of equilibria (Table 2, p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The paper gives the first complete enumeration of extreme equilibria for polymatrix games (abstract p. 1; p. 22). Stated limits:
  - Eχ-MIP beats EEE only on low-density games. On larger, denser bimatrix games EEE is faster, increasingly so with size, which the authors attribute to MIP solve times (pp. 21–22).
  - Polymatrix solve time grows exponentially (p. 22).

  Future work: equilibrium refinements (p. 1).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the competitor is the group's own state of the art, on its home ground (bimatrix games), and the paper concedes that it is faster there (pp. 21–22). Identical equilibrium counts serve as a correctness cross-check (p. 20). The currency is CPU time and nodes, not evaluations.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (outside DFO): preprocessing is allowed only when it provably loses nothing. Strong dominance qualifies; weak dominance does not (p. 15). This mirrors Research Taste mark 3 of SKILL.md (*Practical freedom never costs the theory*).
- New-pattern candidate: *data-derived valid big-M* (pp. 5, 10–11). The thesis had flagged the numerical risk of a big-L that is merely assumed large enough (card b2-01).
- New-pattern candidate: *generality over speed, conceded in the conclusion* (p. 22). Compare S029's report of PALMER1.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When you linearize complementarity with binaries, derive each big-M from data ranges and prove it valid by bounding both members of the complementary pair (pp. 5, 10–11).
- To enumerate all vertices of a union of polytopes indexed by binary patterns, combine three pieces: a principal tree over binary patterns, a secondary tree that fixes positive continuous variables to zero, and no-good cuts on visited patterns (pp. 16–17).
- Before comparing the speed of two exact enumerators, check that they find the same number of objects on the same instances (Table 2, p. 20).
- Use only reductions that are provably lossless for the enumeration (strict dominance), and say why the tempting one (weak dominance) is excluded (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "Making sure that the constants L1 and L2 are large enough is often problematic." (p. 5)
- "Compared to the state-of-the-art algorithm EEE (Ref. 3) on bimatrix games, Eχ-MIP finds the same set of equilibria, but suffers from larger solution times." (p. 22)
