# Batch s04 · Charles Audet · 2021–2023 work at the edges of MADS: control, ensembles, polygons, mixed variables, zeroth-order (supplement)

**Batch intent**: These six 2021–2023 papers show the group working away from MADS proper: a DFO/smooth-solver decomposition for optimal control with a discontinuous Mayer cost (S151), a Bayesian-style uncertainty for surrogate ensembles inside the MADS Search (S099), two extremal small-polygon papers with Bingane (S113, S142), a notation framework for meta and categorical variables (S058) and a zeroth-order sign-momentum method with convergence rates (S140). For each paper, record which SKILL.md habits carry over when the mesh and the poll are no longer the setting: the Search/Poll split (Method 1), classifying before solving (Method 2), auditing hypotheses (Method 3) and budget-aware benchmarking (Method 5). Also record where a paper contradicts the skill. The main case is S140's rates and evaluation complexity, against the skill's claim that Audet's work has no complexity-style guarantees.
**Focus dimensions**: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line), D8. These are supplement short cards. The emphasis is on D2 (what is carried over from MADS and what is changed) and D4 (the proof or certification device).
**Material roles**: S151 supplement · S099 supplement · S113 supplement · S142 supplement · S058 supplement · S140 supplement.

Reading note: page numbers are the `[[page N]]` markers of the text files. All six texts are arXiv versions, so pages are arXiv pages, not journal pages: S151 arXiv v1 (1 Dec 2021), S099 arXiv v1 (9 Jul 2021; published in COAP 2022), S113 arXiv v4 (25 May 2022), S142 arXiv v2 (3 Feb 2022), S058 arXiv v1 (2 Apr 2022), S140 arXiv v2 (11 Aug 2023). ### S151 · A derivative-free approach to optimal control problems with piecewise constant Mayer cost function (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv:2112.00413)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P.-Y. Bouchet (first author), L. Bourdin (XLIM, Limoges) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: SKILL.md lists Bouchet among the MSc co-authors and in the thesis-to-paper pipeline of Heuristic 10. The same three authors later wrote the 2024 Math. Program. counterexample note that SKILL.md cites under Method 3. The first author is funded by NSERC Discovery Grant 2020-04448 (p. 1). Reader's inference, not verified: this is an early case of the "structure exploitation" tension and a possible precursor of the 2026 partitioned structure-aware framework listed under Latest. **Read**: all 20 pages, with references skimmed. Axis ticks in the plots are extraction noise, but the Bolza costs printed in the figure legends survived.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem class comes from applications: Bolza optimal control problems whose Mayer cost is piecewise constant. The motivating example is the floor of the distance to a target, as in precision sports (p. 2), and a parking example motivates polygonal plateaus (p. 3). Before any experiment, the paper argues that three standard routes fail, and Section 4 is announced as confirming each failure (p. 3):
  - a Pontryagin principle is unlikely, because optimal trajectories tend to end on plateau boundaries where gradients and generalized gradients are undefined (pp. 2–3);
  - smoothing the Mayer cost leaves gradients that are zero or almost zero, so a smooth solver only improves the Lagrange cost (p. 3);
  - MADS on the full discretization faces far more than the few dozen variables it handles well (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *Variable split*: separate the few "singular" variables y, which affect the discontinuity, from the "smooth" variables z, and solve min_y inf_z (R). NOMAD (Mads with the progressive barrier) optimizes y and Ipopt solves the inner smooth subproblem S(y) (pp. 3–5).
  - *Choice of DFO variables in control*: the DFO variables are the states x_λ for λ in an index set Λ. Λ must contain Ω, the set of times at which the Mayer cost is evaluated, and the final index N. The inner subproblems find minimum-Lagrange-cost trajectory pieces between consecutive fixed states (§3.1, pp. 8–9).
  - *Two-phase evaluation*: phase 1 minimizes the squared violation h_c(y,·) to estimate the minimal infeasibility μ̃_y. Phase 2 minimizes ϕ subject to h_c ≤ μ̃_y. Both numbers go to NOMAD's progressive barrier, instead of +∞ when S(y) is infeasible (§2.3, pp. 7–8; Example 2.10, p. 8).
  - The paper separates this from bilevel optimization: there is no leader–follower conflict, only a partition of the variables (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Dynamics, constraints and Lagrange cost are smooth, and the singularities enter only through a known subset of the states (pp. 2, 4–5).
  - The problem is fully discretized with RK4 and the left rectangle rule (p. 11).
  - Prop. 2.9 assumes that h_c(y,·) attains its minimum for every y (p. 7).
  - All three methods, the hybrid included, run "for fair comparison" on a smoothed floor function exp(−e^{−τ(x−q)}) with τ = 75 (pp. 10–11). The hybrid is therefore never tested on the discontinuous problem itself.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No convergence analysis of the hybrid method was found. The theory is a reformulation equivalence, Prop. 2.4, whose proof "follows directly from Definition 2.1" (p. 6). Four 2-D counterexamples bound its hypotheses (Examples 2.5–2.8, p. 7):
  - S(y) is feasible-defined but not well-defined (FD but not FWD) for every y, or for one of two optimal y;
  - S(y) is FWD only at y*;
  - S(y) is infeasible arbitrarily close to y*.
  The proof of Prop. 2.9 is "left to the reader" (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - Setup: four control problems, all started from the uncontrolled trajectory (pp. 10–16):
    - a harmonic oscillator (T = 60, N = 600);
    - two Zermelo boat problems (T = 40, N = 400);
    - a Lotka–Volterra system (T = 60, N = 600).
  - Methods compared: Ipopt alone (MSO), NOMAD alone on the controls (MDFBBO), and the hybrids M{N} and M{N/2,N}.
  - Metric: the final Bolza cost, e.g. on Z1, 32.20 (MSO), 34.35 (MDFBBO) and 4.88 (hybrid) (Fig. 6, p. 14).
  - Not found: repeated runs, evaluation counts or timings.
  - The Lotka–Volterra failure of M{N} is diagnosed with a map of the infeasibility of Ipopt's answer over x_N (Fig. 9, pp. 16–17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and modelling: the reformulation plus the hybrid method (p. 17). The limits are concrete:
  - the best Λ is not simply the smallest one (§4.4, pp. 15–17);
  - Ipopt can return a locally infeasible solution for a feasible subproblem, which acts as a hidden constraint created by the code (pp. 16–17);
  - the class of problems where hybrids work well is not yet identified (pp. 17–18).

  Future work: make the elements of Λ DFO variables, relax exact state matching with a penalty, exploit the links between states, and use early-stopped SO runs as surrogates (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Free Search, rigid Poll) ⚠ variant: the same principle of applying "each solver to a problem it is designed for" (p. 10), but the split is by variable block (DFO outside on y, a smooth solver inside on z), not by algorithmic step (pp. 3–5, 9–10).
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first) ✅ evidence: an infeasible inner problem returns a quantified violation μ̃_y and a finite objective to the progressive barrier, not +∞ (pp. 7–8). Failures of the inner solver are named as hidden constraints produced by the implementation (p. 16).
- Method 3 (Guarantee ladder, bounded by counterexamples) ✅ evidence, small scale: a 2-D counterexample for each hypothesis of the reformulation proposition (pp. 6–7). There is no convergence ladder for the algorithm itself.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: all methods get the same initialization and the same smoothing, "for fair comparison" (p. 10). But each method is run once, only final values are reported and no budget is accounted for (pp. 11–17).
- Taste warning sign 5 (small-n doctrine) ✅ evidence: NOMAD's "critical value of 50" variables is quoted to justify giving it only the n or 2n state variables (p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: *singular/smooth variable split*: give the DFO solver only the variables that carry the nonsmoothness and pass the rest to a gradient-based inner solver (pp. 3–5, 9).
- new-pattern candidate: *diagnose a failed run by mapping the inner solver's output over the outer variables* (Fig. 9, pp. 16–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When the objective is itself an inner optimization that may be infeasible, use a two-phase solve and return two numbers: the minimal infeasibility and the best objective within that infeasibility. Feed both to a progressive barrier instead of returning +∞ (pp. 7–8).
- (D1) Before running experiments, state the mechanism by which each naive baseline should fail (smoothing plus an NLP solver; DFO on the full problem), then check it (pp. 3, 10).
- (D4) Pair a reformulation proposition with minimal 2-D counterexamples, one per hypothesis (pp. 6–7).

**Quotes**:
- "The main idea is thus to optimize the final state alone via a DFBBO algorithm, while a smooth subproblem, which consists in solving the initial problem with the final state being fixed and with a minimal Lagrange cost, is solved via a SO algorithm." (p. 3)
- "We could set the value +∞, but such a choice would not provide any information to pursue efficiently the numerical resolution of (R)." (p. 8)

### S099 · Quantifying uncertainty with ensembles of surrogates for blackbox optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2022, 10.1007/s10589-022-00381-z; arXiv:2107.04360)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Le Digabel, R. Saltet (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: follows the ensemble-of-surrogates Search of JOGO 2018 (SKILL.md, Method 1 evidence), whose order-error cross-validation metric (OECV) it reuses (pp. 5, 11). Funded by IVADO grant PRF-2019-8079623546 (p. 1). The method runs in NOMAD 4 (p. 16). **Read**: all 36 pages, including Appendices A–B, with references skimmed. The text is arXiv v1 (12 July 2021), not the COAP version.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two efficient kinds of surrogate are "fundamentally separated" (pp. 2–3):
  - ensembles give a deterministic aggregate model, which cannot feed Bayesian acquisition functions;
  - stochastic models such as GPs become costly to train as the sample grows.

  The entry is to derive an uncertainty from the disagreement between the members of the ensemble (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *What uncertainty should measure*: disagreement of trends, not of values. A thought experiment makes the point: f̃² = f̃¹/10 + 20 has the same optima as f̃¹, so their uncertainty should be minimal, and −f̃¹ has opposite optima, so the uncertainty should be maximal. The standard deviation used in [29] gets this wrong (p. 8).
  - *Four pairwise measures*:
    - objective, smooth: σ̂ = ½(1 − cos) of the angle between simplex gradients (eq. 2, p. 9);
    - objective, nonsmooth: the fraction of directions of a positive spanning set along which the two models predict opposite trends, via xor (eq. 3, pp. 9–10);
    - constraints: the sign matters, so the measures are sigm(−c̃ᵖc̃ᵠ) and the xor of the two predicted feasibility results (eqs. 4–5, p. 10).
  - *Aggregation*: pairs are weighted by wᵖwᵠ, with weights from OECV. At least two weights must be positive, so the best N_best = 3 or 4 models are kept. The result is scaled by α = 10·Var of the cached values (eqs. 6–7, pp. 10–13).
  - *Probability substitutes*: there is no distribution, so P, PI and EI are replaced by λ-tuned sigmoids, and γ(t) = e^{−t²/2} replaces φ. The EI substitute is justified by listing the qualitative properties EI should keep (pp. 14–15).
  - *Where it goes*: into the MADS Search. Solve one of Talgorn et al.'s formulations SP1–SP8, project the solution onto the mesh and evaluate it. The Poll is unchanged (Alg. 3, pp. 15–16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - X holds the unrelaxable (bound) constraints and the c_j are relaxable (p. 2).
  - No probabilistic foundation is claimed for the extended aggregate model (p. 14).
  - P(x) assumes the constraints are independent. The authors call this possibly incorrect but "the best available approximation" (p. 14).
  - Many constants come from preliminary tests: the factor 10 in α (p. 11), N_best (p. 13), the λ values (pp. 14–15), and the simplex and positive-spanning-set sizes 0.001 and 0.005 (p. 35).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no new theorem. Convergence is inherited from MADS because every candidate lies on the mesh (p. 15). Reader's note: the MADS recap says the mesh and poll sizes increase after an unsuccessful iteration and decrease after a successful one (p. 7), the reverse of the standard MADS update.
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - Setup: NOMAD 4 with its 18 default models (pp. 16–26).
  - Competitors: no search, quadratic-model search, kriging (only where affordable), DFN and SHEBO.
  - Problems and budgets:
    - 7 analytical problems, 15 instances, 1200(n+1) evaluations (p. 17);
    - aircraft range, 10 LHS starts, 1000(n+1) (p. 20);
    - simplified wing, 600(n+1) (p. 23);
    - solar1, 15 problem seeds with 10 replications, 800(n+1) (pp. 24–25);
    - styrene, 12 starts, 600(n+1) (p. 26).
  - Seeds: four NOMAD seeds, except one on solar1 (pp. 16, 25).
  - Outputs: data profiles at several tolerances τ, time data profiles (p. 23), and a table of medians for SHEBO, which takes no starting point (pp. 26–28).
  - Losses are reported: quad search wins on the analytical problems at τ = 10⁻⁷ and on solar1 (pp. 18–19, 25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic: uncertainty measures plus a Bayesian-optimization-style Search for MADS. The paper claims better solutions than no search and quad search on 3 of the 4 expensive problems, at less cost than kriging (p. 28). Stated limits (p. 28):
  - the component models must stay moderately cheap;
  - there is no gain on analytical problems;
  - solar1, with its mostly linear constraints, favours quadratic models.

  Future work: mix uncertainty types between objective and constraints, try other weightings, study the simplex and spanning-set sizes, update λ adaptively, localize α, and understand the formulations, which "were chosen on a purely empirical basis" (pp. 28–29).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: all the ensemble and Bayesian machinery lives in the Search, candidates are projected onto the mesh, and convergence is inherited (pp. 6–7, 15–16).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: budgets proportional to n+1, data profiles over tolerances, several seeds, solvers from other families (DFN, SHEBO), time data profiles to price model training (p. 23), and the competitor's home ground conceded (quad search on analytical problems and solar1, pp. 18–19, 25). ⚠ variant: formulations and λ are selected on the same problems on which they are then compared (pp. 17–20). The later problems reuse the formulations selected on aircraft range (pp. 23, 25, 26).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: solar1 (with its own seed and replication parameters) and styrene come from the bbopt repositories, next to the two MDO problems (pp. 19–26).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (weak): the constraint uncertainty is built on sign and feasibility, not on trend (p. 10). To run SHEBO, violated constraints are recast as hidden constraints (p. 16).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: built on NOMAD 4 and its default model library (p. 16).
- new-pattern candidate: *design a metric with invariance thought experiments* before writing the formula: a monotone rescaling should leave it unchanged, and an opposite model should maximize it (p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate: *compare each model class with its own best configuration*, not inside the configuration tuned for your own method (pp. 20–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) When a competitor is costly per iteration, add time data profiles next to the evaluation-based data profiles (p. 23).
- (D5) When a solver cannot follow the common protocol (SHEBO takes no starting point), report a separate table: the median best value, the time and the number of evaluations to reach it, and the total time (pp. 26–28).
- (D2) When replacing a Gaussian quantity with a non-probabilistic substitute, list the qualitative properties of the original (monotone in ŷ; limits as σ̂ → 0 or ∞) and design the replacement to keep them (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "In this case the uncertainty shall be minimum since using either model will yield the same candidate points." (p. 8)
- "The resulting algorithm benefits from the convergence results of MADS since all the candidate points lie on the mesh" (p. 15)

### S113 · Tight bounds on the maximal perimeter of convex equilateral small polygons (Archiv der Mathematik 119(3):325–336, 2022, 10.1007/s00013-022-01745-x; arXiv:2105.10618)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: C. Bingane (first author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: continues the extremal small-polygon line that SKILL.md's Research Trajectory dates "2002–2013 (parallel)". Audet's earlier results are the anchors: the optimal 8-gon (Audet, Hansen, Messine 2007) and the optimal equilateral 8-gon (Audet, Hansen, Messine, Perron 2004), the latter found by the authors, who "used both geometrical arguments and methods of global optimization" (pp. 1–2, 4). The line therefore runs at least to 2022. **Read**: all 10 pages (arXiv v4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The maximal perimeter of a convex equilateral small n-gon (diameter 1) is open for n = 2^s, s ≥ 4 (p. 1). The entry is the best known construction: Mossinghoff's 2008 family is within O(1/n⁴) of the upper bound L_n = 2n sin(π/2n) (p. 2). The target is a family with a smaller constant (Theorem 1, p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and construction**: The polygon A_n is specified by its diameter graph: an axis of symmetry v0–v_{n/2}, a half-path of length 3n/8 − 1, and pendant edges. The coordinates are then closed-form sums of sines and cosines of one angle t, and the equilateral condition becomes one scalar equation in t (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The vertices lie in the half-plane y ≥ 0, in counterclockwise order, with v0 at the origin. Convexity is imposed through 2×2 determinants σ_i ≥ 0 (p. 5). The results cover n = 2^s with s ≥ 4 only (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - *Asymptotics*: an expansion of the root t0(n), stated as what "An asymptotic analysis produces", with the derivation not shown (p. 6), gives L_n − L(A_n) = π⁴/n⁴ + O(1/n⁵) and L(A_n) − L(M_n) = 2π⁴/n⁴ + O(1/n⁵) (p. 6; Theorem 1, p. 2).
  - *Feasibility checks*: smallness and convexity are verified by expanding the critical distance (< 1) and the critical determinant (> 0) (p. 6).
  - *Self-audit*: for n = 32 and 64, other diameter graphs give longer perimeters (Props. 1–2, pp. 7–8), so A_n is not optimal. Reader's notes: the paper does not say how these graphs were found, and it states that the smallness and convexity of Z32 and Z64 can be verified without showing the verification (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: Numbers only, no experiments. Table 1 gives L(R_n), L(M_n), L(A_n) and L_n for n = 16 to 256, and the fraction of the gap to the upper bound that A_n closes, which tends to 2/3 (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Theoretical: tighter lower bounds for every n = 2^s with s ≥ 4, which prove Mossinghoff's hexadecagon suboptimal (p. 1).
  - Code: OPTIGON, with Julia and MATLAB functions that return the vertex coordinates (p. 7).
  - Limits: the authors expect that another family might improve Theorem 1 (p. 8), and exact optima for s ≥ 4 "appear to be presently out of reach" (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the rigor is a sandwich (the upper bound L_n, a constructive lower bound, and the explicit order and constant of the gap) rather than a convergence ladder. The self-audit habit is there: the paper shows that its own family A_n is not optimal for n = 32 and 64 (pp. 2, 6–8).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the released artifact is a public code package with the coordinates of every constructed polygon (OPTIGON, p. 7), not a blackbox benchmark.
- new-pattern candidate: *graph-parameterized construction*: encode a candidate extremal object by a combinatorial skeleton (diameter graph plus symmetry) that reduces it to one or two scalar equations, then expand asymptotically (pp. 5–6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Report a new bound together with the fraction of the remaining gap it closes and the limit of that fraction as n grows (Table 1, p. 7).
- (D4) After proving that a family is asymptotically good, test whether it is optimal at small n by exhibiting better instances (pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "In particular, for the first open case n = 16, our result proves that Mossinghoff’s equilateral hexadecagon is suboptimal." (p. 1)
- "This suggests that it is possible that another family of convex equilateral small polygons might produce an improvement to Theorem 1." (p. 8)

### S142 · The equilateral small octagon of maximal width (Mathematics of Computation 91(336):2027–2040, 2022, 10.1090/mcom/3733; arXiv:2110.00036)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: C. Bingane (first author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the same extremal-polygon line as S113; the two papers cite each other (S113 p. 10; S142 p. 12). It reuses Audet's width–perimeter inequality (Audet, Hansen, Messine 2009; Theorem 4, p. 6) and the ε-uniqueness notion of the 2004 equilateral-octagon paper (p. 9). **Read**: all 13 pages (arXiv v2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The maximal width of an equilateral small n-gon is unknown for n = 2^s, s ≥ 3. The paper settles the first open case, n = 8, and adds an asymptotic family for s ≥ 4 (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: A hybrid proof in three steps.
  - (i) *Candidate*: construct F8 from a guessed height graph (a 5-cycle with three pendant edges, and axial symmetry) that reduces to three equations. The width of F8 is 0.9537763006…, and t0 = 2 sin α0 is the unique positive root of t⁵ − 6t³ + 3t² + 10t − 7 = 0 (Prop. 1, pp. 5–6).
  - (ii) *Analytic pruning*:
    - the width–perimeter inequality bounds the side length: 0.3794 < c < 0.3870 (p. 6);
    - Lemmas 1–2 and Prop. 2 restrict which vertex can be furthest from each side, leaving exactly two possible height graphs (Corollary 1, p. 7);
    - Ptolemy's inequality tightens the bounds further (Prop. 3, pp. 7–8).
  - (iii) *Global solver*: each remaining configuration goes to the global MINLP solver Couenne, through AMPL on NEOS, with those bounds as constraints (Problem (4), pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: v0 is at the origin, the polygon lies in the upper half-plane, and vertices are in counterclockwise order (p. 4). The certificate is only as rigorous as Couenne's numerical tolerances. The theorem says so: w*₈ = 0.9537763006… + ε with 0 ≤ ε ≤ 10⁻⁶ (p. 1), and the text adds "Within the limit of the numerical computations" (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - *Contradiction by distance*: two disjoint diagonals of length ≥ w would force a distance > 1.0263 (Lemma 2, p. 7).
  - *Case reduction* to two configurations (p. 7).
  - *Ptolemy's inequality* (pp. 7–8).
  - *Solver certificates* (p. 9):
    - configuration a is infeasible (0.32 s);
    - configuration b has optimum 0.95377630014 (3.18 s);
    - no width exceeds 0.953777 (2.05 s).
  - *ε-uniqueness*: add a constraint excluding a ball of radius 1.5×10⁻⁴ around F8 and obtain infeasibility (2.80 s; Prop. 5, p. 9).
  - *Asymptotic family* G_n: the same expansion method as S113, giving W_n − W(G_n) = 2π⁴/(3n⁴) + O(1/n⁶) (pp. 10–11).

  Reader's note: Theorem 1 says the value is "only attained" by F8 (p. 1). The proof gives ε*-uniqueness with ε* = 1.5×10⁻⁴ (p. 9), which is also what the conclusion states (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: The solver runs are proof steps, with their times (p. 9). Table 1 compares the widths of R_n, F_n and G_n with the bound W_n for n = 16 to 256 (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Theoretical: the first open equilateral width case, solved to 10⁻⁶ with near-uniqueness, plus an asymptotic family for s ≥ 4.
  - Code: the AMPL files and OPTIGON on GitHub (pp. 9, 11).
  - Limits: 10⁻⁶ precision, and uniqueness only outside a ball of radius 1.5×10⁻⁴ (p. 11).
  - No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the tolerance is part of the theorem statement (ε ≤ 10⁻⁶), and the claim is backed by an explicit certificate (p. 1, p. 9). The device is exhaustive case reduction plus a global solver, not counterexamples.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the AMPL models are released, so the certificate can be rerun (p. 9).
- Research Trajectory ✅ evidence: global optimization, the toolkit of Audet's PhD period (SKILL.md, 1997–2002) and of the 2004 octagon paper (S113 p. 2), is still in use in 2022, now through an off-the-shelf solver (p. 9).
- new-pattern candidate: *analytic pruning before a global-solver certificate*: derive variable bounds and eliminate configurations by hand until each remaining case is a small MINLP that a global solver closes in seconds (pp. 6–9).
- new-pattern candidate: *ε-uniqueness by an exclusion constraint*: prove near-uniqueness by adding ‖z − z*‖ ≥ ε to the model and certifying infeasibility (p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3) Put the solver tolerance in the theorem statement (value + ε, 0 ≤ ε ≤ 10⁻⁶) instead of hiding it in the numerics (p. 1).
- (D2) Combine a known inequality between two functionals (width/perimeter ≤ (1/2n)cot(π/2n)) with the best known value of one of them to bound the variables of the other problem (p. 6).
- (D4) Certify optimality by raising the lower bound on the objective slightly (w ≥ 0.953777) and showing that the problem becomes infeasible (Prop. 4, p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "This paper solves the first open case and finds the optimal equilateral small octagon." (p. 1)
- "Within the limit of the numerical computations, this corresponds to the octagon F8." (p. 9)

### S058 · A general mathematical framework for constrained mixed-variable blackbox optimization problems with meta and categorical variables (Operations Research Forum 2023, 10.1007/s43069-022-00180-6; arXiv:2204.00886)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: E. Hallé-Hannan, S. Le Digabel (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: this is the "categorical framework 2023" of SKILL.md Heuristic 10 (Hallé-Hannan → categorical framework 2023 and CatMADS). Funding: an NSERC Alliance grant with Huawei-Canada, plus IVADO and Hydro-Québec MSc scholarships (p. 1). The paper formalizes a lineage of Audet papers: mixed-variable GPS with Dennis (2001), filter GPS (2007), MADS for mixed variables (2009) and the granular mesh GMesh (2019) (pp. 2–3). **Read**: all 30 pages, with references skimmed (arXiv v1). Figures 1–5 are not in the text beyond their captions.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Mixed-variable blackbox problems are hard mainly because of two kinds of variables (p. 2):
  - variables that switch other variables and constraints on or off;
  - categorical variables, which have no metric and cannot be relaxed the way integers can.

  Earlier frameworks put categorical and integer variables together in one "discrete" component, and counted meta variables among the categorical ones (pp. 3, 10). A running example threads through the paper: hyperparameter optimization of an MLP, where the number of layers l decides the unit counts and the optimizer o decides its own continuous hyperparameters (pp. 5–6, 16–18, 21).
- **D2 Core idea**: A notation, not an algorithm.
  - *Point partition*: x = (x^m, x^q, x^s), with meta, categorical (nominal or ordinal) and standard (integer or continuous) components (Def. 1, p. 7).
  - *Types and roles*: every variable has a type and a role (meta, decreed or global), and every constraint a role (decreed or global) (§3.1.2, pp. 8–9). A classification tree sorts the variables (Fig. 3, pp. 10–11).
  - *Domain*: written with sets parameterized by the meta component, X^t(x^m), whose dimension n^t(x^m) varies (Defs. 2–3, pp. 11–12), and equivalently as a union of layers (eq. 6, p. 13).
  - *Feasible set*: built from the global constraints and the decreed constraints that are acting (Defs. 4–5, p. 15).
  - *Two solution strategies* are written in this notation:
    - standard subproblems, with the meta and categorical parts fixed, explored through user-defined neighborhood mappings built from rules (Def. 7, p. 21) in a loop of global search then poll (Alg. 1, p. 23);
    - an auxiliary Bayesian-optimization problem with encoders, an auxiliary domain and a pre-image constraint (Defs. 8–9, eq. 32, pp. 24–25). Its mixed kernel compares categorical and standard parts only when the meta components coincide (eq. 41, p. 27).
  - *Generalization*: the "dimensional variables" of Lucidi et al. become meta variables, which may be of any type and need not change the dimension (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Meta variables cannot be decreed by other meta variables. The paper justifies this by rarity in practice and lighter notation (footnote 1, p. 8).
  - Meta variables are not encoded in the Bayesian-optimization strategy (p. 24).
  - The GP is taken to be noise-free with zero mean (p. 25).
  - Constraints are handled inside the subproblem solver, e.g. MADS with the progressive barrier (p. 23).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There are no theorems or proofs, only definitions, and no convergence statement for Alg. 1 was found. Reader's note: some cross-references in the arXiv v1 text point to items that do not exist, e.g. "Definition 27" (p. 23) and "Definition 14" (pp. 18, 28).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**: None. The authors say no computational experiments are presented, and they cite the surrogate management framework as a precedent for a framework paper without experiments (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: a modelling and notation layer that makes meta and categorical variables explicit, plus a demonstration that direct search and Bayesian optimization both fit it (pp. 27–28).
  - Limits: no experiments and no theory.
  - Future work: computational studies, and developing Bayesian optimization "with aim of bridging the communities in optimization and machine learning" (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Constraint semantics first) ⚠ variant: the same classify-before-solving move, applied to variables: a type and a role for every variable, global versus decreed for every constraint, and a decision tree (pp. 7–11, 15). The question asked of each variable is whether it switches other variables or constraints on or off, not whether a constraint is relaxable.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence, for the architecture only: Alg. 1 keeps a flexible global search (random, LHS, Nelder–Mead or GP-based) before a poll over user-defined neighborhoods. The stated reason is that a poll alone misses good points (pp. 22–23). No convergence is claimed.
- Heuristic 10 (thesis-to-paper pipeline) ✅ evidence: this is the "Hallé-Hannan → categorical framework 2023" entry, and the MSc scholarships are acknowledged (p. 1).
- Method 5: not applicable (no experiments, p. 2).

### S140 · Sequential stochastic blackbox optimization with zeroth-order gradient estimators (AIMS Mathematics 8(11):25922–25956, 2023, 10.3934/math.20231321; arXiv:2305.19450)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Bigeon, R. Couderc, M. Kokkolaras (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: part of the noisy-blackbox line that follows StoMADS in SKILL.md; the stochastic MADS versions are cited as related direct-search work (p. 2). Here the group leaves direct search for zeroth-order gradient estimation. SKILL.md lists Kokkolaras as the engineering-design peer. **Read**:
- Read:
  - pp. 1–8 in full: abstract, introduction, contributions, Assumption 1, Lemma 2.1, Algorithms 1–2;
  - the variance lemmas with their proofs (Lemmas 4.2–4.4, pp. 9–11);
  - the statements of Prop. 4.1, Theorem 4.9, Corollary 4.10 and Theorem 4.11 (pp. 9, 14–15);
  - §4.1.6 and Table 2 (pp. 17–18);
  - Assumptions 2–3 and the statements of Lemmas 4.13–4.15 (pp. 18–20);
  - Theorem 4.17 with the end of its proof and the remarks (pp. 21–24);
  - §§5–6 (pp. 24–27) and Appendix C (p. 30).
- Not read:
  - the bias results, Lemmas 4.6–4.8, and Prop. 4.5 (pp. 11–14);

  - Appendices B and D (Appendix A, the notation list, was skimmed).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is min E_ξ[F(x, ξ)] for a noisy blackbox whose noise distribution is unknown (p. 1). The entry is a gap in earlier work. Gaussian smoothing with a large β convexifies f, but only [43] has used this property, and [43] has several shortcomings (pp. 2–3):
  - its sequence of β does not tend to 0;
  - its inner iteration counts are fixed in advance;
  - it has no convergence proof or rate;
  - it is tested only on analytical problems.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *Outer loop*: SSO solves a sequence of smoothed problems min f^{β_i}, with β_i = β0/(i+1)² → 0 (Alg. 2, p. 7).
  - *Inner solver*: each subproblem is solved by a zeroth-order Signum, which steps along the sign of a momentum of Gaussian finite-difference estimates (Alg. 1, pp. 6–7).
  - *Change from Signum*: the momentum step s2 goes to 0 instead of staying constant. The momentum norm then goes to 0 itself and serves as the stopping test ‖m‖ ≤ Lβ_i/(4β0), after at least M iterations (Alg. 1, pp. 6–7).
  - *Between subproblems*: step sizes are reset and shrunk, and the momentum carries over. This is justified by Lemma 2.1.3: the gradients of neighbouring smoothed functions differ by an amount proportional to the gap between their β values (pp. 5–6).
  - *Search step*: an optional, MADS-inspired step (restart from the best cached point, with a finite budget N) precedes the local step, which alone carries convergence (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Assumption 1: F(·, ξ) is integrable and L0-Lipschitz for every ξ, with no differentiability assumed (p. 4).
  - The SSO rates add Assumption 2, a L1-Lipschitz gradient, used "only" so that L1(f^β) does not grow as β → 0 (p. 18).
  - They also add Assumption 3: for i ≥ I, f^{β_i} is convex on a ball around a local minimizer and each subproblem starts inside that ball (pp. 18–19). Reader's note: this constrains the iterates as much as the function f.
  - Bound constraints appear only in the experiments, handled by projection (p. 24).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - *Skeleton*: taken from Bernstein et al. [6]. Prop. 4.1 splits E‖∇f^β‖₁ into a descent term, a bias term and a variance term (p. 9).
  - *Variance without growing mini-batches* (pp. 9–11):
    - the cross term vanishes by conditioning on the σ-field and using unbiasedness (Lemma 4.2, pp. 9–10);
    - E‖g̃‖² ≤ L0²(n+4)² (Lemma 4.3, p. 10);
    - a split-sum bound gives 9s2⁰/k^{α2} (Lemma 4.4, pp. 10–11).
  - *Results*:
    - ZO-Signum, nonconvex case: O(n^{3/2} ln K / K^{1/4}) in expectation at a randomly chosen iterate (Theorem 4.9 and Cor. 4.10, pp. 14–15; the extracted formula is garbled and this is my reading);
    - ZO-Signum, convex case: O(n^{3/2}/K^{1/3}) (Theorem 4.11, p. 15);
    - SSO: O(n⁶L^{7/2}/ε^{7/2}) evaluations to reach ‖∇f^{β_i*}(x^{i*,0})‖ ≤ ε, or O(n^{9/2}L^{7/2}/ε^{7/2}) if every f^{β_i} is convex (Theorem 4.17, pp. 21–22).
  - *Honest comparison*: Table 2 shows ZO-Signum worse by O(n^{3/4}√σ) than ZO-SGD and by O(√n) than ZO-adaMM. The authors attribute this to their milder assumption (p. 17).
  - Reader's notes:
    - the abstract promises "a convergence rate to an ϵ-optimal point" (p. 1), but Theorem 4.17 bounds a gradient norm of the smoothed function (p. 22);
    - the proof goes through "a probabilistic upper bound" on K_i (p. 22), while the statement carries no probability qualifier;
    - the theorem requires K_i ≥ (i+1)⁶, which the authors say is replaced by K_i ≥ M in practice (pp. 21, 23).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - SOLAR instance 2 (pp. 24–25):
    - Problem: turned into a feasibility problem over 12 variables with the 2 integers fixed, minimizing the expected squared constraint violation.
    - Budget and seeds: 1000 evaluations, mean of 5 seeds.
    - Competitors: NOMAD 3.9.1, CMA-ES (pymoo defaults) and ZO-adaMM (β and learning rate changed).
    - Result: SSO performs similarly to NOMAD and CMA-ES.
  - Blackbox adversarial attacks on Cifar10 (100 images, 5000 queries) and ImageNet (Tables 4–5, p. 26):
    - Cifar10: SSO succeeds on 100%, against 79–98% for ZO-adaMM and 99% for CMA-ES.
    - ImageNet: 73% for SSO against 59% for ZO-adaMM.
  - Hyperparameters are set per problem (Table 6, p. 30). Reader's note: they use step-size exponents 1/2 and 1/4, not the 3/4 and 1/2 assumed in Cor. 4.10 and Lemma 4.15 (pp. 15, 20, 30).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: an algorithm, its analysis, and experiments on a small problem (n ≈ 10) and a large one (ImageNet, n = 3×299×299) (pp. 26–27). The single-run ε rate is claimed to be the first of its kind "to the best of our knowledge" (pp. 23, 27).
  - Limits:
    - a stronger dependence on the dimension than in smooth-case analyses (p. 15);
    - the sign operator discards the magnitude of the gradient (p. 17);
    - a fixed term can dominate the bound when the convex region is hard to reach (p. 23).
  - Future work: constrained stochastic problems (p. 27).

**Method links**:
