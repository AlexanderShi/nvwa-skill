# Paper cards · batch c05 (core)

Batch intent: Trace how Vicente built the complexity-plus-probability programme (2013–2015) and how the same "match the single-objective gradient benchmark" logic moved into multiobjective gradient methods (2019) and then into stochastic multiobjective learning for ML fairness (2022). Four questions: (a) Where exactly does the evaluation count O(n²ε⁻²) of Method 2 come from, and which device makes progress countable? (c) In the two multiobjective papers, what is kept from the single-objective method and what is redefined (Method 4)? (d) How are experiments designed and how fair are the comparisons (Method 5)?

### S023 · Worst case complexity of direct search (EURO Journal on Computational Optimization 2013, DOI 10.1007/s13675-012-0003-7)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (single author) · **Position in trajectory**: The first worst-case complexity result for directional direct search. It was written while Vicente was visiting the Courant Institute (footnote, p. 1), and it thanks Overton and Gürbüzbalaban for discussions (p. 9). Later papers in this batch cite it as the deterministic yardstick: S019 [28] and S015 [17]. **Read**: full text (11 pages including references).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an analogy with a known bound. Steepest descent needs at most O(ε⁻²) iterations (Nesterov, p. 2). Directional direct search with positive spanning sets is "of descent type", so it is "not unreasonable to expect" the same bound (p. 2). The enabling fact is that one direction of a positive spanning set always makes an acute angle with −∇f (p. 2). The problem is stated in Nesterov's problem-class format: *Model* (f ∈ C¹_ν, bounded below), *Oracle* (zero order), *ε-solution* (f(x) ≤ f(x₀), ‖∇f‖ ≤ ε) (p. 2). The practical motivation: direct search is slow when it relies only on polling, but it parallelizes naturally, and the n² factor matters "for computational budgetary considerations" (p. 2–3).
  - Transferable: Put your result in Nesterov's Model / Oracle / ε-solution box (p. 2). One sentence then turns an algorithm bound into an upper complexity bound for a problem class.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: No new algorithm. Algorithm 2.1 is the GPS/GSS directional framework from the literature: an optional search step, an opportunistic poll step over a positive spanning set D_k, and a step-size update (p. 3–4). The one design commitment is to accept new points only under *sufficient* decrease, f < f(x_k) − ρ(α_k) with ρ(t) = c tᵖ (p. 3–4), rather than simple decrease on integer or rational lattices (p. 4). The search step is assumed to cost at most O(n) evaluations, so it does not spoil the count (p. 5).
  - Transferable: When the theory needs one property (countable decrease), take the existing framework and fix only that choice. Say explicitly which variant of the framework the result does *not* cover (lattice-based simple decrease, p. 4, p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is bounded below on L(x₀), and ∇f is Lipschitz with constant ν on an open set containing L(x₀) (Theorem 3.1, p. 5). Assumption 2.1 bounds the cosine measure from below, cm(D_k) ≥ cm_min, and the direction norms to [d_min, d_max] (p. 5). Two admitted simplifications. First, the possible dependence of ν on n "will be ignored", with the justification that global Lipschitz constants appear in all such bounds and may depend exponentially on n (p. 7). Second, a bound stated in α_k, which is measurable, would still need ν, so it is not practical either (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The proof has four steps.
  1. Theorem 2.1, imported from Dolan–Lewis–Torczon and Kolda–Lewis–Torczon: at an unsuccessful iteration, ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≤ cm(D_k)⁻¹(ν/2 · α_k max‖d‖ + ρ(α_k)/(α_k min‖d‖)) (p. 4).
  2. Successful iterations (Theorem 3.1). While ‖∇f‖ > ε, every unsuccessful iteration has α_k ≥ L₁ ε^{1/min(p−1,1)}. Backtracking from any successful iteration to the previous unsuccessful one gives α_k ≥ β₁L₁ε^{…} for all k (eq. (4)). Each success decreases f by c α_kᵖ, and summing gives |S| ≤ (f(x_{k0}) − f_low)/(cβ₁ᵖL₁ᵖ) · ε^{−p/min(p−1,1)} (p. 5–6).
  3. Unsuccessful iterations (Theorem 3.2). Use the product α_{j1} ≤ α_{k0} γ^{|S|} β₂^{|U|}, take logarithms, and apply the lower bound on α (p. 6). Iterations before the first unsuccessful one are bounded separately (p. 7).
  4. Corollary 3.1 gives O(ε^{−p/min(p−1,1)}) iterations, best at p = 2, i.e. O(ε⁻²) (p. 7). Corollary 3.2 then uses cm = 1/√n for D = [I −I] and at most O(n) evaluations per iteration to get O(n²ε⁻²) evaluations (p. 7–8). One factor n comes from cm⁻² and the other from the poll size.

  Sharpness comes from a reduction. For n = 1 and p = 2, direct search with {−1, 1} *is* steepest descent with a Goldstein–Armijo line search on a monotone function. The Cartis–Gould–Toint lower-bound example therefore applies (p. 8).
  - Transferable: Count successes by (i) a lower bound on the step size while not ε-stationary and (ii) telescoping sufficient decrease. Count failures by the product of the expansion and contraction factors, taking logarithms (p. 5–6).
  - Transferable: Choose the free exponent of a design parameter (here p in ρ = c tᵖ) by minimizing the ε-power of the bound, then check the choice against earlier numerics (p = 2 "seems to corroborate previous numerical experience", p. 7).
  - Transferable: Get a tightness statement cheaply by showing that your method reduces, in one dimension, to a method with a known lower-bound example (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The only link to numerics is the remark that ρ = c tᵖ with p ≠ 2 performed worse in [18] (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself among the O(ε⁻²) results of Nesterov (steepest descent), Gratton–Toint and coauthors (trust region) and Cartis–Gould–Toint (adaptive cubic regularization with Cauchy decrease). It mentions the O(ε^{−3/2}) second-order methods (p. 8). It concedes openly that the finite-difference ARC bound of Cartis–Gould–Toint [5] is better: the O(n²ε⁻²) bound for direct search is worse in the power of ε (p. 9). It also notes that [5] had already compared its own bound with this result (p. 9). The framework is credited to Audet–Dennis (GPS), Kolda–Lewis–Torczon (GSS) and Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente's book (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A short note of about 9 pages of text. The main claim is the first sentence of the abstract and is repeated on p. 2. There is one intuition sentence per device (sufficient decrease, p. 4). Each constant's dependencies are listed after the corollaries ("The constant in O(·) depends only on", p. 7–8). Hedging is light but present ("not unreasonable to expect", p. 2; "seems to corroborate", p. 7). The final remarks are two paragraphs of extensions and one honest comparison (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A theoretical contribution: iteration and evaluation bounds with explicit n-dependence for a whole class of methods. The limits are specific (p. 8–9):
  - smooth f only; nonsmoothness "poses several difficulties" and is deferred to Garmanjani–Vicente [9];
  - simple-decrease lattice methods need an extra decrease-rate assumption;
  - the ε-power is worse than finite-difference ARC;
  - the practical α-based stopping bound still depends on ν.

  Future direction: bound and linear constraints, with ∇f replaced by the stationarity measure χ(x_k) (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (the origin). Steps 1–4 of Method 2 follow this paper: sufficient decrease, successes counted through the step-size lower bound, failures counted through α-contractions, conversion to evaluations through poll size and cosine measure, then comparison with steepest descent (p. 5–8). The n² decomposition (cm⁻² × poll size) is explicit in Corollary 3.2 (p. 8).
- Method 2, step 5 ✅ partial. The ε-order is shown tight through the n = 1 reduction (p. 8). The n-order is *not* discussed here; it was addressed later (Dodangeh–Vicente–Zhang, per SKILL.md).
- Method 2, SKILL.md row *Different from standard practice*, ✅ confirmed. The paper states that simple-decrease lattice methods admit a complexity bound only under additional strong conditions (p. 9).
- Method 4 ✅ weak evidence. The proposed constrained extension keeps the skeleton and swaps only the stationarity measure (χ in place of ∇f, p. 9).
- Taste mark 4 (theory explains numerics) ✅. The best exponent p = 2 matches earlier numerical experience (p. 7).
- SKILL.md Signature Work Anatomy row *Why then* (sufficient decrease countable, mesh-based variants not) ✅ (p. 4, p. 9). Its *speculation* that the paper was prompted by the contemporary wave of complexity results gets partial support: every comparison in the paper is to 2004–2012 derivative-based complexity results (p. 2, p. 8–9).
- New-pattern candidate: *state where you lose*. The paper volunteers the competitor's better ε-power (p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Two-counter proof: bound successful iterations through sufficient decrease and a step-size floor, and unsuccessful ones through the logarithm of the step-size product (p. 5–6).
- [D4] Separate the n-dependence into the geometry constant (cm⁻²) and the per-iteration cost (poll size), and state each (p. 7–8).
- [D4] Borrow a lower bound by reducing your method to a classical one in dimension 1 (p. 8).
- [D1] Model / Oracle / ε-solution box (p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "Intuitively speaking, insisting on a sufficient decrease will make the function values decrease by a certain non-negligible amount each time a successful iteration is performed." (p. 4)
- "Such an analysis of worst case complexity contributes to a better understanding of the numerical performance of this class of derivative-free optimizations methods." (p. 2)

### S014 · Convergence of trust-region methods based on probabilistic models (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2014, DOI 10.1137/130915984, arXiv 1304.2808)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. S. Bandeira (Princeton PACM), K. Scheinberg (Lehigh) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The founding paper of the probabilistic line. S019 calls its own key definition "inspired by" this paper's probabilistically fully linear models (S019 p. 7) and its convergence argument "a reorganization" of this paper's (S019 p. 8). **Read**: full text of arXiv v1 (9 Apr 2013). The line "October 31, 2018" at the top of the file is an arXiv regeneration date, not the version date. The reference list was skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a cost in model-based DFO. Keeping models accurate needs a "complex mechanism of sample set maintenance" (p. 2). Examples of the cost: estimating Λ-poisedness may take O(n⁶) operations (p. 23), and even one-point-update schemes must rebuild a Λ-poised set in the criticality step (p. 23). The premise is that random sample sets "can yield new and better trade-offs" (p. 2). The evidence cited is heterogeneous: stochastic gradient, randomized coordinate descent, growing sample sizes in stochastic gradient, random positive bases in direct search (p. 2), and the authors' own sparse-recovery models, which need fewer points when derivatives are sparse (p. 2–3). The setting is contrasted with stochastic optimization on two points (abstract, p. 1): f is deterministic, and models must be good with probability ≥ 1/2, not only correct in expectation. A Rosenbrock illustration opens the paper (p. 5–6). Coordinate search takes 11307 evaluations, a direct search with a random basis 5756, random search 3724, and a quadratic-model trust region 62. The reading: random directions find descent, and accurate models are efficient, so randomize the *models* (p. 5).
  - Transferable: Open with a one-problem, four-method illustration where each method isolates one ingredient (random directions vs accurate models, p. 5–6). The combination you propose then looks inevitable.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 3.1 is "almost identical to the classical method" (p. 1). It solves the trust-region subproblem to a fraction of Cauchy decrease (Assumption 3.1, p. 7) and applies the ratio test. The one modification: a step is accepted and the radius increased only if ρ_k ≥ η₁ **and** ‖g_k‖ ≥ η₂δ_k. Otherwise the step is rejected and the radius decreased (p. 8). The rationale: the model step is proportional to ‖g_k‖, so the region should be of comparable size (p. 8). The algorithm never knows whether the current model is accurate (p. 3). What becomes random is the model. Definition 3.2 asks that P(M_k fully linear | σ(M₀,…,M_{k−1})) ≥ p, with p ≥ 1/2 (p. 7). Conditioning on the past is chosen over independence because the iterate and the radius depend on history (p. 7). Algorithm 4.1 adds a practical three-branch radius update (p. 14). Algorithm 5.1 is the second-order version, with a fraction of optimal decrease and a new measure τ (p. 15–18).
  - Transferable: Put the *is this model good?* uncertainty into a probability conditioned on the algorithm's σ-algebra, not into an independence assumption the algorithm cannot guarantee (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is C¹ with Lipschitz gradient on the enlarged level set L_enl(x₀) = ∪ B(x, δ_max) and bounded below (Assumption 3.3, p. 8). Bounded model Hessians (Assumption 3.2) are "introduced for convenience": a large model Hessian can be replaced by a smaller one (p. 7). Function values are exact. Relaxing this is named as future work (p. 27). For second order: C² with Lipschitz and bounded Hessian (Assumption 5.1, p. 15). The measure τ contains the extra term ‖∇f‖/‖∇²f‖ because model Hessians can no longer be bounded (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The proof runs in six steps.
  1. Lemma 3.1: δ_k → 0 for *every* realization. This is a deterministic argument: each non-decrease iteration yields a constant decrease in f (p. 8–9).
  2. Lemma 3.2 (book Lemma 10.6): a fully linear model with δ_k small relative to ‖g_k‖ gives ρ_k ≥ η₁ (p. 9).
  3. Martingale tool (Theorem 4.1): a submartingale with bounded increments either converges or has lim sup = ∞ (p. 9–10).
  4. liminf result (Theorem 4.2). The random walk W_k = Σ(2·1_{S_i} − 1) is a submartingale with ±1 steps, so lim sup W_k = ∞ almost surely. Condition on that event. If ‖∇f‖ ≥ ε, then R_k = log_γ(b⁻¹δ_k) moves +1 on every good model and ≥ −1 otherwise, so it must become positive, which contradicts δ_k < b (p. 10–11).
  5. lim result. Lemma 4.1 shows that one cannot have Σ_P Z < ∞ and Σ_N Z = ∞ at once for Bernoulli signs with P ≥ 1/2 (p. 11). Lemma 4.2 gives Σ Δ_k < ∞ over iterations with ‖∇f‖ > ε (p. 12). Theorem 4.3 uses an "ascent pair" argument: Lipschitz ∇f forces Σ δ_j ≥ ε/2 over each ε-to-2ε crossing (p. 13).
  6. Second order: liminf τ_k = 0 almost surely (Theorem 5.1, p. 19). The lim-type result is left as Conjecture 5.1, with the failed proof written out. The decrease gives only Σδ² < ∞, not Σδ < ∞, and the radius grows only with probability p, so (15) cannot be guaranteed (p. 20–21).

  No complexity or rate is given in this paper.
  - Transferable: Turn *good event with probability ≥ p* into a ±1 random walk, then compare log(radius) with it. This gives almost-sure convergence in half a page (p. 10–11).
  - Transferable: State an unproved extension as a conjecture and write out exactly which inequality fails in the probabilistic case (p. 20–21).
- **D5 Experiments**: Illustrations only. There is no test collection and no profiles.
  - Rosenbrock 2-D: four methods, with evaluation counts and final f in the figure captions (p. 5–6).
  - Five-point minimum-Frobenius-norm models on a milder Rosenbrock. A deterministic coordinate sample set reaches 10⁻⁴ in 8500 evaluations; a random sample set reaches 10⁻⁶ in 2700. An iteration-log table shows the deterministic run oscillating between success and failure (p. 23–24).
  - A 10-D Rosenbrock variant with only 2 active coordinates. Random-sample sparse models (RSTR): 18 iterations, 494 evaluations, 4.0e-11. Greedy sparse models (GSTR): 164 / 185 / 5.0e-8. Minimum-Frobenius-norm models (MFN): 325 / 346 / 2.5e-5 (p. 26). The paper admits RSTR uses more evaluations and argues that they "can be obtained in parallel" (p. 26).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper contrasts itself with stochastic-gradient models assumed "to be “correct” only in expectation" (p. 1) and with random search, whose usefulness is "limited by the fixed step sizes determined by the complexity analysis" (p. 4). Coordinate and compass search are called "inherently slow" (p. 4). Model-based methods are credited through Moré–Wild's finding that they are "typically significantly superior" (p. 5). The paper builds on the authors' book and on Bandeira–Scheinberg–Vicente's sparse-model paper (p. 2–3, p. 25). It cites Vicente 2013 for direct-search rates and Vicente–Custódio for dense random bases (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract is a contrast ("differs from many stochastic optimization approaches in two principal ways", p. 1) followed by a one-line slogan (good models more likely than bad ones, p. 1). There is a notation table for the κ constants (p. 3). Intuition follows the martingale theorem ("a random walk with bounded increments and an upward drift either converges", p. 10). The paper admits limits openly: "we were unable to extend the results in [15] to this case" (p. 20). §6 is a catalogue of model generators, each paired with a probability bound or an open question (p. 22–27).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a framework plus almost-sure convergence theory (first-order lim, second-order liminf) plus a catalogue of models that satisfy the probabilistic condition: Gaussian linear interpolation, sparse recovery, nonuniform recovery (p. 22–26). Concrete limits and future work:
  - lim-type second-order convergence is unproved (Conjecture 5.1, p. 20);
  - function values must be exact (p. 27);
  - reused sample points may break the submartingale property ("subject of our future research", p. 27);
  - condition-number bounds for quadratic models on random sets are left open (p. 25);
  - asynchronous parallel evaluation is suggested as a source of random models (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (origin, with the model as the random object). The deterministic requirement, a fully linear model at every iteration through sample-set maintenance (p. 2, p. 22–23), becomes *fully linear with probability ≥ p given the past* (Definition 3.2, p. 7). The proof is by submartingale (p. 10–13). ⚠ Nuance for SKILL.md: this paper proves **almost-sure convergence only, with no complexity**. The high-probability rate for this trust-region method is sketched later, in S019 §6 (S019 p. 21–22). Here the threshold is the fixed 1/2, which matches the symmetric update (×γ on success, ×γ⁻¹ otherwise, p. 8). (Our remark: S019's p₀ = ln θ / ln(γ⁻¹θ) equals 1/2 when θ = γ⁻¹.)
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. The skeleton is kept, and the only change is in the acceptance and radius rule (‖g_k‖ ≥ η₂δ_k added, p. 8). The new "class" here is random models, not a new problem class.
- Research heuristic 2 ✅. Single-problem numerics, where random directions and random sample sets beat fixed patterns, motivate the relaxation (p. 5, p. 23–24).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. There is no solver, collection or profile, only illustrative runs (p. 5–6, p. 23–26). This is a theory-first paper.
- New-pattern candidate: *name where the proof technology breaks*. Conjecture 5.1 comes with its failed proof (p. 20–21).
- New-pattern candidate: *condition on the past, not independence*. The same modelling choice is made again in S019 (S019 p. 7, p. 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] The log-radius vs ±1 random-walk comparison for almost-sure liminf convergence (p. 10–11).
- [D4] The "ascent pair" argument that upgrades liminf to lim when Σ radius over bad iterations is finite (p. 12–13).
- [D2] Add the condition ‖g_k‖ ≥ η₂δ_k to success, so that the radius tracks the model gradient and good models always enlarge it (p. 8).
- [D8] Publish the conjecture together with the exact failing inequality (p. 20–21).
- [D5] Isolate ingredients in the motivating example: fixed vs random directions, directions vs models (p. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "Hence we show that a standard optimization framework can be used in cases when models are random and may or may not provide good approximations, as long as “good” models are more likely than “bad” models." (p. 1)
- "We believe this condition is more reasonable than assuming complete independence of Mk from the past, which is difficult to ensure given that the current iterate, around which the model is built, and the trust-region radius depend on the algorithm history." (p. 7)

### S019 · Direct search based on probabilistic descent (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2015, DOI 10.1137/140961602)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Gratton (ENSEEIHT-INPT), C. W. Royer (doctoral student, Toulouse), Z. Zhang (CERFACS-IRIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Carries S014's probabilistic idea from models to polling directions and adds the global rates that S014 lacked. It sits between the deterministic complexity of S023 (cited as [28]) and later constrained and noisy extensions. **Read**: full text, including Appendix A (Chernoff proof) and Appendix B (spherical-cap bounds), both read in full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a numerical surprise. The theory requires positive spanning sets (PSS) with at least n+1 vectors and a cosine measure bounded away from zero (abstract, p. 1). But "we were surprised by the numerical experiments reported [18]", where polling directions generated free of PSS rules, possibly fewer than n+1, did better (p. 2). [18] is Gratton–Vicente's merit-function paper (reference list, p. 29). The second motivation is the "obvious connection" to S014 (p. 2). The paper reproduces the surprise in its own Table 1 (p. 6–7). On 10 CUTEr problems with n = 40, m independent random unit directions beat [I −I], [Q −Q] and [Q_k −Q_k], and m = 2 is best on most problems. The key conceptual step (p. 5): cm(D_k) ≥ κ, over *all* vectors v, is much stronger than the proof needs. If −g_k were known, cm(D_k, −g_k) ≥ κ would suffice.
  - Transferable: Find the one instance of a uniform requirement that the proof actually uses (cm(D, −g) instead of min over v of cm(D, v)), then ask for that instance only probabilistically (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 2.1 is plain direct search without a search step. On success it sets α_{k+1} = min{γα_k, α_max}; on failure α_{k+1} = θα_k (p. 4). In the introduction it is summarised in one sentence with concrete constants, ρ = α²/2 with the step doubled or halved (p. 2–3). Only D_k changes. It must be *p-probabilistically κ-descent*: P(cm(D_k, −G_k) ≥ κ | D₀,…,D_{k−1}) ≥ p (Definition 3.1, p. 7). Three consequences:
  - The threshold p₀ = ln θ / ln(γ⁻¹θ) comes from the step-size factors (eq. (8), p. 9).
  - Expansion γ > 1 is *required*; with γ = 1, p₀ = 1 (p. 14, p. 20).
  - Uniform random directions qualify when m > log₂[1 − ln θ / ln γ]. For γ = 2 and θ = 1/2 this means m = 2 "no matter how large the problem is" (Corollary B.1, p. 27–28; p. 20).

  An open issue, raised by Trefethen: D = {d, −d} maximizes the probability among 2-vector sets and did slightly better numerically (p. 22–23).
  - Transferable: Derive the minimal number of random objects per iteration from the algorithm's own expansion and contraction constants, and print the rule (m > log₂[1 − ln θ/ln γ], p. 20).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is bounded below and C¹ with Lipschitz ∇f on ℝⁿ (Assumption 2.1, p. 5). The forcing function ρ is positive, nondecreasing and o(α) (Assumption 2.2, p. 5), plus the scaling bounds ρ(θα) ≤ θ̄ρ(α) and ρ(γα) ≤ γ̄ρ(α), called "not restrictive" (Assumption 4.1, p. 9). Normalized directions are assumed "for the sake of simplicity" (Assumption 2.3, p. 5). Conditioning on the past is a real assumption. §5.3 shows that without it the probability bound no longer tends to one (Proposition 5.3, p. 19). In Appendix B the polling sets are independent across iterations (p. 24).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The authors name their contribution as a proof technique that separates deterministic counting from probability (p. 22).
  1. Lemma 2.1 (Taylor): cm(D_k, −g_k) ≥ κ and α_k < ϕ(κ‖g_k‖) imply success. The auxiliary function ϕ (eq. (3)) equals 2t/(c+ν) for ρ = cα²/2 (p. 5–6).
  2. Almost-sure convergence (§3.2), "no more than a reorganization" of S014 (p. 8). Lemma 3.2: if liminf ‖G_k‖ > 0, then Σ[Z_k ln γ + (1−Z_k) ln θ] = −∞. With p₀ this sum is a submartingale with bounded increments, so the event has probability zero (p. 8–9).
  3. Counting layer, valid for every realization. Lemma 4.1: Σρ(α_k) ≤ β, via geometric sums over blocks between successes (p. 10). Lemma 4.2: Σ_{l<k} z_l ≤ β/ρ(min{γ⁻¹α₀, ϕ(κ‖g̃_k‖)}) + p₀k. The proof uses the decomposition z_l ≤ (1 − v_l) + v_l y_l and the step-size product (p. 11–12). The same lemma re-proves and generalizes the deterministic complexity bound (Proposition 4.1, p. 12).
  4. Probabilistic layer. A Chernoff lower-tail bound for Σ Z_l under *conditional* probability ≥ p: π_k(λ) ≤ exp(−(p−λ)²k/(2p)) (Lemma 4.4, p. 13). It is proved in Appendix A with Markov's inequality and iterated conditional expectations (p. 23–24).
  5. Results. Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.1 give ‖G̃_k‖ ≤ O(1/√k) with probability ≥ 1 − exp(−(p−p₀)²k/(8p)) (p. 14–15). Theorem 4.2 and Corollary 4.2 give O(ε⁻²) iterations with overwhelming probability (p. 15–16). With κ = τ/√n and m directions per iteration this becomes **O(mnε⁻²) evaluations**, against O(n²ε⁻²) for the deterministic method (p. 16). Theorem 4.3 gives an explicit k for a given confidence level P (p. 17). Proposition 5.2 gives the expected minimum gradient norm (p. 18–19). §6 transfers the rate argument to S014's trust-region method (p. 21–22).
  6. Appendix B: a spherical-cap lower bound, ϱ(κ) ≥ 1/2 − κ√(n/2π), via the regularized incomplete Beta function and log-convexity of Γ (p. 25–26).
  - Transferable: Two-layer proof. (i) A deterministic lemma, valid for every run, bounds how many *good* iterations can occur before ε-stationarity. (ii) A concentration inequality for the number of good events under conditional probability ≥ p (p. 9, p. 11–13, p. 22).
  - Transferable: Reuse the new counting lemma to re-derive the deterministic special case (Proposition 4.1, p. 12). This checks the lemma and generalizes the old result for free.
- **D5 Experiments**: The setup (p. 6–7):
  - Matlab; 10 CUTEr problems at n = 40 (Tables 1–2) and n = 100 (Table 3);
  - α₀ = 1, θ = 0.5, γ = 2, ρ = 10⁻³α²; stop at α < 10⁻¹⁰ or 2000n evaluations;

### S015 · Complexity of gradient descent for multiobjective optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2019, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2018.1510928)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. Fliege (Southampton), A. I. F. Vaz (Minho) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Carries the Method-2 yardstick (match the single-objective gradient rates) to multiobjective first-order methods. It sits between DMS-era derivative-free MOO and the Lehigh stochastic multi-gradient work. S016 cites it for the deterministic multi-gradient direction (S016 p. 4). **Read**: full text (preprint dated 18 Jul 2018, 10 pages).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap in the literature. Many multiobjective first-order methods exist with global-convergence proofs: Fliege–Svaiter steepest descent, proximal, trust-region, conjugate-gradient and Newton variants (p. 1). None has rates. The single-objective benchmark is recalled: 1/√k nonconvex (tight by Cartis–Gould–Toint), 1/k convex, linear strongly convex, with direct search [17] also at 1/√k (p. 2). The aim is to "extend this theory to multiobjective optimization" (p. 2). The subtle point is flagged in advance: in the convex cases rates are about function values, and "it is not foreseeable, before a careful analysis, what gap or error should be quantified" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 is the Fliege–Svaiter method. The direction is d = argmin_d max_i ∇f_iᵀd + ½‖d‖² (eq. (1), p. 2), rewritten as the QP (3) (p. 3). Backtracking from t = 1 continues until *all* components satisfy Armijo (eq. (8), p. 4). Algorithm 2, for the convex cases, uses the alternative sufficient decrease F(x+td) ≤ F(x) + t∇Fᵀd + (γt/2)‖d‖²e (eq. (11), p. 5). The key move is to read the KKT multipliers of the QP, d = −Σλ_i∇f_i with Σλ_i = 1 (eq. (4)–(5), p. 3), as an *implicit scalarization*. Every single-objective inequality is proved for the λ-weighted sum. Kept: the gradient-method proof templates. Changed: the stationarity measure (‖d_k‖) and the optimality gap, which is measured for Σλ̄_i f_i with averaged multipliers λ̄ (Theorem 4.1, p. 7–8).
  - Transferable: When a multiobjective direction is a min-norm convex combination of gradients, use its dual weights as the scalarization in the analysis, even though the algorithm never scalarizes (p. 3, p. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Each f_i is C¹ with an L_i-Lipschitz gradient (p. 1). In the nonconvex case at least one f_i is bounded below (Theorem 3.1, p. 4). In the convex cases the whole sequence (x_k, λ_k) is assumed to converge to (x*, λ*), a "slightly stronger assumption" (p. 6). Remark 4.1 explains why: with a fixed step ≤ 1/L_max the key lemma holds without it, but then the sign of the left-hand side, which the rate needs, is unknown (p. 7). Strong convexity has modulus μ = min μ_i (p. 6–7).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Two parts.
  - Nonconvex. Lemma 3.1 gives a step floor t_min = min{(1−β)/(2L_max), 1}, from the failed-doubling argument with Lipschitz continuity (p. 4). Theorem 3.1 telescopes the decrease of *one* bounded-below f_i to get min_ℓ ‖d_ℓ‖ ≤ √((F₀^max − F^min)/M)/√k (p. 4–5). Lemma 2.1 links ‖d‖ = 0 to Pareto criticality (p. 3).
  - Convex and strongly convex. Lemma 4.2 writes the per-objective descent inequality, bounds f_i(x_k) by convexity, multiplies by λ_i^k and sums. Substituting d = −Σλ∇f yields the classical three-point identity (‖x_k − x*‖² − ‖x_{k+1} − x*‖²)/(2t_min) (p. 6–7). Theorem 4.1 sums over ℓ and uses the monotonicity of each f_i to get a 1/k gap for the averaged weights λ̄ (p. 7–8). Theorem 4.2 gives a linear rate in ‖x_k − x*‖, which translates into a linear rate for the gap in f* = Σλ*_i f_i (p. 8).
  - Transferable: Check that a single-objective proof template survives when "decrease" means decrease of every component and "gradient" means the dual-weighted gradient. The only new step is justifying the sign of the weighted gap (p. 7, Remark 4.1).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D6 Positioning**: This is a continuation of Fliege–Svaiter [8], within the Fukuda–Graña Drummond survey context (p. 1). Priority is credited openly: "It was brought to our attention by a Referee" that Grapiglia's thesis, later a paper, had proved the same O(ε⁻²) bound with trust-region methods, and unpublished work had done it for line search (p. 2). The claimed difference is modest: "more concise" and covering the convex cases (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Very short, about 9 pages. The expectation is set with deliberate understatement ("without too much surprise", p. 2). The abstract states the result class (the rates are the same as for single-objective gradient descent) and the convex caveat ("given for implicit scalarizations", p. 1). The conclusion packages the three rates with their complexity translations, 1/ε², 1/ε and log(1/ε) (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory: global rates for multiobjective gradient descent in three convexity regimes. The limits are concrete:
  - convex rates hold only for implicit scalarizations with weights λ̄ (p. 1, p. 7–8);
  - convergence of the whole sequence is assumed (p. 6);
  - acceleration to 1/k² is "more intricate than it seems at least using the steepest descent direction (1) and the proof technology of our paper" (p. 9).

  Future work: momentum and proximal operators (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The whole paper applies Method 2 step 4 (set the result next to the gradient method's bound) in a new class (p. 2, p. 9). It is an earlier (2019) instance of the *recover classical single-objective rates* statement that SKILL.md attributes to 2026. Step 5 (lower bounds) is addressed only by citing the Cartis–Gould–Toint sharpness for m = 1 (p. 2).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The skeleton (gradient step with backtracking sufficient decrease) is kept. *Success* is redefined as sufficient decrease of every component (eq. (8), p. 4), and the stationarity measure becomes ‖d_k‖ (p. 3). The price is stated in the abstract: convex rates hold only for implicit scalarizations (p. 1).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. There are no numerics and no code; this is a pure theory note.
- New-pattern candidate: *state where you lose / credit priority in the text*. The referee-flagged prior result is acknowledged in the introduction, not in a footnote (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: *name where the proof technology breaks* (acceleration, p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Dual multipliers of the direction subproblem as implicit scalarization weights, used only in the analysis (p. 3, p. 6–8).
- [D4] Failed-doubling argument for a uniform backtracking step floor with m objectives, taking L_max = max L_i (p. 4).

- "The results of our paper are derived in a more concise way and cover the convex and strongly convex cases." (p. 2)

### S016 · Accuracy and fairness trade-offs in machine learning: a stochastic multi-objective approach (Computational Management Science 2022, DOI 10.1007/s10287-022-00425-z, arXiv 2008.01132)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Suyun Liu (Lehigh ISE; the correspondence line lists both authors) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The Lehigh period. It applies the Pareto-front stochastic multi-gradient method (PF-SMG) of Liu & Vicente (Annals of OR 2021) to ML fairness. The theory lives in that companion paper (p. 4); this paper is the modelling, experimental and code piece. **Read**: full text of arXiv v3 (18 Mar 2022), in ICML-style two-column format, including Appendices A–E. Appendix E's figures are plots, so only their captions and axis labels were available.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an application. Fairness in credit scoring and criminal justice (abstract, p. 1) is motivated by US policy reports (p. 1). The critique: in-training fairness methods impose a constraint or a penalty, "which ultimately limits the information given to decision-makers" (p. 1). In multiobjective terms these are a priori methods, and Zafar et al.'s constrained problems are essentially the ε-constraint method (Haimes 1971). They depend on the decision maker knowing the fairness magnitude in advance (p. 2). The reframing: a stochastic bi-objective problem whose Pareto front "uniquely and comprehensively" defines the trade-off (p. 1, p. 3).
  - Transferable: Re-describe a neighbouring field's standard method in your field's taxonomy (constrained fair learning = a priori ε-constraint) to make its limitation obvious (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The formulation sets f₁ to the expected (then empirical) logistic loss (eq. (5), (7), p. 4). f₂ is the CV (disparate-impact) score, replaced by the squared decision-boundary covariance of Zafar et al., which makes both objectives convex, smooth finite sums (eq. (8)–(9), p. 4). The algorithm is SMG: stochastic gradients per objective, a QP over the simplex for the min-norm combination, and a step along it (Algorithm 1, p. 13). PF-SMG keeps a list and, at each iteration, does three things (Algorithm 2, p. 13; §3 p. 4):
  1. adds r perturbed points around each point;
  2. runs p₂ SMG trajectories of p₁ iterations from each point;
  3. removes the dominated points.

  Extensions:
  - a multi-valued attribute becomes a max over K binary covariances, smoothed by the softmax-weighted average S_β with β = 8 (eq. (10), p. 6);
  - equal opportunity gives a nonconvex f₄ with a smoothed min (p. 7);
  - tri-objective problems cover two attributes (13) or two fairness measures (14) (p. 8);
  - streaming data: warm-start the list from the current front for each new 2,000-sample batch (§7, p. 9).
  - Transferable: Smooth nonsmooth fairness aggregators (max over groups, min in the FNR surrogate) so that stochastic-gradient machinery applies (p. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Smooth objectives, convex or nonconvex (p. 3). A single binary sensitive attribute "for simplicity", later relaxed (p. 2). Binary classification with a linear classifier (p. 4). The covariance proxy rests on zero covariance being *necessary* for independence (p. 4). The proxy is admitted to be imperfect: on COMPAS, zero covariance does not give zero CV_FNR, possibly because the covariance is poorly approximated from few samples (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No new theory. Rates are imported from Liu & Vicente 2021: SMG has the same rates as stochastic gradient, but toward a nondominated point (p. 4). Appendix B states the gap: "there are no convergence or complexity results to determine the whole Pareto front" (p. 13). PF-SMG hyperparameters are chosen by grid search on validation data (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: The data (p. 5, p. 8, p. 15):
  - Adult Income: 45,222 samples, 12 nonsensitive features, gender (binary) and race (5 values), split 60/10/30;
  - synthetic: 20 sets of 2,000 points from Zafar's generator;
  - COMPAS two-year-violent, Black and White defendants only: 5,278 samples, the whole set used for training and testing "Due to shortage of data".

  The comparison protocol:
  - The baseline is EPS-fair: Zafar's ε-constraint solved by SLSQP over evenly spaced thresholds. 70–80% of its points were dominated and were removed (p. 5).
  - Metrics: purity, spread Γ and Δ (DMS-style), hypervolume (via Pymoo), CPU time per nondominated point, and gradient evaluations per nondominated point. They are shown as performance profiles over 40 datasets (20 synthetic, 20 Adult) (p. 6–7; Appendix C p. 13–15).
  - For fairness, the PF-SMG front is down-sampled to EPS-fair's size before computing purity, spread and hypervolume (p. 6).

  The losses are reported: EPS-fair has slightly better purity, while PF-SMG wins on spread, hypervolume, CPU time and gradient evaluations (p. 6). In the race case dominance is "mixed" (p. 6). A robustness check with 50/50 gender resampling finds the conflict "not alleviated at all" (p. 5). For the nonconvex equal-opportunity problem EPS-fair produced no reasonable trade-offs, so no comparison is made (p. 7). The code is on GitHub (MOO_Fairness, p. 5 footnote). The streaming result is "preliminary", with figures only (p. 9, Figure 10 p. 18).
  - Transferable: When fronts have different cardinalities, down-sample the denser one before computing set-quality metrics, and report cost *per nondominated solution* (p. 6).
  - Transferable: Clean the baseline's output (remove its dominated points) before comparing, and say how much was removed (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the in-training category (p. 2) and among the few frameworks that compute the whole curve (Zafar; Navon et al., p. 2). Credit for SMG goes to Mercier et al. for the algorithm and to Liu & Vicente for the analysis (p. 4). It cites Fliege–Vaz–Vicente (S015) for the deterministic multi-gradient direction (p. 4) and Custódio et al. 2011 (DMS) for the m ≥ 2 spread Δ (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: This is ML-venue style: contribution bullets (p. 3), an organization paragraph (p. 3), and interpretive figures such as the share of positive outcomes per group along the front (p. 5–6). The claims are stronger than in the SIAM papers ("proved to be computational efficient", p. 3, where the proof is numerical). The concluding remarks repeat the advantages (p. 9). Some honesty remains: "mixed" dominance (p. 6), and the proxy mismatch on COMPAS (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a modelling framework, numerical evidence against the standard constrained approach, and released code. Limits:
  - no theory for the whole front (p. 13);
  - covariance proxies are imperfect (p. 8);
  - small COMPAS data (p. 8);
  - only logistic regression;
  - streaming results are preliminary (p. 9).

  Future work: multi-class classification, regression, other predictors, and nonconvex surrogates such as mutual information and fairness risk measures (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, and Research heuristic 4 ✅. Keep a nondominated list and do not aggregate a priori. The list is PF-SMG's core (p. 4, p. 13), and the a priori critique is the paper's framing (p. 2). This supports the SKILL.md warning sign *Aggregating objectives before the front is known*.

### Batch synthesis (c05)

- **The counting device behind Method 2 is fixed**: sufficient decrease plus a step-size floor gives successes, and the log of the step-size product gives failures (S023 p. 5–6). The same device is reused as a realization-wise lemma in S019 (p. 10–12) and in multiobjective form in S015 (p. 4–5).
- **Method 3 has two instantiations in this batch.** S014 makes the model random, with threshold 1/2 and almost-sure convergence only (p. 7, p. 10–13). Both condition on the past rather than assume independence (S014 p. 7; S019 p. 7, p. 19).
