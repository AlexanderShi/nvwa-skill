### S049 · A merit function approach for direct search (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2014, DOI 10.1137/130917661)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Gratton (ENSEEIHT-INPT, Toulouse) · **Position in trajectory**:
- Coimbra period, funded partly by FCT and by the French aeronautics and space research network (Fondation STAE) under grant ADTAO (p. 1).
- It builds on the Vicente–Custódio analysis of direct search for discontinuous functions (ref. [25], p. 2).
- It comes just before the probabilistic-descent work. The conclusion calls a study of random directions and random positive spanning sets one "which we are currently undertaking" (p. 17).

**Text read**: preprint dated 2 May 2014, pp. 1–19, read in full, including Appendix A (pp. 17–18).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min f(x) s.t. x ∈ Ω = Ω_r ∩ Ω_nr (p. 1). The entry is a classification of constraints by what the black box allows:
  - **Unrelaxable constraints (Ω_nr)** must hold wherever f is evaluated. They include hidden constraints, which show up only as failed evaluations.
  - **Relaxable constraints** c_i(x) ≤ 0 need hold only approximately or asymptotically (pp. 1–2).

  The literature is then arranged as a gap (pp. 2–3):
  - feasible methods: the extreme barrier, with either MADS lattices or free dense directions under sufficient decrease; and projections;
  - penalty and augmented-Lagrangian methods, which handle relaxable constraints only;
  - filters;
  - progressive barrier (Audet–Dennis), described as "The first general approach" to handle both types.

  The paper offers an alternative to progressive barrier, with a merit function in place of a filter (p. 3). A footnote adds that model-based trust-region DFO with nonlinear constraints existed only in implementations, with "no convergence theory" (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: open a constrained-DFO paper by sorting the constraints by evaluation status (can f be evaluated when the constraint is violated?). Then place each existing method by the class it handles (pp. 1–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The skeleton is kept: optional search, poll and step-size update (Algorithm 2.1, pp. 5–6). Only the notion of success changes (pp. 4–5). A trial point y_k is successful in one of two ways:
  - it decreases the violation g(x) = Σ max(c_i(x), 0) sufficiently while x_k is far from feasibility, i.e. g(y_k) < g(x_k) − ρ(α_k) and g(x_k) > Cρ(α_k); or, failing that,
  - it decreases the ℓ1 merit function M(x; µ) = f + µg sufficiently, with µ_k = max{µ̄, (f(y_k) − f(x_k))/(Cρ(α_k))} (eq. 5).

  The µ_k update transcribes the classical rule (Nocedal–Wright (18.33)) into derivative-free terms (p. 4). The difference quotient [f(x_k + α_k d_k) − f(x_k)]/α_k replaces ∇f(x_k)ᵀd_k, and ρ(α_k)/α_k replaces g(x_k). The merit function is used only to judge a step that has already been computed (p. 3).

  **Restoration** (Algorithm 2.2, p. 6) works as follows:
  - It is entered when g decreases sufficiently but f increases by more than µ̄ρ(α_k) (p. 5).
  - Inside it, only g is minimized.
  - It is left when an unsuccessful iteration finds a direction that decreases M(·; µ̄) (p. 6).

  Unrelaxable constraints are handled by the extreme barrier: infeasible poll points are dropped (pp. 5–6). As in progressive barrier, a relaxable constraint can be moved to the unrelaxable set. After N Restoration calls, g is minimized to zero and Ω_nr is redefined as Ω_nr ∩ Ω_r (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: to port a derivative-based parameter update (penalty, trust-region ratio) to DFO, replace each directional derivative by its difference quotient along the poll step. Replace each derivative-dependent quantity by ρ(α)/α (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The standing assumptions are:
  - f and the c_i are only locally Lipschitz near accumulation points (p. 2), and f is bounded below (Thm 3.1, p. 7).
  - Ω_nr has a nonempty hypertangent cone (p. 8). Because Ω_nr need not be closed, the limit point must be *assumed* to lie in Ω_nr (p. 8).
  - Stationarity needs refining directions that are dense in T^Cl_Ωnr(x*) ∩ S^{n−1} for the feasibility result (Thm 4.2, p. 9), and dense in the set T(x*) of eq. (10) for the optimality result (Thm 4.4, p. 11).
  - Because of nonsmoothness and unrelaxable constraints, the poll directions must be dense in the unit sphere (p. 3). Positive spanning is not required (p. 4).
  - No boundedness of the penalty parameter is assumed (p. 17).

  The case where Restoration is entered and left infinitely often needs three extra hypotheses. The first two restrict the algorithm and the third assumes something about its output: α_k is never increased, there is no search step, and {x_k} converges (Thm 6.1, p. 12). The authors state that these conditions are "not the ideal ones" (p. 12).

  The smooth particularization (§7) assumes C¹ data, Ω_nr = ℝⁿ, poll sets converging to a positive spanning set (PSS), and the squared violation (11) (pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable technique: when a hypothesis is needed only for one pathological branch of the analysis (here, infinitely many mode switches), state it only in that branch's theorem and admit that it is not ideal. Do not impose it globally (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The analysis is split by how many times Restoration is entered: finitely often (§§3–4), entered and never left (§5), and entered and left infinitely often (§6) (p. 4).
  - **Step size (Theorem 3.1, p. 7)**: lim inf α_k = 0, by contradiction with three branches.
    - Infinitely many g-successes would contradict g ≥ 0.
    - Merit-successes with µ_k = µ̄ would drive M(·; µ̄) to −∞, because g-successes outside Restoration do not increase M(·; µ̄).
    - A merit-success with µ_k equal to the ratio in (5) is impossible, because that choice forces M(x_{k+1}; µ_k) ≥ M(x_k; µ_k).
    - The third branch shows (my reading of the proof) that, in the theory, merit-successes always use µ̄. This fits the referee's remark, reported in the conclusion, that setting µ_k = µ̄ would not change the theory (p. 17).
  - **Feasibility (Theorem 4.1, pp. 8–9)**: at a refining iteration there are two cases. If g(x_k) ≤ Cρ(α_k), then g(x*) = 0. Otherwise g-decrease failed, which gives g°(x*; d) ≥ 0 by Lipschitz continuity (to swap d_k for d) and ρ(α)/α → 0.
  - **Optimality (Theorem 4.3, pp. 10–11)**: the same two-case split on µ_k gives bounds (7) and (9), and both vanish. The result is f°(x*; d) ≥ 0 along refining directions with g°(x*; d) ≤ 0.
  - **Density**: density of refining directions then gives Clarke stationarity (Thms 4.2, 4.4, pp. 9–11). §5 reuses the same proof paragraphs (pp. 11–12).
  - **Smooth case**: it uses the mean value theorem on a limit PSS D* (Thm 7.1, pp. 13–14).

  No worst-case complexity or rate is derived (checked pp. 1–19).
  - Transferable technique: for an algorithm with an auxiliary mode (restoration, fallback), split the convergence proof by how often the mode is entered: finitely often, entered and never left, or entered and left infinitely often. Give each case its own section, so that extra hypotheses appear only where they are needed (pp. 4, 6–13).
- **D5 Experiments**: The paper calls this section a "proof of concept" (p. 4). The protocol (p. 14):
  - a MATLAB implementation run "without any parameter tuning", with α0 = 1, β1 = β2 = 0.5, γ = 2, ρ(α) = min{10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁵α²}, µ̄ = max{10, g(x0)} and C = 100;
  - no search step, and the nonsmooth violation (3);
  - random unit poll directions with |D_k| ∈ {n/2, n+1, 2n}. The paper states there is "no guarantee" that they form a positive spanning set, and that the theory does not need one;
  - averages over 40 random seeds.

  Three problems from the progressive-barrier paper [6] were run, each from a feasible and an infeasible start (pp. 14–15):
  - a linear objective on a ball, n = 50;
  - a linear objective on a nonconvex region between two balls, n = 50;
  - the styrene process "truth model": 8 variables, 4 yes–no unrelaxable constraints, 7 relaxable constraints, and bounds treated as unrelaxable. It was run through NOMAD's C++ code.

  The plots show f against evaluations. The budgets are 600n (Figs. 1–2) and 300 evaluations for styrene (Fig. 3) (pp. 15–16). Findings:
  - convergence in all cases, with non-monotone f when starting infeasible;
  - |D_k| = n/2 is the least robust;
  - fewer than 2000 iterations, so parallel polling would pay (p. 15);
  - the returned points were always feasible for the relaxable constraints, the penalty parameter never caused scaling trouble, and "Restoration was only entered a negligible number of times" (p. 16).

  Progressive barrier was not re-run. It is compared only through the numbers reported in [6], and the results "must be considered good when compared to those reported in [6]" (p. 15). There are no profiles.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents its approach as "an alternative approach to progressive barrier [6]", swapping the filter for a merit function (p. 3). The tone is neutral and descriptive. Credits:
  - MADS (lattice with simple decrease) and Vicente–Custódio (dense directions with sufficient decrease), as the two known ways to globalize with an extreme barrier (p. 2);
  - Lewis–Torczon and Kolda–Lewis–Torczon (augmented Lagrangian) and Diniz-Ehrhardt–Martínez–Pedroso (p. 3);
  - Liuzzi–Lucidi(–Sciandrone) (penalties) and Martínez–Sobral (restoration on thin domains) (p. 3);
  - Audet–Dennis (filter) and Dennis–Price–Coope (filter with an envelope) (p. 3).

  The refining-subsequence and refining-direction vocabulary is taken from Audet–Dennis [3, 5] (p. 8), and the Clarke–Jahn derivative from Jahn and [5] (Appendix, p. 18).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Novelty claim**: one sentence in the abstract: "The novelty of this contribution relies mostly on how relaxable constraints are handled" (p. 1).
  - **Reusable definitions**: key definitions are set off between "Begin (successful point)" and "End", and between "Begin (Restoration identifier)" and "End", so that both the search and the poll steps can use them (pp. 4–5).
  - **Roadmap**: the introduction announces the case split (p. 4). Each theorem repeats its case hypothesis, e.g. "Assume that Restoration is entered finitely many times" (pp. 7–11).
  - **Smooth translation**: a separate section restates the Clarke results as gradient projections for readers who work with smooth problems (§7, pp. 13–14).
  - **Hedges**: "proof of concept" (p. 4), "relatively promising" (p. 17), and Restoration kept "as it seemed to us as a potentially useful tool" (p. 17).
  - **Referee**: a simplification the referee proposed is reported and politely declined "For sake of generality and algorithmic flexibility" (p. 17).
  - **Awkward phrase**: the abstract claims convergence "for first-order stationary constraints" (p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic (a merit-function acceptance test with Restoration) and theoretical (Clarke-stationarity results for each Restoration case). The numerics are illustrative only.

  Limits and open items the paper states (p. 17):
  - the infinite enter/leave case needs extra conditions (pp. 12, 17);
  - there is no comprehensive comparison with other direct-search methods for nonlinear constraints;
  - random directions and random PSSs remain to be studied, a study the authors say they are "currently undertaking";
  - the effect of a search step, of the choice of µ̄, and of resetting the step size when the mode changes is not yet understood.

  Limits that are visible but not stated:
  - there is no complexity bound;
  - there are only three test problems, compared through published numbers;
  - the Restoration machinery takes up half of the theory (§§5–6) but was barely used in the runs (p. 16);
  - no code release is mentioned (checked pp. 1–19).

**Method links**:
- **Method 4 ✅ evidence.** The search/poll/step-size skeleton is unchanged. Only "success" is redefined, as sufficient decrease in g far from feasibility or else in M(·; µ_k) (pp. 4–6).
  - Heuristic 6 ✅ describes this paper exactly: a merit function plus Restoration for relaxable constraints, and an extreme barrier for unrelaxable ones (pp. 1, 3, 5–6).
  - Method 4 step 5 (*state the price in the abstract*) ⚠ is not done: the abstract names no price, and there is no complexity result.
- **Method 1 ✅ evidence** for its *Different from standard practice* paragraph. The paper states that sufficient decrease is the price for generating dense directions freely, without integer lattices, and that "in practice, sufficient decrease can be imposed as not to differ much from simple decrease" (p. 2). The numerics cap the forcing function at 10⁻⁵ (p. 14). No heuristic occupies the search step here (p. 14), so Method 1 applies only at the level of the skeleton.
- **Method 3 ✅ evidence (origin).** The numerics already poll with as few as n/2 random unit directions, with "no guarantee" of positive spanning, and n/2 is the least robust choice (pp. 14–15). A study of random directions and random PSSs is announced as under way (p. 17). This is Heuristic 2 (numerics before theory), just before GRVZ 2015.
- **Method 2 ⚠ variant.** Method 2 is not applied. The constrained nonsmooth extension stops at lim-inf and Clarke-stationarity statements (pp. 7–13). This is a counter-instance to Method 2's one-liner (*for every derivative-free variant, give a worst-case bound*).
- **Method 5 ⚠ variant.** The numerics are a proof of concept on three problems, with no profiles. The baseline enters only through numbers published in [6], and no code release is stated (pp. 4, 14–17). A proper benchmark is deferred (p. 17).
- **New-pattern candidate: mode-recurrence case analysis.** The convergence theory is split by how often an auxiliary mode recurs (pp. 4, 6–13). See also S073 (Full-Eval vs Low-Eval, pp. 6–11).
- **Existing candidate *name where the proof technology breaks*** gets new evidence. The theorem for the infinite enter/leave case states exactly which algorithmic freedoms must be removed (no search, no step increase) and calls the conditions "not the ideal ones" (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A two-part success test for constrained DFO:
  - accept on sufficient decrease of the violation g only when g(x_k) > Cρ(α_k), i.e. far from feasibility;
  - otherwise accept on sufficient decrease of f + µ_k g, with µ_k = max{µ̄, Δf/(Cρ(α_k))} (pp. 4–5).
- A derivative-free Restoration trigger: g drops by ρ(α) while f rises by more than µ̄ρ(α). Leave Restoration when an unsuccessful restoration poll finds a direction that decreases M(·; µ̄) (pp. 5–6).
- Moving a constraint from relaxable to unrelaxable by counting Restoration calls: after N calls, minimize g to zero, then treat the constraint as unrelaxable through the extreme barrier. This can be done constraint by constraint (p. 13).
- A protocol for random polling:
  - report |D_k| ∈ {n/2, n+1, 2n};
  - average over 40 seeds;
  - set budgets as multiples of n;
  - report iteration counts separately, to argue for parallel polling (pp. 14–15).

**Quotes**:
- "The merit function and the corresponding penalty parameter are only used in the evaluation of an already computed step, to decide whether it will be accepted or not." (p. 3)
- "in practice, sufficient decrease can be imposed as not to differ much from simple decrease." (p. 2)

### S050 · Globally convergent evolution strategies (Mathematical Programming 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10107-014-0793-x)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**:
- Y. Diouane (CERFACS), supported by TOTAL Exploration & Production, Depth Imaging and High Performance Computing (p. 1);
- S. Gratton (ENSEEIHT-INPT, Toulouse) (p. 1).

**Position in trajectory**: SKILL.md cites this paper as its main case of Method 1 and Heuristic 1. A follow-up on search steps and constraints is announced as "a forthcoming paper" (p. 19). **Text read**: preprint dated 9 July 2014, pp. 1–22, read in full, including all figures.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Evolution strategies (ES) such as CMA-ES are widely used for continuous optimization without derivatives. The paper states: "To the best of our knowledge, there are no global convergence results" for (µ/µ_W, λ)-ES in the nonlinear-optimization sense. Only asymptotic results for (1, λ)-ES exist, and those have no recombination (p. 2). Global convergence is defined as a numbered display (1): "some form of convergence to some form of first-order stationarity, independent of the starting point" (p. 2). The stated goal is "to help bridge this gap" between two communities separated by terminology and notation (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when you bring a guarantee into a heuristic community, first state the target property as a numbered definition in that community's terms. Then cite what that community already knows (here, asymptotic results for (1, λ)-ES) to show what is missing (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper asks "how to change Algorithm 2.1, in a minimal way" (p. 4). It prints Algorithm 2.1, the ES as practitioners run it (pp. 3–4), and then the modified Algorithm 3.1 (pp. 5–6). There are two modifications (p. 4):
  - **Separate step size.** A step size σ_k is kept apart from the ES step length σ^ES_k, and offspring are x_k + σ_k d^i_k.
  - **Sufficient decrease**, in one of three versions (p. 6):
    - **mean/mean** (3): f(x^trial_{k+1}) ≤ f(x_k) − ρ(σ_k);
    - **max/max** (4): the worst selected parent sufficiently decreases the previous worst;
    - **max/mean** (5): the worst selected parent sufficiently decreases f at the previous mean.

  A fourth combination was dropped because of "The difficulty in proving global convergence" (p. 5).

  The step-size rule (p. 6):
  - on success, x_{k+1} = x^trial_{k+1} and σ_{k+1} ≥ σ_k, e.g. max{σ_k, σ^ES_k}, which switches back to the ES step whenever it is larger;
  - on failure, x_{k+1} = x_k and σ_{k+1} = β̄_k σ_k.

  What is kept: offspring sampling from C_k, selection, recombination, and the ES updates of σ^ES, C and the weights. Their exact form is "not relevant" to the analysis (pp. 4, 6).

  Safeguards added:
  - direction bounds d_min ≤ ‖d‖ ≤ d_max, called "very mild" (p. 5);
  - for the max versions, a weight condition (2), f(Σωỹ) ≤ Σω f(ỹ), which can always be met with ω = e₁ (p. 6).

  The modification brings some elitism into a non-elitist ES (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: print the unmodified algorithm and the modified one in the same format, so that readers can check the *minimal change* claim line by line (pp. 3–6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is bounded below and Lipschitz near the limit point x* (pp. 6–7). The theorems need:
  - **mean/mean** (Thm 4.1, p. 8): ‖a_k‖, with a_k = Σω^i d^i_k, must stay bounded away from zero as σ_k → 0, and the normalized a_k must be dense in the unit sphere along the refining subsequence K.
  - **max versions** (Thm 4.2, p. 10): the normalized d^i_k must be dense for each i.

  The authors concede that density along a refining subsequence, rather than along the whole sequence, is a strengthening that "seems necessary" for directional methods (p. 9). They justify it probabilistically (p. 9):
  - with fixed weights and Gaussian d^i_k, a_k is Gaussian;
  - every spherical cap then has probability at least η > 0, which gives density with probability one;
  - resampling whenever ‖a_k‖ < ε keeps this property.

  **Theory–implementation gap, disclosed.** The weight update that enforces (2), which the max/max and max/mean theory needs, "was not included in the runs" (p. 11). The reasons given are to change CMA-ES as little as possible, and that the update had no visible impact, "perhaps due to the convexity near the solutions" (p. 11). The d_min/d_max safeguards were never triggered (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The analysis follows the standard directional direct-search chain:
  - **Lemma 4.1**: lim inf σ_k = 0, by contradiction, version by version (pp. 6–7). For max/mean, condition (2) turns decrease of the worst parent into decrease at the mean.
  - **Lemma 4.2**: a refining subsequence exists (p. 7).
  - **Theorem 4.1**: a Clarke analysis along a_k (p. 8). Write x^trial = x_k + σ_k a_k. An unsuccessful step gives f(x_k + σ_k a_k) > f(x_k) − ρ(σ_k). Normalize by σ_k‖a_k‖. The Lipschitz correction r_k ≤ ν‖a_k/‖a_k‖ − d‖ and ρ(σ_k)/(σ_k‖a_k‖) → 0 then give f°(x*; d) ≥ 0.
  - **Theorem 4.2 (max versions)**: backtrack to the last successful iteration k − p_k − 1 (pp. 9–10). During unsuccessful iterations x^µ and x do not move. Chaining the failed max test with condition (2) at that past iteration gives f(x_k + σ_k d^{i_k}_k) > f(x_k) − ρ(σ_k), and the mean/mean proof takes over.

  There are no rates and no complexity results (checked pp. 1–19).
  - Transferable technique: if the acceptance test compares quantities from different iterations (e.g. the worst offspring against the previous worst), keep them fixed during unsuccessful iterations. Then chain back to the last success to recover a standard *no sufficient decrease at x_k along d* inequality (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Base heuristic**: CMA-ES, chosen because it "appeared well ranked" among the stochastic solvers in Rios–Sahinidis (p. 10).
  - **Parameters**: all CMA-ES parameters follow Hansen's tutorial, e.g. λ = 4 + ⌊3 log n⌋ and µ = ⌊λ/2⌋. The added ones are σ0 = 1, ρ(σ) = 10⁻⁴σ², β1 = β2 = 0.5 and σ_{k+1} = max{σ_k, σ^CMA-ES_k} (p. 11).
  - **Test set**: the 53 Moré–Wild problems, in four versions: smooth, nonstochastic noisy, piecewise smooth and stochastic noisy (p. 12).
  - **Data profiles**: budget 50n, justified by expensive functions, at accuracies 10⁻³ and 10⁻⁷ (pp. 12–13).
  - **Performance profiles**: f* taken from 500000-evaluation runs of the four CMA-ES variants, at accuracies 10⁻² and 10⁻⁴, capped at 1500 evaluations (p. 13).

  Ablation of the three versions (pp. 13–14): mean/mean is best, and max/mean performs poorly, "in particular for unimodal functions". Only the smooth class is shown; the other classes are said to "follow a similar trend".

  Against pure CMA-ES and MADS (NOMAD 3.6.1 with DISABLE MODELS) (pp. 14–16):
  - MADS "exhibits a slightly better performance" in the data profiles (small budgets);
  - mean/mean matches MADS in efficiency and is more robust in the performance profiles;
  - mean/mean beats pure CMA-ES, except on piecewise-smooth problems, where pure CMA-ES is the most robust.

  Global-optimization check (pp. 16–18):
  - 19 multimodal BBOB functions: 10 noiseless (f15–f24) and 9 noisy (f122–f130);
  - n = 10 and 20, λ = 2n or 10n, 20 random starts, medians reported;
  - runs stop at f* + 10⁻⁷, at 250000 evaluations, or when σ_k < 10⁻¹⁰. The authors disclose that the last rule may restrict the global search (p. 17).

  Outcome: pure CMA-ES "behaves slightly better", especially with larger populations, and both mostly fail to find the global minimizer. The authors suggest multistart (pp. 17–18). No code release is mentioned (checked pp. 1–22).
  - Transferable technique: benchmark a globalized heuristic twice. First, run a local-solver collection with data and performance profiles to measure the efficiency gained. Second, run a multimodal suite with medians over random starts to check that the heuristic's original strength, global exploration, was not lost (pp. 12–18).
- **D6 Positioning**: On the ES side, the paper cites Rechenberg, Beyer–Schwefel, Hansen et al., and Hansen–Ostermeier for CMA-ES. It contrasts itself with the asymptotic (1, λ)-ES analyses of Auger, Greenwood–Zhu and Jägersküpper (p. 2).

  On the DFO side, it explains why MADS does not fit: "random sampling in these ES’s would have to be discrete rather than continuous" for an integer lattice (p. 2). It therefore follows the sufficient-decrease line: Vicente–Custódio [29], frame-based methods (Coope–Price) and GSS (Kolda–Lewis–Torczon) (pp. 2–3, 8).

  The tone toward ES is constructive, not critical. The aim is to "improve the efficiency and rigorousness of a widely used evolution strategy" (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Framing**: the paper is framed as a single question (p. 4).
  - **Motivation by analogy**: step-size control is motivated by Newton's method without a line search (p. 4).
  - **Assumptions discussed after the theorem**: the discussion follows Theorem 4.1 and includes a probabilistic justification of density (p. 9).
  - **Candour about proofs**: the difficulty of proving a variant is openly given as the reason for dropping it (p. 5).
  - **Measured numerical claims**: e.g. "roughly the same" (p. 16) and "slightly better" (p. 17).
  - **Abstract consistent with results**: the abstract claims the efficiency comes "without weakening significantly" global performance (p. 1). That matches §5.5, where pure CMA-ES is only slightly better (p. 17).
  - **Naming slip**: the text twice writes "mean/max" for the max/mean version (pp. 5, 11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an algorithmic modification that applies to a whole class of ES, a global convergence theory, and a numerical study.

  Stated limits and future work (pp. 18–19):
  - only CMA-ES was tested;
  - the interplay with CMA's covariance and step-length updates "must be better understood";
  - CMA's implicit first- and second-order information is not used;
  - a search step with quadratic models and the constrained case are left to a forthcoming paper.

  Limits visible but not stated: there is no complexity or rate result, and the max-version theory is not what was run (weight condition (2), p. 11).

**Method links**:
- **Method 1 ✅ strong evidence.** The heuristic's own generator is kept intact. The guarantee comes from a separate step size, sufficient decrease, and contraction on failure (pp. 4–6).

### S041 · On the optimal order of worst case complexity of direct search (Optimization Letters 2016, DOI 10.1007/s11590-015-0908-1)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M. Dodangeh (Coimbra PhD student, FCT scholarship) and Z. Zhang (CERFACS-IRIT, Toulouse) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the third step of the worst-case complexity line:
1. Vicente 2013, O(n²ε⁻²) (ref. [20]);
2. Dodangeh–Vicente, the convex case, O(n²ε⁻¹) (ref. [9] = S046);
3. this paper (p. 2).

It ends by pointing to probabilistic descent (GRVZ, ref. [14], p. 8), which the reference list gives as "to appear" (p. 9). **Text read**: preprint dated 14 May 2015, pp. 1–9, read in full.

**Extraction note**: Theorem 4.1 (p. 6) is rendered as `|D| / cm(D)2 ≥ c n2`, i.e. c n². The same bound appears elsewhere in two other forms:
- the introduction renders it as `mκ−2 ≥ c n` (p. 2);
- Theorem 4.2 renders it as `c n−2` followed by `ϵ−2` (p. 8).

I read both as rendering or typesetting slips for c n² (and c n²ε⁻²). Reasons: Theorem 4.1, its proof (p. 8), and the abstract's statement that the factor n² is optimal (p. 1). The published version was not checked.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The existing bounds, O(n²ε⁻²) in the nonconvex case and O(n²ε⁻¹) in the convex case, were derived with D⊕ = [I −I] (p. 1). The question is whether n² is an artefact of that choice.
  - **Entry move**: rewrite the known bounds in the general form O(mκ⁻²ε⁻ᵃ), where m bounds the PSS size and κ bounds the cosine measure from below (p. 2).
  - **The question becomes an optimization over positive spanning sets**: min |D|/cm(D)² (problem (6), p. 5).
  - **Worked trade-off (p. 5)**:

    | Poll set | Directions | cm | Resulting factor |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | minimal uniform basis | n+1 | 1/n | n³ |
    | D⊕ | 2n | 1/√n | n² |
    | any PSS | n⁴ | | at least n⁴ |
  - Transferable technique: to ask whether a complexity bound is tight in a parameter, first rewrite it so the parameter enters only through a design choice (here |D|·cm(D)⁻²). *Is n² optimal?* then becomes a well-posed optimization over designs (pp. 2, 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: There is no new algorithm. Algorithm 2.1 is standard sufficient-decrease directional direct search, as in the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente book (pp. 2–3). The argument runs in three steps:
  1. **Equivalence lemma (Lemma 4.1, pp. 6–7).** If D is a unit PSS with cosine measure κ, then caps of geodesic radius arccos κ centred at its vectors cover the sphere. cm(D) is exactly the largest κ for which this holds, so the paper calls this "an equivalent definition" of the cosine measure in covering language (p. 7).
  2. **Imported covering bound (Lemmas 4.2–4.3, pp. 7–8).** A known result from discrete geometry gives cm(D) ≤ ζ√(n⁻¹ log(n⁻¹|D|)).
  3. **Conclusion (Thm 4.1).** Hence |D|/cm(D)² ≥ c n² for every PSS, so D⊕ is optimal up to a constant (pp. 5–8).

  In 2-D, five directions at uniform angles are optimal (p. 5), as Fig. 1 illustrates (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The standing assumptions (pp. 3–4) are:
  - **Assumption 3.1**: f is bounded below and C¹ with a ν-Lipschitz gradient.
  - **Assumption 3.2**: ρ(α) = Cα^p.
  - **Assumption 3.3**: cm(D_k) ≥ κ, |D_k| ≤ m, and direction norms lie in [µ1, µ2].
  - The search step costs at most a multiple of m evaluations.

  p = 2 is fixed, as the known optimal choice (p. 5).

  **Scope of "optimal" (pp. 5, 8).** Theorem 4.2 bounds from below the *expression* mκ^{−p̂}ε^{−p̂} that appears in the upper bounds. It shows that no choice of PSS improves the n-order of those bounds. It is not an oracle lower bound on the evaluations that any direct-search run must use. The abstract's own wording is "no other positive spanning set will yield a better order of n" (p. 1).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The whole proof consists of three short lemmas:
  1. **Reformulation**: cosine measure → spherical covering, with a one-paragraph proof (pp. 6–7).
  2. **Imported theorem**: Tikhomirov (2015) for n+1 ≤ m ≤ 2n, and earlier results for m ≥ 2n (Bárány–Füredi, Böröczky, Bourgain–Lindenstrauss–Milman, Carl–Pajor, Gluskin) (p. 7).
  3. **Elementary inequality**: log x ≤ x − 1, which gives √(n⁻¹ log(n⁻¹|D|)) ≤ n⁻¹|D|^{1/2} (p. 8).

  The complexity bounds themselves are quoted from [20] and [9] (Thm 3.1 and Cor. 3.1, pp. 4–5).

  **Interpretation of κ (p. 4).** In the worst case, the best poll direction makes an angle of arccos κ with −∇f, so κ⁻² is the price of missing gradient information. The factor n_b in randomized block coordinate descent (Nesterov's RCDM) is said to arise "for similar reasons".
  - Transferable technique: look for an equivalent definition of your key constant in another field's language (here, the largest cap radius that still covers). Then import that field's extremal bounds instead of proving them yourself (pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. Figure 1 plots |D|/cm(D)² for uniform 2-D PSSs with |D| = 3, …, 10, against n² and against the D⊕ value 8 (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Own prior work**: the paper builds on [20] and [9].
  - **Credit given precisely, with its restriction**: Konečný–Richtárik are credited for rederiving the bounds "in a simpler way, but for a restricted version of direct search where step size increases are not allowed" (p. 2).
  - **Discrete geometry acknowledged generously**: a footnote thanks Böröczky for pointing to the references (p. 7). The acknowledgements thank four discrete geometers for discussions (p. 9).
  - **Randomized comparison**: the paper ends by comparing with GRVZ probabilistic descent. That method needs O(mnε⁻²) evaluations with m random directions, which beats the proven optimal deterministic order when m ≪ n (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Length**: nine pages (pp. 1–9).
  - **Abstract**: states the result and the proof idea: "The proof is based on an observation that reveals the connection between cosine measure in positive spanning and sphere covering" (p. 1).
  - **Question in the text**: the guiding question is asked outright: "An important question is how to maintain such a balance in order to have the algorithms exhibiting optimal overall measures of performance?" (p. 4).
  - **Open problems** (p. 8), each with its status:
    - problem (6) itself is open;
    - problem (12), finding the most economical covering by m caps, is "widely open";
    - D⊕ is proved optimal for m = 2n only when n = 3 or 4, and the question is open for n ≥ 5.
  - **Conjecture phrasing**: "it is intuitive to conjecture" (p. 8).
  - **Closing move**: the closing sentence turns the negative result into an argument for randomization (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is purely theoretical: the n² factor is optimal for PSS-based bounds. The limits are listed as open problems (p. 8). Also visible: the result concerns the bound, not the algorithm (see D3), and it covers deterministic PSS polling only. The implied future direction is randomized polling (p. 8).

### S046 · Worst case complexity of direct search under convexity (Mathematical Programming 2016, DOI 10.1007/s10107-014-0847-0)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M. Dodangeh (Coimbra PhD student) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the second step of the worst-case complexity line, after Vicente 2013 (ref. [17]). S041 cites it as [9]. **Text read**: preprint dated 18 November 2014, pp. 1–24, read in full. The extraction has lost the ε glyph throughout (e.g. `O(−2)`), and this card restores it from context.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry comes from the gradient-method yardstick:
  - under convexity, steepest descent improves from O(ε⁻²) to O(ε⁻¹) and gains a 1/k rate in f (Nesterov) (pp. 2–4);
  - Nesterov's random Gaussian DFO method reaches O(ε⁻¹) only in expectation (p. 2).

  The question is whether a *deterministic* zero-order method can match this. Direct search is chosen because it "offers a simple and instructive setting to answer such a question" (p. 2).

  A second target is the strongly convex case: a global r-linear rate for the whole sequence. This improves the local r-linear result of Dolan–Lewis–Torczon, which covers unsuccessful iterates only (pp. 2–3, 19).
  - Transferable technique: pick the next complexity target by finding a class where the gradient method's bound improves, while the only DFO result is weaker in kind (here, in expectation rather than deterministic) (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The algorithm is essentially unchanged: the standard Algorithm 3.1, with a PSS poll and sufficient decrease ρ = Cα^p (p. 5). The only algorithmic addition is an optional cap on the step size, α_k ≤ M (Assumption 3.2). It is needed only when p ≠ 2 and is "not a strong assumption at all" (pp. 6–7).

  The paper also discusses parameter choices (p. 6):
  - a larger C gives fewer successes and possibly wasted unsuccessful iterations;
  - a larger γ raises the number of unsuccessful iterations relative to successful ones: "Setting γ = 1 leads to an optimal choice in this respect."

  The novelty lies entirely in the analysis. It reproduces, for direct search, Nesterov's convex argument, which §2 recaps first (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Smoothness and convexity**: f is convex and C¹ with a ν-Lipschitz gradient on an open convex set containing the level set (Assumption 4.2, p. 8).
  - **Poll sets**: the PSSs have cm ≥ cm_min and bounded norms (Assumption 3.1, p. 6).
  - **Assumption 4.1 (p. 7)**: the solution set is nonempty, and R = sup_{y ∈ L_f(x0)} dist(y, X*) < ∞. This holds under bounded level sets or strong convexity. The paper shows that f(x, y) = y² satisfies it without either (p. 7). It also gives a pathological convex function, √(x² + y²) − x, for which it fails; that function is nonsmooth at the origin (p. 23).
  - **Why Assumption 4.1 is needed**: for the gradient method, ‖x_k − x*‖ ≤ ‖x0 − x*‖ comes for free, but "The lack of knowledge of the gradient" makes this harder to control in direct search (pp. 7–8).
  - **Strongly convex results**: these assume p = 2 (Assumption 5.1, p. 16).
  - **Constants**: they depend on dmin, dmax, cm_min, C, p, β1, β2, γ, α0 and the lower bound of f (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The skeleton of the 1/k rate proof (Theorem 4.1, pp. 8–12):
  1. **Gradient bound at failures.** At an unsuccessful iteration, ‖∇f‖ ≤ ω_g⁻¹ α^{min(1,p−1)} (Cor. 3.1, p. 7, from the cosine-measure bound (4)).
  2. **Charging successes to failures.** Go back from any successful iteration to the nearest earlier unsuccessful one. The step-size rules then give each success a decrease of at least ω‖∇f(x_{k_i})‖^{p̂}, where k_i is that unsuccessful iteration. A run of successes after k_i therefore decreases f by at least (number of successes)·ω‖∇f‖^{p̂} (eqs. (8)–(9), pp. 8–9).
  3. **Convexity.** Convexity plus R gives Δf_i ≤ R‖∇f(x_{k_i})‖ (eq. (10), p. 9).
  4. **Reciprocal telescoping.** Divide by Δf_iΔf_{i−1} and sum over 1/Δf (eq. (12), p. 9). This is Nesterov's device, applied over the indices of unsuccessful iterations.
  5. **Counting failures.** Unsuccessful iterations are counted by the logarithm of the step-size ratio: |U| ≤ ω1|S| + ω2 + log_{β2}(…) (Lemma 4.2, pp. 10–11).
  6. **Absorbing the log.** The log term is absorbed with ln x ≤ x (eq. (20), p. 12).

  **Gradient bound (Theorem 4.2, p. 15).** Lemma 4.3 splits the successful iterations at a midpoint l (pp. 13–14). It combines the 1/k decrease up to l with the gradient-norm decrease after l. This mirrors the gradient-method sum over k..2k recapped in §2 (p. 4).

  **Evaluations.** Only cm_min depends on n, so 1/ω ≤ O(n^{p̂/2}), and a poll costs O(n) evaluations. The result is O(n^{(p̂+2)/2}ν^{p̂}ε^{1−p̂}) evaluations, which is O(n²ν²ε⁻¹) for p = 2 (Cor. 4.1, pp. 15–16).

  **Strong convexity (pp. 16–19).**
  - The inequality ‖∇f‖² ≥ 2µ(f − f*) (25) turns each success into a contraction by (1 − 2ωµ) (Lemma 5.1, p. 17).
  - Log counting of unsuccessful iterations (Lemma 5.2) then gives r-linear rates in f and in ‖x_k − x*‖ for the whole sequence (Thm 5.1, p. 18).
  - The comparison with Dolan–Lewis–Torczon lists three improvements: the rate covers the whole sequence, needs only C¹, and allows step increases (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: to port a gradient-method rate proof to an adaptive-step DFO method, run the gradient argument only over unsuccessful iterations, where ‖∇f‖ = O(α). Charge each successful iteration to the nearest earlier unsuccessful one. Bound the number of unsuccessful iterations by the logarithm of the step-size ratio (pp. 8–11).
- **D5 Experiments**: All results are average evaluation counts over 20 runs from x0 = 0, at accuracies 2^{−(r+7)}, r = 2..9 (pp. 19–21).

  **Quadratic test (Tables 1–2).** The problem is Nesterov's quadratic (34), with n = 2, 4, 8, 16. The two solvers are set up as follows:
  - direct search with D⊕, γ = 1, halving, ρ = 10⁻³t² and random poll ordering;
  - Nesterov's random Gaussian method, with its theoretical step and λmax.

  Direct search is far better: at n = 16 and the tightest accuracy it needs 12613 evaluations against 615513 (Tables 1–2, pp. 20–21). Variants that did not help (central differences, h_k = 1/(k+1)) are mentioned (p. 20).

  **Two-point Gaussian (Duchi et al., Alg. 6.2).** It is given the exact r0 and ν. The authors call these choices "unrealistic in DFO" (p. 21) and "a huge advantage" (p. 22). It beats one-point Gaussian but loses to direct search on the quadratic (Table 3, p. 22).

  **CUTEr test (Tables 4–5).** Five convex CUTEr problems with n = 8 (pp. 22–23). The text says direct search "performs overall much better" (p. 22). The tables also show two-point Gaussian needing fewer evaluations on arglinc at every accuracy (179–224 against 195–235). The text does not mention this.

  There are no profiles, and no code release is mentioned (checked pp. 1–24).
  - Transferable technique: when the competitor's theory needs oracle constants (Lipschitz constant, distance to the solution set), give it the exact values, say this is "a huge advantage", and compare anyway. A win then cannot be blamed on poor tuning of the competitor (pp. 21–22).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the worst-case complexity (WCC) literature (p. 2):
  - Nesterov's bounds for the gradient method, and Cartis–Gould–Toint showing their sharpness;
  - Cartis–Gould–Toint's finite-difference adaptive cubic overestimation, O(n²ε^{−3/2});

- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:

**Transferable techniques**:
- Recap the derivative-based proof you will mirror (the sum over k..2k, the 1/Δf telescoping) in its own short section. Then follow the same structure in the DFO proof (pp. 3–4, 9, 13–14).
- Split the successful iterations at a midpoint. Choose l so that the number of successes after l exceeds the number before it by at most one. The 1/k function-value rate bounds the first half, and the gradient-norm decrease bounds the second half (Lemma 4.3, pp. 13–14).
- Replace *bounded level set* with the weaker condition R = sup_{L_f(x0)} dist(·, X*) < ∞. Show one example where the weaker condition holds and the stronger one does not, and one example where even the weaker one fails (pp. 7, 23).
- Count unsuccessful iterations with a logarithm. From α_k ≤ α_{k0}γ^{|S|}β2^{|U|}, and a lower bound on α_j in terms of f(x_k) − f*, one gets |U| ≤ ω1|S| + ω2 + log_{β2}(·) (Lemma 4.2, pp. 10–11).

### S073 · Full-low evaluation methods for derivative-free optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2023, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2022.2142582; arXiv:2107.11908)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. S. Berahas (University of Michigan) and O. Sohab (Lehigh ISE) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**:
- From the Lehigh period; support from the Centre for Mathematics of the University of Coimbra is also acknowledged (p. 1).
- It combines two strands of Vicente's earlier work: probabilistic direct search (GRVZ, [34]) and fully linear models with criticality steps, from the DFO trust-region theory ([21, 22]) (pp. 12, 22).
- It names Vicente's own earlier hybrids (SID-PSM, MFN) among the pioneer examples of model/direct-search hybrids that lacked a comprehensive theory (p. 3).

**Text read**: arXiv v2 (28 Oct 2022; title page dated 31 October 2022), pp. 1–27, read in full, including all figures.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a diagnosis that each DFO paradigm suits only some problem types (pp. 2–3):
  - **Directional direct search** is robust, parallelizable and works on nonsmooth problems, but is slow with PSSs.
  - **Finite-difference (FD) quasi-Newton line search** is efficient on smooth problems but "no longer suitable" when f is nonsmooth.
  - **Model-based trust-region methods** are efficient on small smooth problems but scale badly and are hard to parallelize.
  - **Model/direct-search hybrids** give promising numerics, but "no comprehensive convergence theory" (p. 3).

  The goal is one rigorous class with "sustainedly good performance across all possible function types" (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: motivate a hybrid by tabulating where each existing paradigm wins and where it fails (smooth, nonsmooth, noisy, dimension, parallelism). Then define success as never being the worst across these regimes (pp. 2–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: There are two iteration types (Algorithm 1, p. 5):
  - **Full-Eval** (Algorithm 2, p. 6): a line search along p_k built from an approximate gradient g_k, with Armijo decrease (2.1) (p. 5);
  - **Low-Eval** (Algorithm 3, p. 6): one direct-search poll with sufficient decrease f(x_k + α_k d_k) ≤ f(x_k) − ρ(α_k) (2.3).

  **Switching from Full-Eval to Low-Eval.** The key coupling is condition (2.2): a Full-Eval iteration counts as successful only if its line-search step satisfies β ≥ γρ(α_k), where α_k is the *direct-search* step size. If backtracking violates (2.2), the Full-Eval step is abandoned and the method switches to Low-Eval (pp. 5–6). Full-Eval iterations do not change α_k (p. 6).

  **Switching back from Low-Eval to Full-Eval.** This happens once the number of consecutive unsuccessful Low-Eval iterations reaches the number of backtracks in the last Full-Eval iteration (pp. 6, 14).

  **Practical instance (pp. 13–14)**:
  - forward FD gradient with h = √(machine precision);
  - BFGS, skipping the update unless sᵀy ≥ 10⁻¹⁰‖s‖‖y‖;
  - β̄ = 1, τ = 0.5, γ = 1, ρ(α) = min(10⁻⁵, 10⁻³α²);
  - Low-Eval polls D_k = [d, −d] with d uniform on the sphere, and λ = 1/θ = 2.

  The labels refer to the amount of algorithmic effort per iteration, not to the fidelity of a surrogate (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Smooth case (pp. 7, 12–13)**:
    - f is C¹ with an L-Lipschitz gradient (Assumption 3.1).
    - The gradient error satisfies ‖∇f(x_k) − g_k‖ ≤ u_g β_k‖g_k‖ (Assumption 3.2, p. 7).
    - For FD gradients this is enforced by a criticality-step loop (Algorithm 4), which shrinks h_k until h_k ≤ u′_g β‖∇_h f‖. The loop terminates finitely unless ∇f(x_k) = 0 (Prop. 3.1, pp. 12–13).
    - The direction satisfies an angle condition, cos(−g_k, p_k) > κ, and ‖g_k‖ ≤ u_p‖p_k‖ (Assumption 3.3, p. 7).
  - **Nonsmooth case (pp. 10–11)**: the analysis assumes "Full-Eval Failure" (Assumption 3.4): ‖g_k‖ > g > 0 on successful Full-Eval iterations (Assumption 3.4). It also needs a forcing function, bounded iterates, f Lipschitz near x*, and dense refining directions.
  - **Noise**: "Although we do not consider the noisy case explicitly in our theory" (p. 2). Later the text says the noisy case "will be addressed within the non-smooth analysis" (p. 6), but no noise-specific result follows (checked §3).
  - **Theory–implementation gap, visible but not stated as a gap**: the implementation fixes h_k at √eps (p. 13), and the text does not say that Algorithm 4 is run.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Smooth case, step-size bound (Lemma 3.1, pp. 7–8)**: a Taylor expansion, plus the failure of the previous backtrack β_k/τ, gives β_k ≥ min(β̄, 2τ(1−c)(−g_kᵀp_k)/((2u_g u_p + L)‖p_k‖²)).
  - **Smooth case, rate (Theorem 3.1, p. 8)**: each successful Full-Eval iteration decreases f by at least M‖g_k‖². Hence min‖∇f(x_i)‖ ≤ const/√(ns_k), where ns_k counts *successful Full-Eval iterations only*.
  - **Backtracks**: under an extra condition, the number of backtracks per iteration is O(1) (p. 9).
  - **Strong convexity**: the PL inequality gives linear decrease in ns_k (pp. 9–10).
  - **Nonsmooth case (Lemma 3.2, pp. 10–11)**: condition (2.2) together with ‖g_k‖ > g makes each successful Full-Eval iteration decrease f by at least c(κ/u_p)γ g ρ(α_k). Full-Eval steps are thus "essentially considered as search steps" of the direct search (p. 10). Summability then forces α_k → 0 along a subsequence of unsuccessful Low-Eval iterations.
  - **Nonsmooth case, stationarity (Theorem 3.2, pp. 11–12)**: the standard refining-direction Clarke argument.
  - **Slips**: the proof of Lemma 3.2 writes τ where the direct-search contraction θ is meant, and lists I_SL twice in an index union (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: to combine a fast method with a convergent one, require the fast method's accepted steps to be at least γρ(α), a function of the convergent method's step size. Its successes then count as sufficient-decrease search steps in the convergent method's proof (pp. 5, 10–11).

**Method links**:
- **Method 1 ✅ evidence.** An efficient generator (FD-BFGS) keeps its own step, and convergence in the hard case comes from a direct-search poll with sufficient decrease. The paper states that Full-Eval steps are "essentially considered as search steps" (p. 10). Step 6 (ablation against the raw heuristic) is done: BFGS-FD alone is one of the baselines (pp. 14, 17–22).
- **Method 1 ⚠ variant.** The *heuristic* here has its own theory: a gradient-descent-type rate whenever enough Full-Eval steps succeed (p. 8). The coupling is also different. It is not a reset rule (heuristic step ≥ α) but a floor on the heuristic step, β ≥ γρ(α), whose violation triggers a switch (pp. 5–6).
- **Method 3 ✅ evidence (practice).** The implemented Low-Eval is probabilistic direct search with [d, −d], at most 2 evaluations per iteration, chosen for its "superior performance" over PSS polling (p. 13).
- **Method 3 ⚠ (theory).** The stated theory (Theorem 3.2) is a density argument. The text connects the random [d, −d] choice neither to its density hypothesis nor to probabilistic descent (pp. 11, 13).
- **Method 2 ⚠ variant.** The rate is stated per successful Full-Eval iteration and set against gradient descent (p. 8), but it is not converted into total function evaluations (pp. 8–9).
- **Method 5 ✅ evidence**: problem collections, performance and data profiles, component ablations, and losses reported class by class (pp. 15–22).
- **Method 5 ⚠ (step 5).** SKILL.md says *the Lehigh papers* ship GitHub code, but this Lehigh paper's arXiv text mentions no code or repository.
- **New-pattern candidate: mode-recurrence case analysis.** The smooth-case results hold if infinitely many Full-Eval iterations succeed. The nonsmooth results hold under "Full-Eval Failure" (Assumption 3.4), with Low-Eval driving α → 0 (pp. 6–11). S049 is a second instance.
- **Existing candidates, with new evidence:**
  - *iteration taxonomy for complexity*: iterations are partitioned into I_SF, I_SL, I_UF and I_UL, and the nonsmooth proof handles each class in turn (pp. 4, 10–11);
  - *idealized theorem + practical algorithm with the gap written out*: the abstract separates "For the theory" from "In practice" (p. 1), though the h_k and criticality-step gap is not written out (p. 13);
  - *state where you lose*: DFO-TR and NOMAD each win classes (pp. 17–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A coupling condition, β_LS ≥ γρ(α_DS): accept a line-search step only if it is not tiny relative to the direct-search forcing function; otherwise abandon it and switch. This single inequality is what lets the convergence proof treat line-search successes as direct-search search steps (pp. 5, 10–11).
- An effort-matched switching rule: return to the expensive mode once the cheap mode has had as many consecutive failures as the expensive mode's last backtracks (pp. 6, 14).
- A criticality-step loop for FD gradients: shrink h to ω^j u′_g β‖∇_h f^{(0)}‖ until h ≤ u′_g β‖∇_h f^{(j)}‖. This gives ‖∇f − g‖ ≤ u_g β‖g‖, and termination is proved by contradiction (pp. 12–13).
- A BFGS safeguard for nonconvex DFO: skip the update unless sᵀy ≥ 10⁻¹⁰‖s‖‖y‖. Initialize H0 = (yᵀs/yᵀy)I only if the first iteration is Full-Eval (p. 13).
- Test the same 53 problems under four noise models (additive or multiplicative, deterministic or stochastic). Then check whether the ranking changes at noise levels 10⁻² and 10⁻⁴ (pp. 16, 22).
