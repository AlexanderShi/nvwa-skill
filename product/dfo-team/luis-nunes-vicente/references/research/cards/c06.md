### S017 · The stochastic multi-gradient algorithm for multi-objective optimization and its application to supervised machine learning (Annals of Operations Research 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10479-021-04033-z, arXiv 1907.04472)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Liu (Lehigh ISE; Vicente is at Lehigh ISE and CMUC Coimbra) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This paper belongs to the Lehigh period.
- It is the theory companion of S016 (CMS 2022). S016 imports its rates by citation, and S017 itself points forward to the fairness study as "[36]" (p. 19).
- It extends the deterministic multi-gradient rates of Fliege–Vaz–Vicente (S015, cited as [25], pp. 3, 8) to stochastic gradients.

**Read**: full text, including Appendix A (proof of Theorem 5.3, pp. 26–27) and Appendix B (definitions of the Purity, Γ and Δ metrics, pp. 27–28). The reference list (pp. 28–30) was scanned for self-citations.

**Year note**: the batch list gives 2024. SKILL.md (Method 4 evidence) and S016 cite the paper as 2021. The published version was not seen.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is a gap where two well-understood methods meet. The paper says SG is "well studied and understood" for one objective and deterministic multi-gradient descent is "well understood" for MOO, "However, little is known yet about stochastic multi-gradient descent" (p. 5). The predecessor that introduced and analyzed SMG, [43] (EJOR 2018, listed with authors M. Quentin, P. Fabrice and J. A. Désidéri, p. 30), is criticized on two counts:
  - it missed a critical property of the direction and therefore needed "strong and unnatural assumptions" (p. 5);
  - it had no Pareto-front version of the method (p. 5).

  The problem is stated formally as the SMOO problem (4): min F(x) = (E[f₁(x,w)], …, E[f_m(x,w)]) over x ∈ X (p. 4). That statement comes after a long tour of the background:
  - a posteriori vs a priori MOO, and three concrete drawbacks of scalarization: weights are hard to preselect, even weights give uneven fronts, and nonconvex parts are missed (pp. 2–3);
  - population heuristics without theory (p. 3);
  - SAA vs SA for a single objective (p. 4).

  SMOO methods are sorted into two families: "multi-objective methods" reduce SMOO to a deterministic MOO problem, and "stochastic methods" aggregate it into a single stochastic objective (p. 5). The paper restricts itself to the first family by assuming that the random variables of different objectives are independent (p. 5). The pull from applications is supervised learning (pp. 4, 19).
  - Transferable technique: Enter at the product of two mature methods (SG × multi-gradient). State in one sentence what is unknown about the combination, then name the one property the predecessor missed (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper starts from the deterministic base method.
  - **Base method**: the steepest common descent QP (7) and its dual (8). The direction d = −Σλ_i∇h_i is the negative of the minimum-norm element of the convex hull of the gradients, with λ in the simplex Δ_m (p. 7).
  - **SMG**: sampled gradients g_i(x_k,w_k) are fed into the same dual QP (10). This gives the stochastic multi-gradient g = Σλ^g_i g_i (11) and the step x_{k+1} = P_X(x_k − α_k g) (Algorithm 1, p. 8).
  - **Kept from SG**: the step form, the step sizes α_k, projection onto a closed convex X, and the absence of a stopping test. The paper says "there is also no good stopping criterion" (p. 8).
  - **Changed**: only the direction, which is now the output of a QP solved on noisy inputs (p. 8).
  - **Key insight**: the QP solution "acts as a mapping" on the unbiased stochastic gradients. The mapped direction is therefore biased with respect to both E[∇ₓS(x,λ^g)] and the true multi-gradient ∇ₓS(x,λ) (p. 9). The bias vanishes only in the full-batch regime (p. 9; Fig. 2, p. 10).
  - **Design consequence**: tolerate a bias proportional to α_k and enforce that bound by increasing the batch size (pp. 6, 17).

  PF-SMG (Algorithm 2, p. 18) keeps a list of nondominated points. Each iteration does three things:
  1. It adds r perturbed points around each list point.
  2. From each point it runs p independent SMG trajectories of q steps. Stochasticity makes the endpoints differ (p. 18).
  3. It removes dominated points. Only the new points are compared, a device credited to DMS [13] (p. 18).

  In the implementation the perturbation is applied only to the point pairs at the m largest "holes" along each objective axis, to stop the list from growing too fast (p. 19). The benchmark PF-MG is the same algorithm with deterministic multi-gradient steps and p = 1 (p. 18).
  - Transferable technique: When you extend a method by plugging estimates into a subproblem, ask whether the subproblem's solution map is nonlinear. If it is, unbiased inputs give biased outputs, and the analysis must budget for that bias (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: Grow a Pareto-front list only at the largest gap per objective axis (p. 19).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **5.1**: each gradient is L_i-Lipschitz, so S(·,λ) = Σλ_i f_i is L-smooth with L = max L_i (p. 10).
  - **5.2**:
    - (a) unbiased individual gradients;
    - (b) a new first-moment bound, E‖g_i − ∇f_i‖ ≤ α_k(C_i + Ĉ_i‖∇f_i‖), which ties the error to the step size;
    - (c) variance ≤ G_i² + Ĝ_i²‖∇f_i‖² (p. 11).

    Part (b) is "needed to bound the amount of biasedness of the stochastic multi-gradient in terms of the true gradient norm and the stepsize" (p. 11). A simpler aggregate version would have sufficed for (14), but the per-objective form is needed later for the weights (p. 11).
  - **5.3**: X is bounded with diameter Θ, which is used to bound gradient norms (p. 12).
  - **5.4**: the QP solution λ is a Lipschitz function of the gradients (constant β). This is admitted as a convenience: it "follows locally from classical sensitivity theory but that we assume globally too" (p. 12).
  - **5.5**: strong convexity with c = min c_i (p. 13).
  - **5.7**: convexity, with S(·,λ) attaining a minimizer for every λ (p. 16).
  - **5.6**: ∇ₓS(x*,λ_k)ᵀ(x_k − x*) ≥ 0, i.e. λ_k "identifies well the optimal role of λ∗". It is admitted to be a "stronger assumption" (p. 6, p. 15).

  Further, the discussion after Theorem 5.1 assumes that the whole sequence λ_k converges (p. 15). The batch-size realization in §5.3 additionally assumes Gaussian gradients N(∇f_i, σ_i²I_n) (p. 17). In the synthetic experiments noise is added to the variables. The authors state that the stochastic gradients then "will not be unbiased estimates of the true gradients" (p. 22), so those runs fall outside Assumption 5.2(a).
  - Transferable technique: State the tolerated error in the units of the step size (error ≤ α_k·const). It then enters the SG recursion at the same α_k² order as the variance term (pp. 11, 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Skeleton**: the classical SG distance recursion (Nemirovski et al. [38]). Nonexpansiveness of the projection gives the expansion (21) of E‖x_{k+1} − x*‖² (p. 14).
  - **Null-term split**: the proof adds the "null term" 2α_k(E[∇ₓS(x_k,λ^g_k)] − E[∇ₓS(x_k,λ^g_k)] + ∇ₓS(x_k,λ_k) − ∇ₓS(x_k,λ_k)) (p. 14). This splits the cross term (22) into three parts:
    - (i) the ideal term ∇ₓS(x_k,λ_k)ᵀ(x_k − x*);
    - (ii) the multi-gradient bias;
    - (iii) the weight perturbation λ^g_k vs λ_k.
  - **Bounds on the parts**:
    - (ii) ≤ α_k L_∇S (16). This follows from Jensen and 5.2(b) (pp. 11–12).
    - (iii) ≤ α_k β L_∇S M_S (19). This uses the Lipschitz QP map (5.4) and the linearity of ∇ₓS in λ, with M_S = √(mn)(M_∇ + LΘ) (p. 13).
    - Both are multiplied by ‖x_k − x*‖ ≤ Θ, which puts them at order α_k².
    - The second moment of g is bounded by L_g² (17) through ‖Σλ_i g_i‖² ≤ mΣ‖g_i‖² (p. 12).
  - **Theorem 5.1 (strongly convex)**: take α_k = 2/(c(k+1)), multiply the recursion by s and telescope. This gives min_s E[S(x_s,λ_s)] − E[S(x*,λ̄_k)] ≤ (2L_g² + 4Θ(L_∇S + βL_∇SM_S))/(c(k+1)), with λ̄_k = Σsλ_s/Σs (pp. 13–15). The reference point x* can be "any point in X" (p. 13).
  - **Caveat the authors state**: because λ* is defined only at convergence, the left-hand side "is not even guaranteed to be positive" (p. 15).
  - **Theorem 5.2 (stronger version)**: Assumption 5.6 gives (26) and the contraction (1 − 2α_k c). With α_k = γ/k and the induction of [38, Eqs. (2.9)–(2.10)], E‖x_k − x*‖² = O(1/k), and the gap in S(·,λ*) is also O(1/k) (pp. 15–16).
  - **Theorem 5.3 (convex)**: α_k = ᾱ/√k and the standard Θ² telescoping give O(1/√k) against the simple average λ̄_k (p. 16; Appendix A, pp. 26–27). In this case λ̄_k also approaches λ* more slowly (p. 16).
  - **Footnote 2**: the footnote sets Assumption 5.6 against [43]'s Assumption H5, which asks for a.s. quadratic growth of the noisy values toward the nearest Pareto point. It shows that [43]'s key inequality would follow from (26) if g were unbiased (p. 15).
  - **§5.3, realizing the bias bound**: Var of a b_i-batch ≤ σ_i²/b_i, and E‖X‖ ≤ σ√n for Gaussian X. Hence the bias is at most Σσ_i√n/√b_i, and this is required to be ≤ α_k(…) (p. 17). The paper states the qualitative rule only: batch sizes should increase as the step sizes shrink (p. 17). My own algebra from that inequality gives b_i ≳ nσ_i²/α_k². The paper does not derive a total-sample count.
  - **Not analysed**: the nonconvex case (future work, p. 26) and any convergence of the PF-SMG front (no theorem in §6, pp. 17–18).
  - **Small inconsistencies in the extracted text**:
    - Theorem 5.2 states γ ≥ 1/(2c), while its proof uses γ > 1/(2c) and the bound contains (2cγ − 1)⁻¹ (pp. 15–16).
    - The first bound in the Theorem 5.2 statement shows M̄², while the proof shows M̄ (pp. 15–16).
    - Appendix A's inequality (32) writes E[S(x_k,λ_k)] inside a min over s (p. 27).
  - Transferable technique: The *null-term three-way split*: ideal term + estimator bias + weight perturbation. Each error is made O(α_k) and multiplied by the domain diameter Θ, so it joins the variance term at order α_k² and the SG template goes through unchanged (pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable technique: When the algorithm cannot know its limiting target (here λ*), prove a weak theorem against the iterates' averaged weights and state the sign caveat. Then prove a strong theorem under one explicitly named extra assumption (pp. 13–16).
- **D5 Experiments**: There are three experiments.
  - **Bias exhibit (§4, pp. 9–10)**:
    - Setup: n = 4 and m = 2, with finite sums of N = 3,000 gradients drawn from N(v_i, 0.2I₄), and 10,000 batches per batch size.
    - What is plotted: ‖E[error]‖ against batch size, measured relative to λ^g (Fig. 2a) and to the true λ (Fig. 2b).
    - Result: the bias decreases with batch size and vanishes at full batch.
  - **Fairness in logistic regression (§7.2, pp. 19–22)**:
    - Data: the LIBSVM sets heart, australian, svmguide3 and german.numer. Each is split by one binary feature, and each objective is one group's regularized logistic loss (28), with λ₁ = λ₂ = 10⁻³ (pp. 19–20).
    - Motivating baseline: single SG on the union exposes group accuracy gaps, e.g. svmguide3 at 79.4% vs 23.0% (Table 1, p. 20).
    - Reported: the PF-SMG fronts (Fig. 3, p. 21). Table 2 reports the per-group accuracy at five representative front points, the iterations, the list size and the CPU time (p. 21).
    - Against full-batch PF-MG: PF-MG needs 0.94, 2.5, 1.3 and 2.5 times PF-SMG's CPU time to reach approximately the same number of nondominated points (p. 20).
  - **Synthetic (§7.3, pp. 22–25)**:
    - Problems: 13 bound-constrained problems from the DMS collection [13], with m = 2, n from 1 to 30, and convex, concave, mixed and disconnected fronts (Table 3, p. 22).
    - Noise: x + w with w uniform over an interval 1/10 the length of the bound interval, built "the same as in [43]" (p. 22).
  - **Protocol**:
    - Both methods use the same 30 random starting points and q = 2. The step size is halved every 200 iterations, with initial step 0.3 on the synthetic set. Runs stop at 1000 iterations or 1500 list points (pp. 18–19, 23).
    - The new points per iteration are equalized: p = 2, r = 5 for PF-SMG vs p = 1, r = 10 for PF-MG (p. 19).
    - PF-SMG is run 10 times, and the run with the average Γ is reported, "To be fair" (p. 23).
    - Metrics are Purity, Γ and Δ (Appendix B, pp. 27–28), shown as performance profiles (Fig. 5, p. 24) and in Table 4 (p. 25).
  - **Losses are reported**: PF-MG has higher purity, "which is reasonable" (p. 24). Examples: FF1 0.630 vs 0.982, MOP2 0.841 vs 1.000 (p. 25). Spread is "comparable" (p. 24).
  - **Gaps in the protocol**:
    - Cost is counted in iterations and CPU time only, not in sampled gradients. The synthetic cost advantage is argued hypothetically ("suppose that the computational cost…", p. 24).
    - The text says PF-SMG "takes more iterations than PF-MG to achieve the same tolerance level" (p. 24). Yet Table 4's #Iter at termination is lower for PF-SMG in 8 of 13 problems, higher in 4 and equal in 1 (p. 25), and no tolerance level is defined.
    - The batch size PF-SMG used in §7, and whether the §5.3 rule was applied, are not stated. A grep for "batch" in §7 finds only PF-MG's full batch (p. 20).
    - The only baseline is the authors' own deterministic counterpart. NSGA-II, DMS and [43] are cited but not run (pp. 3, 18).
    - The text has no code-availability statement (grep).
  - Transferable technique: Before any theorem, show a new phenomenon with a cheap Monte Carlo figure: 10⁴ replications of ‖E[estimator − target]‖ against batch size (pp. 9–10).
  - Transferable technique: When comparing list-based front algorithms, equalize the number of new points added per iteration (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Lineage**: SG from Robbins–Monro [44] and the multi-gradient method of Fliege–Svaiter [24]. The deterministic rates come from Fliege–Vaz–Vicente [25] (pp. 3, 8). The standard SG analysis is taken from Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal [8] and Nemirovski et al. [38] (pp. 10, 16).
  - **Direct critique of the predecessor that introduced SMG, [43]**: it "failed to identify a critical property" (p. 5), and footnote 2 compares assumptions line by line (p. 15).
  - **Critique of population heuristics**: NSGA-II and AMOSA admit "no theoretical convergence properties … under reasonable assumptions" and are "slow in practice due to the lack of first-order principles" (p. 3).
  - **Critique of scalarization**: three listed drawbacks (pp. 2–3). The "stochastic methods" family is said to "inherit the drawbacks of a priori scalarizarion methods" (p. 5).
  - **Self-citations**: 3 of the 52 references.
    - DMS [13] is used four ways: as a rigorous derivative-free MOO method (p. 3), for the list-update device (p. 18), as the source of the test collection (p. 22) and for Δ with m ≥ 2 (p. 28).
    - [25] (pp. 3, 8).
    - The fairness report [36] (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: When you call a predecessor's assumption too strong, say in a footnote exactly how it relates to yours and what it would give if your missing property held (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Order**: The introduction reads like a short survey: deterministic MOO (§1.1) and stochastic single- and multi-objective optimization (§1.2), over pp. 2–5. Contributions come in §1.3 (p. 5).
  - **Framing**: The contribution sentence is modest: the paper contributes "by providing an understanding of the behavior and basic properties" of SMG (p. 5).
  - **The core finding (bias) recurs**: it is sentence 3 of the abstract (p. 1) and returns in §1.3 (pp. 5–6), §4 (p. 9) and the conclusions (p. 25).
  - **Phenomenon before theorem**: a separate section (§4) gives the intuition figure for n = m = 2 (Fig. 1, p. 9) and the Monte Carlo bias plot (Fig. 2, p. 10), before the convergence theory of §5.
  - **Theorems are interpreted**: each is followed by an interpretive paragraph that includes what it does not give (pp. 15, 16).
  - **Hedging**: moderate and specific: "Rigorously speaking" (p. 15), "perhaps due to" and "This might be due to" (p. 24). The conclusion restates the results and then lists concrete extensions (pp. 25–26).
  - **Typos surviving in v3**: "synthesis MOO problems" (p. 6), "founds applications" (p. 4), "scalarizarion" (p. 5), "repectively" (p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: Give the new phenomenon its own short section, with a picture and a simulation, between the algorithm and the theory. The unusual assumption (bias ∝ α_k) then reads as necessary rather than arbitrary (pp. 9–11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution layers**:
    - theory: the bias is identified, with rates O(1/k) under strong convexity and O(1/√k) under convexity for a weighted gap;
    - algorithm: PF-SMG;
    - application: fairness in binary classification (pp. 5–6, 25–26).
  - **Limits stated specifically**:
    - no stopping criterion (p. 8);
    - the λ*-rate needs the stronger Assumption 5.6 (pp. 6, 15);
    - the left-hand side may be negative (p. 15);
    - λ̄_k converges more slowly in the convex case (p. 16);
    - PF-SMG has lower purity (p. 24);
    - the synthetic noise makes the gradients biased (p. 22).
  - **Limits not stated**:
    - no theory for the PF-SMG front;
    - no sample-complexity count, although the batch has to grow;
    - the only baseline is the authors' own deterministic counterpart;
    - the synthetic experiments sit outside Assumption 5.2(a), and the paper does not connect this back to the theory (p. 22).
  - **Future work** (p. 26): noise/variance reduction for SMG, feasible regions other than boxes, a proximal term for nonsmooth regularizers, deep learning, and a nonconvex rate theory.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (with ✅ on steps 5–6).
  - Variant: the skeleton kept is SG (step, projection, diminishing α_k). The object that changes is the *direction subproblem's input* (true → sampled gradients) (p. 8). There is no acceptance test to generalize, since SG has none.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Null-term three-way split (ideal + bias + weight perturbation). Make every error O(α_k) and multiply by the domain diameter Θ so the SG template survives (pp. 13–14).
- [D4] If the limiting target is unknown, state the rate against the iterates' (weighted) average target and flag the sign issue. Then add one named assumption for the rate against the limit (pp. 13–16).
- [D3] Write a new noise assumption in step-size units (error ≤ α_k·const) and show how a batch-size rule enforces it (pp. 11, 17).
- [D5] A Monte Carlo bias plot (10⁴ replications per batch size) as evidence before the theory (pp. 9–10).
- [D5] Equalize the new points added per iteration when comparing list-based Pareto-front methods. Perturb only at the largest gap along each objective axis (p. 19).
- [D6] Put the line-by-line comparison with the predecessor's assumption in a footnote (p. 15).

### Batch synthesis (c06)

- **Method 4 needs a wording variant for gradient-type methods.** SG and multi-gradient methods have no acceptance test, so the object that changes is the *direction subproblem* (S017 p. 8). In S015 (c05) it was the success test (every component satisfying Armijo). A SKILL.md phrasing such as *change the smallest object (acceptance test or direction map)* would cover both.
- **Averaged-weight gap now appears in 2 papers** (S015 in c05; S017 pp. 13–16). Both flag the sign problem of the weighted gap and add an assumption about the weights to get the stronger statement (S017 pp. 15–16). One more paper would meet the card spec's ≥3 threshold.
- **Theory/application split confirmed from the theory side.** S017 carries the rates plus a first fairness demonstration and forwards to [36] for the detailed study (p. 19). S016 (c05) imports the rates by citation.
- **The Method 5 gap is on the theory side.** S017 reuses the DMS collection and profiles, but benchmarks only against its own deterministic counterpart and has no code statement (pp. 18, 22–25). The application paper S016 is the one that fully matches Method 5 (c05).
