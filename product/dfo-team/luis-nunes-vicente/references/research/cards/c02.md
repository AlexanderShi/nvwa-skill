# Paper cards · batch c02 (core)

Batch intent: See Vicente at the turn from derivative-based NLP to derivative-free optimization (2004–2007), on four fronts: filter globalization of primal-dual interior-point methods, pattern search driven by application-supplied points, bilevel optimization recast as multicriteria optimization, and particle swarm inside pattern search (PSwarm). Three questions. (a) How early does Method 1 (heuristic inside a convergent skeleton) appear, and with what glue: an integer-lattice mesh or sufficient decrease? (b) How does nondominance, the multicriteria idea behind DMS 2011 and Method 4, already shape his single-objective and bilevel work? (c) What experimental protocol comes before the Method 5 standard of solver, collection and profiles?
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all; core batch). Extra weight on D2 (what was kept from the base method and what was changed), D4 (proof skeleton and named devices) and D5 (comparison protocol).
Material roles: S007 core (Math. Program. 2004) · S025 core (SIAM J. Optim. 2004) · S022 core (JOTA 2006) · S005 core (J. Glob. Optim. 2007).

### S007 · A globally convergent primal-dual interior-point filter method for nonlinear programming (Mathematical Programming 2004, DOI 10.1007/s10107-003-0477-4)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M. Ulbrich, S. Ulbrich · **Position in trajectory**: This paper is from Vicente's derivative-based interior-point period, before his DFO work. His own prior interior-point local-convergence papers are cited as refs [24], [25] (p. 32). The manuscript is dated "April 2000. Revised February 2002 and March 2003" (p. 1). The collaboration started at the First Workshop on Nonlinear Optimization "Interior-Point and Filter Methods" in Coimbra, October 1999, and was largely done at Rice CRPC/CAAM (Acknowledgments, p. 31). **Read**: full text, including the appendix proofs of Lemmas 1, 2 and 13 (p. 27–31). Formulas are garbled (see caveat), so lemma statements are taken from their prose.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a *technique transfer into an open gap*. The Fletcher–Leyffer filter had "mostly" been applied to SLP/SQP, where it proved "very successful numerically" (p. 1–2). For interior-point methods in NLP, local convergence is well studied, but global convergence is open "because there seems to be no ideal merit function" (p. 2). The problem is a smooth NLP with equality constraints and nonnegativity bounds. The bounds are handled by the interior-point step (p. 2–3). The stated aim is to avoid merit functions and penalty-parameter updates (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The core is a primal-dual Newton step on the perturbed KKT system, with μ = σ·(complementarity measure), the El-Bakry et al. choice (p. 5–6). The key move is to split the *right-hand side* of the Newton system into two parts, (10)–(12) (p. 6–7):
  - a **normal step** toward the quasi-central path (feasibility plus centrality);
  - a **tangential step** that reduces the Lagrangian gradient and complementarity.

  The two filter coordinates follow the same split. The first is a quasi-centrality measure, which plays the role of feasibility. The second is an optimality (criticality) measure that *replaces the objective value* (p. 6, p. 10). The step lengths along the two components are min{1, Δ/‖s‖}-type rules, with the tangential length ≤ the normal length and Δ acting "comparable to the trust-region radius" (p. 7–8). The iterates are kept in a centrality neighbourhood (17) (p. 8–9). Algorithm 1 (p. 11–12) combines:
  - filter acceptability with a margin (Def. 3, p. 10);
  - a ratio test on the optimality measure against a quadratic model, which is skipped when the model decrease is small relative to θ; in that case the old iterate is added to the filter (p. 10–11);
  - restoration when θ is too large relative to (a power of) Δ (p. 11).

  Kept from the SQP-filter of Fletcher et al. [12]: the trust-region framework, the normal/tangential decomposition, restoration, and the rule for when to add entries (p. 2–4). Changed: the step comes from the primal-dual Newton system, and both filter components are new. The method "does not require the recomputation of the normal and tangential steps as the step bound Δ is reduced" (p. 4). Step 11 keeps the iterate and the steps and only reduces Δ (p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and the constraints are twice continuously differentiable (p. 2). The analysis assumes:
  - (A1) bounded iterates;
  - (A2) Lipschitz derivatives on a set containing the iterates and trial segments (symbols garbled);
  - (A3) a uniformly bounded inverse of the Jacobian of the perturbed KKT system (p. 13).

  (A3) holds near regular points with second-order sufficiency and strict complementarity (Remark 2, p. 13). The authors admit the cost: (A3) "rules out infeasibility", so the analysis covers only problems that are not infeasible (p. 13). They also admit that (A3) can fail in practice (§6.2, p. 25). They flag the optimality measure as a convenience: it does not reflect well that one is *minimizing* f (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The result is global convergence only, with no rates. The proof has a standard filter architecture:
  - Step estimates: Lemma 1 bounds how the measures change after the step, and gives a model-decrease lower bound proportional to the tangential step length × (1−σ) × the optimality measure (p. 9). The appendix proves these bounds from a complementarity estimate (Lemma 13, p. 27–28). Lemma 2 shows the neighbourhood is preserved for Δ ≤ Δ_cen (p. 9).
  - Structural lemmas: filter entries have θ > 0 (Lemma 4, p. 13). Every iterate is acceptable, by an induction the authors say "requires no analysis" (Lemma 5, p. 13–14). Once step 5 is reached, θ at the trial point is small in Δ, because restoration was not triggered (Lemma 6 and Remark 3, p. 14).
  - Acceptability lemmas for small Δ, in two cases: away from KKT points (Lemma 7) and with only optimality bounded away from zero (Lemma 8) (p. 15–17).
  - **Disjoint-squares argument** (Lemma 10, p. 17): each added entry reserves a square of side ∝ γ_F ε in a bounded (θ, optimality) region. Infinitely many such squares are impossible, so lim inf θ = 0 along the added entries. The authors call it "a commonly used argument in filter convergence proofs" (p. 17). Lemma 9 supplies the required transitivity of dominance (p. 17).
  - Remark 4 (p. 18) handles a subtlety: acceptability to a pair does not imply acceptability to a dominated pair. Two fixes are given: never remove dominated entries, or modify the acceptance test.
  - **Case split**: infinitely many filter additions (Lemmas 11–12 → Theorem 1) versus finitely many (Theorem 2) (p. 18–22). Corollary 1 gives lim inf (θ + optimality measure) = 0 (p. 22).
  - The restoration algorithm terminates finitely (Theorem 3, p. 23–24). The normal step is a descent direction for the restoration merit ½(‖h‖² + ‖centrality‖²), and the tangential step is orthogonal to its gradient, (41) (p. 22).
- **D5 Experiments**: No tables. A Matlab implementation was tested on QP and small NLP; the results are called "encouraging" but no numbers are given, and a Fortran 90 code is announced (p. 26). One qualitative *failure* experiment is reported. On the Wächter–Biegler example (46), Algorithm 1 with restoration Algorithm 2 showed "bad numerical behavior". The authors trace it to iterates approaching a point where (A3) fails (p. 25). An alternative restoration was then tried: a regularized least-squares problem (48) solved with Bertsekas' projected Newton method. It solved the example (p. 25–26).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper extends Fletcher–Leyffer [14] and Fletcher et al. [12] ("partially motivated by", p. 2). Merit-function interior-point globalizations [5, 9, 11, 16, 17, 23] are listed without criticism (p. 2). It notes that two other filter–interior-point papers ([3]; [26, 28]) appeared "Since the appearance of this paper" (p. 2), which is a priority claim for the April 2000 preprint. It cites the Audet–Dennis pattern-search filter for DFO [2] (p. 2). This is an early contact with the DFO line.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is the first sentence of both the abstract and the introduction (p. 1). §2 re-teaches the base method (SQP-filter) and then lists the new features as bullets: "Essentially, our application … has the following features" (p. 4). Technical proofs go to an appendix (p. 9). Hedging is moderate and specific: "We are aware that this choice certainly allows some room for improvement" (p. 6), and "hopefully a first step" (p. 27). Open questions are listed concretely (p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory plus algorithm, and the authors claim it is the first filter globalization of primal-dual interior-point methods (p. 26). The limits are stated concretely:
  - the optimality measure is imperfect (p. 6);
  - (A3) excludes infeasible problems and can fail (p. 13, p. 25);
  - there are no numerics (p. 26).

  Future work: numerics in Fortran 90 (p. 26); 3D filters that separate feasibility and centrality; whether the filter becomes locally inactive so that fast local rates survive; better optimality measures, ideally ones for which the tangential step gives a fraction of Cauchy decrease (p. 27); and restorations that work when (A3) fails (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (derivative-based precursor). The globalization changes only the *acceptance test*: nondominance in a 2-D filter replaces merit-function decrease. The Newton/trust-region skeleton is kept (p. 1–4, p. 10). The nondominance concept is explicitly "borrowed from multi-criteria optimization" (p. 1). This is seven years before the DMS dominance lists.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The efficient core that is kept is a Newton step, not a heuristic, and the glue is a filter, not sufficient decrease (p. 4, p. 11–12). The shape is the same: keep what is efficient locally ("numerically efficient notion of centrality", p. 2) and add a globalization layer.
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (era/field). This is a theory paper whose solver and numerics are deferred to "a future paper" (p. 26). Method 5 cannot be read as a rule that holds for all of Vicente's work.
- Method 2: no link. There is no complexity count, only lim inf results (p. 22).
- New-pattern candidate: *nondominance as a design primitive for single-objective difficulties* (filter here, p. 1–3; bilevel in S022; DMS 2011).
- New-pattern candidate: *run the class's known counterexample and report the failure with a diagnosis* (Wächter–Biegler, p. 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Split the right-hand side of one Newton system into two parts. You get two directions from one factorization, each decreasing one measure, with separate step lengths (p. 6–7).
- [D2] When shrinking the trust-region-like parameter, keep the computed directions and rescale only the step lengths min{1, Δ/‖s‖}. No re-solve is needed (p. 4, p. 7, p. 12).
- [D4] Filter proof skeleton: (i) all entries have θ > 0; (ii) disjoint squares of side ∝ ε in a bounded region give lim inf θ = 0; (iii) split into infinitely vs finitely many filter additions (p. 13, p. 17, p. 21–22).
- [D4] If you remove dominated filter entries, check whether acceptability is inherited by dominated pairs. If it is not, keep all entries or use the modified test (Remark 4, p. 18).
- [D5] Run the canonical failure example of your algorithm class (here Wächter–Biegler). Report the failure, name the violated assumption, and show one remedy (p. 25–26).

**Quotes**:
- "However, given that the investigation of filter methods is still in its beginnings, we think that our choice of the optimality measure is appropriate for the purpose of this paper." (p. 6)
- "This paper is hopefully a first step in this challenging topic." (p. 27)

### S025 · Pattern search methods for user-provided points: Application to molecular geometry problems (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2004, DOI 10.1137/s1052623400377955)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: P. Alberto and F. Nogueira (Physics, Coimbra), H. Rocha · **Position in trajectory**: This is the earliest DFO paper in this batch. It is application-driven, from a collaboration with physicists, and extends an ICCS 2001 short paper [1] (p. 20). Audet and Dennis are thanked (p. 20). Its closing wish, to put evolutionary algorithms in the search step (p. 14), foreshadows PSwarm (S005, 2007) and the globally convergent ES work (2015). **Read**: full text, including Appendix A (geometrical transformations, p. 15–18) and Appendix B (density-functional total energy, p. 18–20; physics background, read but not used in the card beyond cost claims).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is the application. The task is to find the minimum-total-energy geometry of atomic clusters (sodium), where the energy comes from a local-density plane-wave calculation (p. 1–2, App. B). Three features make this hard (p. 1–2, p. 11–12):
  - one evaluation is expensive: 16 to 186 s CPU for Na4 to Na32;
  - there are many local minima, and the number of isomers grows exponentially (2 → 988 for 6 → 13 Lennard-Jones atoms);
  - gradients are costly or unavailable.

  The incumbent methods (Langevin dynamics, Car–Parrinello) are faulted on concrete practical points: they are not parallelizable, run a fixed number of iterations with no autonomous stopping criterion, and need gradients (p. 2). The question is generalized in the abstract: how to exploit *any* user-provided scheme that proposes points of likely decrease (p. 1, p. 3, p. 14).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The domain move generator uses *physically meaningful geometric transformations*: stretches, compressions and torsions about the principal axes (App. A, p. 15–17). These depend on x_k, so they "cannot themselves define a pattern and a mesh", because that would ruin the finiteness of the pattern matrices (p. 6). They enter the method in two ways:
  - **Search**: each of N_p processors computes a transformed point, which is *rounded to the nearest mesh point* by a small integer program (3.9) at O(n³) linear-algebra cost (p. 9–10).
  - **Poll**: a transformed point selects *which* positive basis to poll, namely the one containing the vector at the smallest angle to the transformation direction. Poll points are ordered by that angle and evaluated in parallel groups of N_p (p. 7, p. 10).

  A new pattern is built from 2n minimal positive bases with *uniform angles*, with pairwise cosine −1/n (§3.1, p. 7–8). The search uses the poll's transformation parameters ×5, plus random rescalings, for global exploration (p. 11). Kept: the Torczon / Audet–Dennis search/poll/mesh framework (Algorithm 2.1, p. 5). Changed: the pattern, and the rule for choosing and ordering the poll basis.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The problem is unconstrained and smooth (abstract, p. 1). The inherited theory (Thm 2.4) requires a compact level set, C¹ f, and mesh columns of the form G z̄ with integer z̄ (p. 6). Global minimization has no theory, which the paper states itself: "despite the inexistence of any supporting theory" (p. 6). Six rigid-motion coordinates are removed by fixing three atoms, "not constraints in shape space" (App. A, p. 15). The stopping rule is borrowed from PDS (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No new convergence theorem is proved. Convergence is inherited ("The proof can be found, for instance, in [2, 20, 29]", p. 6). The paper's design argument is that rounding user points onto the mesh leaves the integer-lattice theory intact (p. 6, p. 9). The new mathematics is small and constructive:
  - n+1 unit vectors with equal pairwise angles must have cosine −1/n. The proof sums the Gram relations (3.3)–(3.5) (p. 7).
  - They are constructed as V = QΛ^{1/2}Qᵀ from the eigendecomposition of the positive definite A (1 on the diagonal, −1/n off it), with v_{n+1} = −Σvᵢ. Corollary 2.1 makes this a positive basis (p. 7–8).
  - 2n rotated copies are obtained with one Householder reflection plus permutation matrices, with no further Householder computations (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**: Sodium clusters Na4, Na8, Na16 and Na32 were run from 8 starting points each (only 2 for Na32 "Due to limited access to our cluster"). The tables report iterations, function evaluations and best energy (Tables 4.1–4.4, p. 12–13). The runs used 12 PCs with MPI (p. 11). An empirical *scaling fit* fevals ≈ A·n^B gives B ≈ 1.6, "slower than quadratic" (Fig. 4.1, p. 13). The authors note that the known optima for Na4/Na8 are reached only after an extra local optimization (p. 12).

  The head-to-head against PDS ran on a different machine and a different code version, so its energies are not comparable with the earlier tables. PSM:MGP was better on 6 of 9 starts and lost 3, all reported (Tables 4.5–4.6, p. 13–14). The paper calls this "mildly favorable" (p. 13). There is no problem collection and no profiles. The code is "far from being exhaustively tuned" (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the pattern-search lineage: Hooke–Jeeves, PDS, Torczon's unified framework "improved by Audet and Dennis" (p. 2–3), and positive-basis theory from Davis and Lewis–Torczon (p. 3–4). Prior molecular work is credited, not criticized: Meza–Martinez found PDS as good as GA, and Hart's evolutionary pattern search (p. 3). The incumbent physics methods are criticized on practical grounds only (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The physics motivation comes first (p. 1–2). The method is then stated in general terms, and the application details go to the appendices. The generality claim closes both the abstract and the conclusion (p. 1, p. 14). Results are hedged heavily and honestly: "preliminary", "promise", "mildly favorable", "plenty of room for improvement" (p. 1, p. 12–14). A code-availability paragraph sits in the introduction (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an algorithm class, software (Fortran 95, serial and MPI versions, downloadable from mat.uc.pt/~lnv/psm/, p. 3) and an application. Limits: preliminary and untuned numerics, an incomplete Na32 study (p. 13–14), no global-optimality theory (p. 6), and no local refinement (p. 2). Future work: evolutionary programming or evolution strategies in the search step, other clusters and molecular contexts, and other uniform-direction patterns (p. 14–15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (earliest instance in the corpus). Domain moves are free in the search step, and convergence comes from the poll and mesh (p. 5–6, p. 9–10). ⚠ The glue is variant: simple decrease on an integer-lattice mesh, with rounding onto the mesh, not the sufficient-decrease forcing function ρ(α) of Method 1, Step 3.
- Research Heuristic 7 ⚠ precursor. Ordering the poll by angle to a guidance direction (p. 10) is the same move as ordering the poll by simplex gradients (SID-PSM 2007), with the direction coming from physics instead of from past evaluations.
- Method 5 ⚠ partial. Code is released and there is a real application (p. 3, p. 11), but there is no collection, no profiles, and only one baseline (PDS) on a different set-up (p. 13).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. Cost is measured in function evaluations and reported as an *empirical* growth rate in n (≈ n^1.6, p. 13), not a worst-case bound.
- Research Taste 6 (anchored in an application) ✅ (p. 1–2, p. 11–14).
- New-pattern candidate: *project the domain or heuristic proposal onto the convergence structure* (mesh rounding here, p. 9; PSwarm particles projected onto the mesh, S005 p. 8–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] If your domain's move generator depends on the current iterate (so it cannot define a fixed pattern), round its output to the nearest mesh point and use it only in the search step. The convergence proof is then untouched (p. 6, p. 9).
- [D2] Let the domain move choose *which* positive basis to poll and in what order (maximum cosine with the move direction). This gives a guided but still provable poll (p. 10).
- [D4] Uniform-angle minimal positive basis recipe: V = QΛ^{1/2}Qᵀ for A = (1 on the diagonal, −1/n off it), v_{n+1} = −Σvᵢ. Rotate with one Householder reflection and permutations to get 2n bases (p. 7–8).
- [D5] Across problem sizes, fit log(fevals) = A + B log n and report B as an empirical scaling exponent together with the regression error (p. 13).
- [D2] Make the search-step parameters a fixed multiple (×5) of the poll-step ones. This separates exploration from local polling with one knob (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "The new pattern search methods can be used in any other context where there is an user-provided scheme to generate points leading to potential objective function decrease." (p. 1)
- "We point out that the implementation of PSM:MGP used in these computations is far from being exhaustively tuned." (p. 14)

### S022 · Multicriteria approach to bilevel optimization (Journal of Optimization Theory and Applications 2006, DOI 10.1007/s10957-006-9136-2)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. Fliege · **Position in trajectory**: This is a theory paper dated December 2004 (p. 1). It is Vicente's first multicriteria paper, written with a vector-optimization specialist (Fliege's quadratic-scalarization work is Ref. 15, p. 14). It sits between the filter (S007) and DMS (2011), and its declared motivation is derivative-free bilevel design (p. 12). **Read**: full text. Formulas are heavily garbled (see caveat), so the definitions of the order relations, the four-criteria map and the cone are reconstructed only from the prose and the proofs. The worked example in §5.1 (p. 9–10) is **not read at formula level**: only its conclusions, stated in prose, are used.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a *history of failed links*. Several attempts tied linear bilevel programs to bicriteria optimization (Bard; Ünlü), and all were refuted by counterexamples (Candler; Clarke–Westerberg; Haurie–Savard–White; Marcotte). The Wen–Hsu sufficient condition was also refuted (Marcotte–Savard) (p. 1–2). Fülöp saw that more than two criteria are needed, but characterized only the *feasible set* of *linear* bilevel programs (p. 2). The new question: can the *solutions* of *nonlinear* bilevel problems be characterized as nondominated points (p. 2)? The deeper motivation appears only at the end. Engineering bilevel design uses black-box simulations, and the aim is to rewrite the problem in one-level form "to allow the application of such derivative-free techniques" (p. 12).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The multicriteria problem is built "not directly from the upper and lower level objective functions but by using information from the whole bilevel optimization problem", including lower-level optimality (p. 2). The argument has four steps:
  1. Define an order relation such that bilevel solutions are exactly its nondominated points (Theorem 6, p. 8). By the proof's prose, a point fails either because it is infeasible (a point with the same upper variables has a lower lower-level value) or because another lower-level-optimal point has a lower upper-level value (p. 8).
  2. That relation is "difficult to work with", so replace the argmin condition with a gradient condition in y. This gives a weaker relation, equal to the first when the lower level is convex in y, with the one-way inclusion (7) (p. 8).
  3. Map to a four-criteria image space (p. 2, p. 13) and define a *nonconvex* cone K whose induced order implies the weaker relation (Theorem 7; Corollaries 1–2, p. 8–9).
  4. §5 (practice): K is a union of two convex cones, so standard linear scalarization cannot be used directly. Decouple into two convex cones and take the min of the two monotone functions (p. 9). In the example, linear scalarization fails (unbounded below, or it returns the induced region but not the solution), while a diagonal *quadratic* scalarization with "sensible" parameters recovers the unique solution (p. 10).

  Extensions: constrained bilevel via a projection onto the lower-level feasible set (p. 11), and three-level problems with two candidate orders, "none of which seems totally satisfactory" (p. 12). No algorithm is proposed.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The core analysis is unconstrained bilevel (6), with the lower level continuously differentiable in y. Convexity in y is needed for the two relations to coincide (abstract, p. 8). The paper discusses which cone properties are desirable (convex, closed, pointed, total) and notes that "we can not have all of these at the same time" (p. 3–4). It admits the cone is not convex (p. 9), and that the choice of scalarization type and parameters is "highly problem dependent" (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: All results are characterization theorems. There is no convergence or complexity analysis. Devices:
  - an order relation designed to encode optimality exactly, so that the first theorem follows "immediately from the definition" (Thm 6, p. 8);
  - replacing a global condition (argmin) by a checkable necessary condition and tracking the direction of the resulting inclusion of nondominated sets (7) (p. 8);
  - cone-induced orders and their properties (Thm 1, p. 3), plus monotone-scalarization theorems quoted from the literature: linear (Thms 2–3, p. 4–5) and Fliege's quadratic (Thms 4–5, p. 5–6);
  - a small worked example used as a *negative test* of linear scalarization (p. 9–10).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. There is one analytic example (p. 9–10).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper defines itself against the linear bilevel/bicriteria literature by listing its counterexamples (p. 1–2). It credits Fülöp as "the first who observe that more than just two criteria are needed" (p. 2). The differences from Fülöp are stated as three: nonlinear problems, solutions rather than feasible points, and use of lower-level optimality (p. 2). The standard bilevel books are cited for optimality conditions (p. 7). Fliege's own quadratic scalarization is the tool (p. 5–6).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper is short (14 pp.) and reads as definition → theorem → corollary. Background sections come with a skip-ahead note for experts (p. 2). The failures are candid: "Unfortunately" appears twice (p. 8, p. 9); the questions "are hard to answer" (p. 11); the three-level orders are "none … totally satisfactory" (p. 12). The DFO application motive is saved for the conclusion (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical: a characterization of bilevel solutions as nondominated points, and a four-criteria cone that is sufficient for bilevel optimality. Limits: the cone is nonconvex, so standard scalarization is lost (p. 9); scalarization choice is an open question (p. 11); the three-level case is unsatisfactory (p. 12); there is no algorithm and no numerics. Future work: learn about scalarization choice by studying "particular classes of bilevel optimization problems" (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (at the problem level). The move is not "change the acceptance test inside a skeleton". It is "redefine what counts as better (the order relation) so that existing machinery can be reused" (p. 7–9, p. 12). It is the same idea as DMS and the filter (S007), applied to a formulation instead of an algorithm.
- Workflow E checkpoint ✅ evidence. The worked example shows that linear (fixed-weight) scalarization fails when the relevant cone is nonconvex, and that quadratic scalarization succeeds (p. 9–10).
- Research Taste 3 (minimal modification of what practitioners already use) ✅ evidence. The stated route is to reuse the "several sophisticated algorithms and implementations for derivative-free (one-level) optimization" after reformulation (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: *nondominance as a unifying lens* (S007 filter p. 1–3; here p. 2, p. 8; DMS 2011).
- New-pattern candidate: *reformulate to reuse existing DFO solvers rather than design a new solver* (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] To link problem class A with class B, first define the order relation that makes A's solutions *exactly* B's nondominated points (a near-tautological theorem). Then replace the intractable part (argmin) by a checkable necessary condition and state the direction of the inclusion (p. 7–8).
- [D2] When the induced cone is a nonconvex union of convex cones, scalarize with the minimum of one monotone function per convex piece (p. 9).
- [D4] Before recommending a scalarization, test it on a two-variable example where the answer is known. Here linear failed and quadratic worked (p. 9–10).
- [D1] Open with the chronological list of counterexamples that sank earlier formulations. It justifies why a *different* formulation, not a patch, is needed (p. 1–2).

**Quotes**:
- "It should not be seen as a surprise that the optimal solution of a bilevel problem can be dominated in terms of upper and lower level functions." (p. 2)
- "It is important to stress the high complexity of hierarchical optimization problems and to convey the point that the jump from two-level to three-level should not be regarded as a simple increase in dimensionality." (p. 12)

### S005 · A particle swarm pattern search method for bound constrained global optimization (Journal of Global Optimization 2007, DOI 10.1007/s10898-007-9133-5)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. I. F. Vaz (Minho) · **Position in trajectory**: This paper is dated December 21, 2006 (p. 1). It is the PSwarm solver paper, cited in SKILL.md as a canonical case of Method 1. It follows S025, which it cites as prior use of the search step for global optimization (p. 2, ref [1]). It is contemporary with Custódio–Vicente's SID-PSM (ref [8], p. 29). **Read**: full text, including Table 2 (p. 23–26).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Pattern search is local but has a free search step that can host global heuristics. Particle swarm is simple, parallel and needs few user-set parameters, but has no guarantees (p. 1–2). The goal: "to show how particle swarm can be incorporated in the pattern search framework" while keeping a pattern search algorithm, with integer lattices and positive spanning sets (p. 2). The problem is min f(z) subject to ℓ ≤ z ≤ u. No smoothness is needed to *run* the method; smoothness is needed to characterize stationarity (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 4.1 (p. 9–10) works as follows:
  - One particle-swarm iteration is run in each search step, and its trial points are (in theory) projected onto the mesh (p. 8–9).
  - The swarm's best position ŷ(t) is the pattern-search iterate. If the search fails, the method polls around ŷ with D⊕ (coordinate search) (p. 8–10).
  - Consecutive successful searches therefore reduce to plain particle-swarm iterations (p. 8).
  - Bounds use the extreme barrier f̂ = +∞ outside Ω. D⊕ contains the feasible descent directions for bounds (p. 6–7).
  - The stopping rule is hybrid: α < α_tol **and** all velocities < v_tol (p. 10).

  Kept: the particle-swarm velocity update (2), unchanged (p. 4, p. 10), and the coordinate-search poll. Changed: the leader is improved by the poll, and particles within α(0) of the leader are removed to save evaluations (p. 15–16). The authors contrast their work with Hart's evolutionary pattern search: they use particle swarm, their pattern-search part is deterministic, and so α → 0 along a subsequence deterministically rather than with probability one (p. 3). **The implementation departs from the theory**: the projection onto the mesh "has not been implemented in the search step" (p. 15). Other implementation choices: expansion only after two consecutive successful polls in the same direction, contraction 0.5, opportunistic polling, and inertia decreasing linearly from 0.9 to 0.4 (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Theorem 5.1 needs a bounded level set and the integer-lattice mesh conditions (D = GẐ, a rational τ̄) (p. 6–7, p. 10). ∇f = 0 at the limit needs strict differentiability there, and the lim inf ‖∇f‖ result needs C¹ f (p. 11). Theorem 5.2 treats the swarm quantities as random variables and assumes that, for large t, the expected personal and global bests are constant and that projection onto the mesh does not change the expected position (p. 11). The authors concede: "It is difficult to show that the conditions of Theorem 5.2 can be rigorously satisfied" (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Thm 5.1 is the standard integer-lattice argument: a subsequence of unsuccessful iterations with α → 0 converges (p. 10). The text notes that a *sufficient decrease* condition would avoid the integrality requirements (p. 10–11), which foreshadows the later sufficient-decrease line.
  - Thm 5.2 (termination of the heuristic): take expectations of the velocity update. This gives a second-order linear recurrence in E(v). Once the forcing term vanishes, it is homogeneous with characteristic roots a and b (8)–(9). If max{|a|, |b|} < 1, then E(v) → 0, and the algorithm stops "almost surely" in finitely many iterations (p. 11–13). Fig. 1 shows the root moduli as a function of the inertia (p. 13).
  - The assumptions are then justified informally. The best-value sequences are monotone and bounded, and the projection error is a multiple of α, which goes to 0 along a subsequence (p. 13–14).
- **D5 Experiments**: The test set has 122 bound-constrained global problems from the literature, coded in AMPL and put online (p. 14). The baselines are DIRECT, ASA, MCS and PGAPack with *default options*, except for the evaluation caps and PGAPack's population of 200 (p. 14–16).
  - Budget: maxf = 1000 (7500 for the 13 problems with n ≥ 100) and 10000 (15000) (p. 18).
  - Runs: 30 for each stochastic solver, 1 for each deterministic solver (p. 18).
  - Unit: function evaluations, "instead of" iterations or CPU (p. 16). The authors disclose that MCS overshoots the cap (14296 evaluations for a 1000 cap on lj1_38, p. 16).
  - Metric: performance profiles of the best, average and worst *final objective value*. They use a shifted ratio r = 1 + t − min when min < ε = 0.001, to handle zero or negative optima (p. 16–17).
  - Findings (p. 18–21):
    - Best-case profiles: PSwarm has a slight edge.
    - Average and worst cases: PSwarm "loses in performance against DIRECT and MCS" but is the most robust.
    - Evaluation profiles: MCS is first and PSwarm second.
    - Table 1 (average function evaluations): PSwarm is lowest at 686 / 3603. Because DIRECT stops on its budget, its final values should not be read as a win.
  - Ablation: PSwarm vs PS (coordinate search alone) vs PSOA (particle swarm alone) with the same parameters. PSwarm is better than both (Figs. 10–11, p. 26–27).
  - Per-problem Table 2 reports evaluations, iterations, number of polls, % successful polls and gap. For Lennard-Jones and a few others, the value reported is the final function value (marked *) because no optimum is known (p. 23–26).
- **D6 Positioning**: The method is placed in the search/poll view of Audet–Dennis, which "better suits the incorporation of heuristic procedures", rather than the generating-set-search view of Kolda–Lewis–Torczon (p. 5). Other search-step uses are cited: surrogates [5, 30] and global optimization via S025 [1] (p. 2). The paper differs explicitly from Hart (p. 3). Particle swarm is credited to Eberhart–Kennedy, and to van den Bergh for the variant used (p. 2–3). The benchmarking methodology comes from Dolan–Moré and Ali et al. (p. 16–17).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The goal sentence sits in the introduction (p. 2). The mechanism is summarized in one line: "It is the poll step that guarantees the global convergence" (p. 6). Theory hedges are explicit (p. 13). The results prose reports losses directly ("loses in performance against DIRECT and MCS", p. 18). The conclusion's claim is stronger than the profiles: "the most robust among all global optimization solvers tested" and "highly competitive in efficiency with … MCS" (p. 28).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an algorithm, a solver (PSwarm, in C with an AMPL interface, p. 14), a public test collection (p. 14), a convergence and termination analysis, and a benchmark. Limits: only stationarity is guaranteed (p. 26); the assumptions of Theorem 5.2 are unverifiable (p. 13); the mesh projection is not implemented (p. 15); there was no tuning (p. 26); the Lennard-Jones instances are poorly solved (Table 2, p. 24–25). Future work: a parallel version, more general constraints, and particle swarm run on a cheap *surrogate* in the search step (p. 28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Run one heuristic iteration per search step and centre the poll at the heuristic's best point. Successful searches are then just heuristic iterations, and failures trigger a provable local poll (p. 8–10).
- [D2] Handle bounds with an extreme barrier (f̂ = +∞ outside Ω) plus D⊕. No projection is needed in the pattern-search part (p. 6–7, p. 9).
- [D4] To show that a stochastic heuristic terminates, take expectations of its linear update. This gives a linear recurrence, and a root-modulus bound gives E(v) → 0 (p. 11–13).
- [D5] Build performance profiles on the final objective value under a fixed evaluation budget, with best/average/worst over 30 runs and the shift r = 1 + t − min when min < ε (p. 17–18).
- [D5] Ablate the hybrid against each component alone, using identical parameters (p. 26–27).
- [D5] Count cost in function evaluations. Enforce the budget in wrappers and report any solver that overshoots it (p. 16).
- [D2] Drop population members that come within α(0) of the leader, to stop paying for redundant evaluations late in the run (p. 15–16).
