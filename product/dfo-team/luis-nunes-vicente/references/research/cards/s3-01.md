### S053 · A new technique for generating quadratic programming test problems (Mathematical Programming 1993, DOI 10.1007/bf01582148)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. H. Calamai (Waterloo), J. J. Júdice (Coimbra) · **Position in trajectory**: Coimbra period (Vicente's affiliation is Universidade de Coimbra, p. 1). Much of the paper was completed during Calamai's sabbatical in Coimbra (p. 1). It was motivated by the authors' earlier bilevel generator (ref. [3], a 1991 Waterloo report titled "Generating quadratic bilevel programming problems"; p. 2 and p. 14). S026 has nearly the same title and was received in Dec. 1991 (S026 p. 17). **Read**: full (15 pages; the plot debris on pp. 5–8 is unreadable).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: QP and global-optimization algorithms need test problems, which come from real-world collections or from random generators (p. 1). The existing generators have two weaknesses (p. 2):
  - They need an LP, a convex program or a linear system to be solved (Rosen; Sung–Rosen; Kalantari–Rosen; Pardalos).
  - Most place the global minimum at a vertex, so algorithms that systematically visit vertices may do well on them and poorly on other problems.

  Hager et al. allow nonextreme boundary minima; Kalantari gives exponentially many local minima, but only with box constraints (p. 2). Entry: one cheap, unified generator for convex, strictly concave and indefinite QPs, including jointly constrained bilinear ones. It must produce the known hard features: an interior global maximum, exponentially many local minima with distinct values, and nonextreme global minima (p. 2). The paper claims that no other generator exists for jointly constrained bilinear problems (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & construction**:
  - **Blocks**: take m disjoint two-variable subproblems SQP_l, each with three linear constraints, so that the QP in 2m variables is separable. Minima of the blocks combine into minima of the whole problem (§2, p. 3).
  - **Class by block data**: the per-block data select the class — strictly concave (§3.1, p. 4), jointly constrained bilinear (§3.2, pp. 5–6), convex (§3.3, p. 7) — or a mix of the three, which gives an indefinite problem (§3.4, pp. 8–9).
  - **Counts by combination**: minima counts multiply across blocks. The concave case has 3^{m0} local minima and a unique global one (Property 3.1, p. 4); the bilinear case has 2^m local minima (Property 3.5, p. 7). The §6 example has 18 local minima, 2 of them global (p. 13).
  - **Disguise**: a nonsingular change of variables M = DH hides the separability and adds randomness. H is a random Householder matrix with a sparse generator v; D is a positive definite diagonal matrix with a prescribed condition number (§4, p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Linear constraints and a quadratic objective. Distinct values at local minima require certain powers of 4 built from l and L to stay computationally distinguishable from 0 and 1 (the exponents are lost in the extraction). This bounds the integer L (p. 4, p. 12). The active-constraint gradients are linearly independent at all minima (Properties 3.4, 3.6, 3.13; pp. 5, 7, 9).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Elementary, by blocks. Each block's minima are read off its geometry (Figs. 1–3, pp. 4–8). Property 3.2 (all local minima have distinct values) is proved by induction: the smallest gap in the next block exceeds the sum of the largest gaps of all earlier blocks, because the blocks are scaled by powers of 4 that grow with l and L (p. 5). Propositions 4.1–4.3 show that strong local minima correspond one-to-one under z = Wz̄ (pp. 9–10). Proposition 4.4 counts the nonzeros of M, AM and MᵀQM from the nonzeros of v (p. 10). Proposition 4.5 gives the condition number of MᵀQM from the block entries scaled by D (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. A worked example (m = 3, n = 6, 9 constraints) is shown before and after the transformation (§6, pp. 12–13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: a generator with user-adjustable size, density, number and type of minima, and curvature (p. 14). The Fortran 77 code is available by e-mail request (p. 14).
  - **Concrete limits**:
    - Removing noncrucial constraints makes the feasible region no longer closed (p. 12).
    - The floating-point bound on L caps the number of local minima; two remedies are sketched, a √3 base and a number-theoretic choice of parameters (p. 12).
    - The full transformation destroys the bilinear structure unless M is block diagonal (Remark 4.2, p. 11).
  - **Not done**: the paper does not run any algorithm on the generated problems. There is no future-work list.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant, early (p. 14). The released artefact is benchmark infrastructure (a generator plus code on request), not a solver, and there are no profiles. It anticipates Method 5 Step 2 (make the problem collection available to others).
- Taste warning sign #5 ✅ stated (p. 14). The conclusion opens with a warning about testing and benchmarking (quoted below).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant, outside algorithms (pp. 2–9). The block-plus-transformation construction is carried over from the bilevel generator (ref. [3], p. 2). Only the per-block data change to reach each new class (convex, concave, bilinear, indefinite).
- Existing candidate *controlled-difficulty test-problem generators* (c01, S002) ✅ direct evidence (pp. 1–2, 14).
- Related to the s01 candidate *ground truth first*: every minimum of every generated problem is known by construction (Properties 3.1–3.13, pp. 4–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To get test problems with known minima, take a direct sum of 2-D blocks whose minima can be seen in a picture. Then disguise the sum with z = Wz̄, W = M⁻¹ = HD⁻¹, where M = DH, H is a sparse random Householder matrix and D a diagonal matrix with a chosen condition number. Minima map one-to-one; the sparsity of v sets the density, and D sets the conditioning (pp. 3, 9–11).
- To make all local minima have distinct values, scale block l geometrically (powers of 4 that grow with l). Each block's smallest gap then exceeds the sum of all earlier blocks' largest gaps (p. 5).
- Aim the construction at the weakness of the rival generators: place global minima at nonextreme points, so that vertex-enumerating methods cannot exploit the construction (pp. 2, 7, 9).

**Quotes**:
- "Unlike most existing methods our construction technique does not require the solution of any subproblems or systems of equations." (p. 1)
- "Care must be exercised in the testing and benchmarking of algorithms and in the interpretation and dissemination of the corresponding results [4, 5, 14]." (p. 14)

### D001 · Algorithm 728: FORTRAN subroutines for generating quadratic bilevel programming test problems (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 20(1), 120–123, 1994, DOI 10.1145/174603.174410)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. H. Calamai · **Position in trajectory**: software companion to S026, "(this issue)" (p. 1). Vicente's address is Rice University (p. 1). The paper was received in Dec. 1991 and accepted in Apr. 1993 (p. 4). **Read**: full (4 pages).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The authors know of no other technique for generating quadratic bilevel test problems. The routines are offered for "testing, verifying, and comparing" new and established methods (p. 1; the OCR prints "comparmg" in the abstract).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: Portable ANSI FORTRAN 77 with modified level-1 BLAS and Schrage's portable random number generator (p. 2). The central routine qbpgen controls the problem properties. The other routines evaluate (qbpfun), check (qbpchk), build and apply the transformation (qbpmkm, qbpmx), and handle sparse inner products (qbpdot) (p. 2). qbpgen takes the numbers of upper- and lower-level variables, the solution characteristics m1–m4 with their ρ-vectors, and the condition number and nonzero count of the transformation (lcondm, nonO) (p. 2). The checker qbpchk compares a candidate with *all* minimizers. It works in the untransformed space, because doing so in the transformed space "would be a combinatorial process", and it returns the nearest minimizer, the distance and the minimizer type (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Not applicable (software). Precision is switched with Grcar's change.f or by hand-editing comment blocks (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis**: None.
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The package ships an installation test ("verify", p. 4) and a program "usage" with annotated example calls (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is software. The stated limit is concrete: maxn = 2000 is fixed in parameter statements in five routines and in verify (p. 4). Design goal: flexibility, while meeting the requirements of several existing solution techniques (p. 2). No future work.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence of the release habit, very early (pp. 1–4). The artefact is a separate, citable software publication with an installation test. It is a generator, not a solver.
- Existing candidate *infrastructure as a stand-alone citable publication* (c2-02) ✅ (p. 1). Existing candidate *controlled-difficulty test-problem generators* (c01) ✅.
- Candidate *theory paper / application paper split* (c05) ⚠ variant: here the split is method paper (S026) + software paper (D001), published in the same issue (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ship a solution checker with any generator. It maps the candidate back to the untransformed space and returns the nearest known minimizer, the distance to it and its type (local or global) (p. 3).
- Expose the difficulty knobs as arguments of the generator: the number and type of minima, and the conditioning and sparsity of the disguising transformation (p. 2).
- Include a verification program and a usage program with annotated example calls (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "Our hope is that these codes will be used for testing, verifying, and comparing new (and established) solution methods." (p. 1)
- "To encourage the use of these codes, every effort has been made to keep them as flexible as possible." (p. 2)

### S026 · Generating quadratic bilevel programming test problems (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 1994, DOI 10.1145/174603.174411)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. H. Calamai · **Position in trajectory**: the earliest of the Calamai–Vicente generators in this batch (received Dec. 1991, p. 17). S053 cites a 1991 report by the same authors with nearly the same title as its motivation (S053 p. 2). Vicente's address is Rice (p. 1). The code is published as D001. **Read**: full (17 pages). The prose is fully read; the displayed formulas on pp. 2–4 and 12–16 are OCR-garbled and are used only through the prose around them.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Bilevel programming is an important field with numerous applications and many proposed solution methods (p. 1). Generators exist for nonlinear programming, but "none have addressed the need" for standardized quadratic bilevel test problems (p. 2). Entry (abstract, p. 1): generate sparse or dense QBPs with a selectable number of known global and local solutions, without solving any subproblem. Since most methods start by solving the relaxed QP, the global solutions are constructed to differ from the relaxed solution in a selectable number of upper- and lower-level variables. The problems also satisfy the requirements of all solution techniques known to the authors.
- **D2 Core idea & construction**:
  - **Blocks**: a separable parametric QBP(ρ) splits into m two-variable, one-parameter bilevel problems QBP(ρ_k) with ρ_k ≥ 1 (§3–4, pp. 3–4).
  - **Four cases**: by ρ_k = 1, 1 < ρ_k < 2, ρ_k = 2 and ρ_k > 2, which set the global and local solutions of each block (pp. 4–9).
  - **Relaxed solution**: the relaxed QP's solution is (1, 0) in every block (p. 9).
  - **Counts**: combining blocks gives 2^{m3} global solutions and 2^{m2+m3+m4} − 2^{m3} additional local ones (Properties 1–2, pp. 9–10). The global solution differs from the relaxed one in both levels whenever m1 < m (Property 3, p. 10). Which cases the blocks are in also decides whether the active lower-level constraint gradients are linearly independent and complementarity is strict at the global solution(s) (Properties 4–5, p. 10).
  - **Extensions**: fewer inequality constraints, added equality constraints (with conditions that keep the gradients linearly independent), and a linear lower-level term (§6, pp. 10–12).
  - **Transformation**: M = HDH, with H block-diagonal and built from separate sparse Householder generators for x and y, and D diagonal with a prescribed 2-norm condition number (the OCR renders the value as "106", p. 12). Proposition 2 gives the equivalence (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The upper-level objective is continuous and convex; the lower-level objective is strictly convex in y; the constraints are linear; there are no upper-level constraints (pp. 15–16). The generated matrices keep MCM positive definite and (MSM)_yy positive definite (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Proposition 1: the induced region is a union of three line segments. The proof is a KKT case analysis of the lower-level problem with contradiction by multiplier signs (pp. 5–6).
  - Global and local minimizers of each block come from a level-circle argument: the circle of radius r around the relaxed minimizer (1, 0) meets the induced region only at the claimed point (pp. 8–9).
  - Properties 1–5 follow by combining the blocks (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The §8 example (nx = 4, ny = 2) has a unique global minimum with value 0.3125 and 3 additional local solutions (pp. 13–15). In place of experiments, §9 checks the generated problems against the standing assumptions of three method families (pp. 15–16): branch and bound/cutting (Edmunds–Bard), steepest descent (Gauvin–Savard) and exact penalty (Bi–Calamai–Conn).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: a generator with control of the number and type of minimizers, sparsity, conditioning, and the number of variables in which the global solution differs from the relaxed one (p. 16). The Fortran 77 code is available by e-mail (p. 16).
  - **Limits**: few are stated. A bounded feasible set needs the extra constraint x_k ≥ 0 (p. 16).
  - **Future**: a similar method for linear and linear-quadratic bilevel problems (p. 16).
  - The acknowledgments credit a reader who found an error in the draft of §7 (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant, early (pp. 1, 16): collection infrastructure plus code, released separately as D001.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant, construction reuse (S053 p. 2; S026 pp. 3–13). The block-plus-transformation skeleton moves from the bilevel class (S026) to single-level QP (S053); only the block data change.
- Existing candidate *controlled-difficulty test-problem generators* (c01) ✅ (p. 1, pp. 9–10).
- new-pattern candidate: **aim the benchmark at the solvers' first move and certify it against their assumptions**. The global solution is made to differ from the relaxed-QP solution that most methods compute first (p. 1, Property 3 p. 10). The problems are then proved to satisfy the standing assumptions of three method families (§9, pp. 15–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Make the global solution differ from the obvious warm start (the relaxed problem's solution) in a controlled number of variables, so that a solver cannot succeed just by solving the relaxation (p. 1; Property 3, p. 10).
- Before releasing a benchmark, list the standing assumptions of each target solver family and show, item by item, that the generated instances satisfy them (§9, pp. 15–16).
- When disguising a structured problem, make the random transformation block-diagonal along the structure (separate Householder factors for upper- and lower-level variables), so that the structure survives (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "the global solutions are constructed to be different than the global solution of this relaxed problem in a selectable number of upper- and lower-level variables." (p. 1)
- "Test problems are only useful when they can be used to test solution techniques." (p. 15)

### S084 · Local convergence of the affine-scaling interior-point algorithm for nonlinear programming (Computational Optimization and Applications 2000, DOI 10.1023/a:1008774924658)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, Coimbra; p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the derivative-based interior-point line. It cites Dennis–Vicente on bounds (ref. [12]) and Dennis–Heinkenschloss–Vicente on TRIP SQP (ref. [10]) (p. 12). S085 (COAP 2008) continues it (see the S085 card in s2-03). **Read**: full (13 pages, including the references).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Local convergence theory exists for *primal-dual* IPMs (El-Bakry et al., Martinez et al., Yamashita–Yabe; p. 1). Affine-scaling IPMs (Coleman–Li) are tailored to problem classes and have strong properties there, but the author finds no general local analysis for them comparable to the primal-dual one (p. 2). Entry: fill that gap for nonlinear programming, with quasi-Newton Hessian approximations and inexact linear solves (p. 2). Global convergence and polynomiality are explicitly excluded (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Problem**: min f(x) s.t. g(x) = 0, x ≥ 0 (problem (1), p. 2).
  - **Eliminating z**: the Coleman–Li diagonal scaling D(x, λ) removes the bound multipliers z, which turns the KKT conditions into D²∇ℓ = 0, g = 0, x ≥ 0 ((6)–(8), p. 3). The Newton step adds a diagonal E(x, λ) from the product rule ((9), p. 3).
  - **Algorithm 2.1**: an approximate solve of the quasi-Newton system with a centring perturbation μ_k e, a fraction-to-boundary step applied to x only, and λ updated with the same step (p. 5). The residual r_k of the inexact solve is explicit ((14), p. 6).
  - **Key move**: define a modified scaling D[x*, λ*, k] whose branch is fixed by the limit point and the current iterate. It coincides with D near the solution and makes the system Lipschitz continuously differentiable, so each step is a genuine quasi-Newton step on a smooth system (§2.4, pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and g are twice Lipschitz continuously differentiable. x* is regular, satisfies strict complementarity and the second-order sufficient conditions (Assumptions 2.1, p. 4). For q-linear convergence, H_k satisfies the bounded deterioration property (22) (p. 10). Each rate needs its own asymptotic conditions on τ_k, μ_k, ‖r_k‖ and ‖s¹_k‖, stated in powers of ‖F(w_k)‖ (Theorems 4.1–4.3, pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **Lemma 3.1** bounds |1 − α_k| ≤ |1 − τ_k| + τ_k(c₂‖Δw_k‖ + c₃‖s¹_k‖) by splitting the indices (pp. 7–8). Inactive indices are handled by their distance to the boundary. For active ones, the first block row of (15) is solved for Δx_i/x_i, and strict complementarity gives (E_k)_ii ≥ (E*)_ii/2. (The symbols are reconstructed from the garbled extraction; the constant names are ours.)
  - **Lemma 3.2**: A_k is nonsingular and close to ∇F(w*), by add-and-subtract in the Frobenius norm (p. 9).
  - **The rest is cited**: with the error decomposition (21) (p. 8), the theorems follow Broyden–Dennis–Moré, Dennis–Moré and Yamashita–Yabe, and the paper omits the proofs as similar (p. 10). q-superlinear convergence is characterized by a Dennis–Moré condition (23) (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: None.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory: q-linear, q-superlinear and q-quadratic local rates (Theorems 4.1–4.3, pp. 10–11). The limits are stated up front: no global convergence and no polynomiality (p. 2). Strict complementarity is assumed, although the survey notes that Heinkenschloss–Ulbrich–Ulbrich avoid it for simple bounds (p. 2). This contrast is not drawn as a limitation. The paper ends at Theorem 4.3, with no conclusion and no future work (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- s01 candidate *bridge inequality to a classical proof* ✅ (pp. 7–10). Two new lemmas (the step-length deficit and A_k ≈ ∇F(w*)) plug into the Yamashita–Yabe / Dennis–Moré argument, whose proofs are then omitted as similar (p. 10). Together with S037, S031, S039 (s01) and S085 (Lemma 3.3, s2-03), the move now appears in five papers.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, derivative-based (pp. 2, 5, 10). The quasi-Newton local theory is kept; the one object that changes is the Newton system, which becomes the affine-scaled one. The paper says its technical results are obtained differently from Yamashita–Yabe [29] (p. 2).
- s2-03 candidate *deterministic inexactness tied to the step* ⚠ variant (pp. 6, 10–11). Here the residual tolerance is tied to ‖F(w_k)‖, not to the step, and one tolerance is given for each target rate.
- Method 2: no link (local rates, no evaluation counts).
- new-pattern candidate: **branch-freezing to smooth a piecewise map near the solution** (pp. 6–7). The scaling's branch is fixed by the limit point so that classical smooth theory applies to a nonsmooth reformulation. One paper only.

**Transferable techniques**:
- If a reformulated KKT map is nondifferentiable where some component of ∇ℓ vanishes, define a surrogate whose branch is fixed by the limit point and the current iterate. Near the solution it coincides with the algorithm's map and is Lipschitz differentiable, so standard quasi-Newton theory applies (pp. 6–7).
- Give one rate theorem per level (linear, superlinear, quadratic), each with the decay each algorithm parameter (1 − τ_k, μ_k, ‖r_k‖, ‖s¹_k‖) must have, written in ‖F(w_k)‖ (Theorems 4.1–4.3, pp. 10–11).
- Bound a fraction-to-boundary step deficit 1 − α_k by treating inactive indices (distance to the bound) and active indices (strict complementarity) separately (pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "The proofs are similar and are omitted." (p. 10). This is the only usable quote: the extraction replaces every "c" with a carriage return (see the batch header).

### S109 · Matemática industrial em Portugal. Análise e perspectivas (Fundação Calouste Gulbenkian, Tema Ciência e Sociedade 2006, no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, Coimbra; p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a policy essay for the Gulbenkian Foundation's 50th anniversary (p. 1). It grew out of Vicente's contribution to the "Matemática e Indústria" debate at the SPM national meeting in 2004 (ref. [4], pp. 2, 7). This is a *stated-methodology* source, not a research paper. **Read**: full (8 pages; Portuguese).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Mathematics is now used in all sciences and technologies (illustrated by a table from the 1998 SIAM report on mathematics in industry, pp. 1–2). The essay analyses industrial mathematics in Portugal under three aspects (p. 2): (i) how it is researched and taught in universities; (ii) the careers of mathematics graduates; (iii) collaboration between academics and industry.
- **D2 Core argument**:
  - **Pure vs applied**: the split is artificial. Much of "applied" mathematics is no less pure than some "pure" mathematics, and such divisions flourish in isolated academic settings (p. 2).
  - **Industry side**: company headquarters are concentrated in Lisbon and technology is imported (p. 2). Mathematicians placed in service firms drift into IT or administrative work (p. 3). The operational-research community is more dynamic than the mathematics community in placing graduates and linking to firms (p. 3). The derivatives market is small (pp. 3–4) and manufacturing does little quantitative work. Hope lies in university spin-offs (p. 4).
  - **Academic side**: Portuguese academia wrongly believed pure mathematics superior, while other countries reoriented research around real problems (p. 4). An opposite error is to think that applications appear from goodwill. The unanimous view at the Coimbra Industrial Workshop (Oct. 2005) was that personal contacts are the main source of academia–industry collaboration, and that they grow out of the academic's own research activity (pp. 4–5). Training in industrial and computational mathematics is not cheaper than abstract training. It combines the mathematics, modern scientific-computing tools and the language of other disciplines (p. 5).
  - **Recommendations**:
    - Teaching: interdepartmental programmes and continuous assessment; train students in creativity, individual and then team work, and written and oral reporting, because industrial problems arrive unformulated, with blurred borders and diffuse goals (pp. 5–6). More PhDs, so that some move into industry (p. 6).
    - National organization: a national body, possibly by reusing the SPM or the CIM (lower impact, higher feasibility) (p. 6).
    - State role: either an institute of graduate studies or a state laboratory, or an attractive competition for Associated Laboratories. The author judges the second option insufficient (p. 7).
- **D3 Evidence base**: The SIAM report (more than 200 industrial staff, p. 1), a 2002 Coimbra department study of firms in the Centro region (p. 2), the 2005 Coimbra Industrial Workshop (p. 4) and the 2002–2004 evaluation panel for mathematics research units (p. 4). No data of the author's own.
- **D4 Analysis**: Not applicable.
- **D5 Experiments**: None.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Diagnosis plus concrete institutional options, each weighed by impact against feasibility (pp. 6–7). The author concedes that neither option is original, since other countries launched both long ago (p. 7). No evaluation of past measures is offered beyond noting that the evaluation panels' recommendations had no practical effect yet (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Taste #6 (anchored in an application) ✅ stated (pp. 4–5). The essay says where applications come from: the researcher's own internationally demanding research portfolio and the personal contacts built on it over years, not goodwill.
- Method 5 ✅ stated side (p. 5). Mastery of modern scientific-computing tools is named as an essential part of training in industrial mathematics. This adds a second stated source next to the Lehigh 2018 quote in SKILL.md.
- Research Heuristic 8 (formalize informal notions) ✅ stated background (p. 6). Industrial problems "não aparecem formulados" (do not arrive formulated), so formulation is part of the work.
- new-pattern candidate: **applications through one's own research network** (pp. 4–5). This is a stated belief with no practice evidence in this card; the application papers in other batches would be the test.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To position a policy or perspective piece, state the three aspects to be analysed at the outset and back each with a cited survey or report (p. 2).
- When proposing options, weigh each by impact against feasibility, and name the cheaper option's weakness (pp. 6–7).

**Quotes**:

### S030 · Using simplex gradients of nonsmooth functions in direct search methods (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2008, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drn045)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. L. Custódio (FCT-UNL), J. E. Dennis Jr. (Rice) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the theory follow-up to Custódio & Vicente, SIAM J. Optim. 2007 (S008, ref. [8]). It extends simplex-gradient poll ordering to nonsmooth f under GPS and MADS. S059 later cites it as the basis of a SID-PSM stopping criterion (S059 p. 7). **Read**: full (14 pages, preprint).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Ordering the poll directions by the negative simplex gradient cuts function evaluations when polling is opportunistic (ref. [8], pp. 1–2). Direct search applies to nonsmooth f, and GPS and MADS have a Clarke-type convergence analysis, but simplex-gradient accuracy is known only for smooth f (Theorem 2.1, pp. 2–3). Two questions (abstract, p. 1): what do simplex gradients at refining subsequences mean when f is only Lipschitz, and does the ordering still pay off?
- **D2 Core idea & design**: No new algorithm. The object of study is a simplex gradient built from a subset Z_k of the poll points at iterates of a refining subsequence, i.e., unsuccessful iterates with α_k → 0 (pp. 3–4), with Λ-poisedness ‖Σ⁻¹‖ ≤ Λ (p. 3). The minimal-frame-centre property is *not* needed; only convergence and α_k → 0 are used (p. 3). Two uses follow:
  - A stopping test: ∇_s f(x_k)ᵀ(α_k d) ≥ −tol for all d ∈ E_k (p. 9).
  - Poll ordering: by the angle with −∇_s f (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is Lipschitz near x*. Iterates stay in a compact set. |D_k| is uniformly bounded, α_k‖d_k‖ → 0, and normalized directions converge (taken without loss of generality) (pp. 3–5). The strongest result (Theorem 3.5, p. 8) additionally needs strict differentiability at x*.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **Lemma 3.1**: simplex gradients stay bounded, ‖∇_s f‖ ≤ ‖Σ⁻¹‖√|D_k| γ_f (p. 4).
  - **Theorem 3.2** (determined or underdetermined case): f°(x*; v) ≥ ∇_s f*ᵀv (pp. 5–6).
  - **Theorem 3.3** (overdetermined case): the same bound plus a lim-sup least-squares-residual term (3.4) (pp. 6–7). The residual projector for coordinate search is R_k = ½[I I; I I] (p. 7), and the term vanishes under strict differentiability (Corollary 3.4, pp. 7–8).
  - **Theorem 3.5**: with |Z_k| ≥ n and strict differentiability, a subsequence of simplex gradients converges to ∇f(x*) (pp. 8–9). The proof uses the Lipschitz mean value theorem with Clarke subgradients and the closedness and local boundedness of ∂f. The paper calls the result a nonsmooth counterpart of Theorem 2.1 (p. 9).
  - **Proposition 1**: ‖Σ⁻¹‖ ≤ 1/(d_min κ(D)), so the cosine measure bounds the poisedness of a poll set (proof via Courant–Fischer, p. 10).
  - **§5** verifies the conditions for GPS, where Λ depends only on D, and for LTMADS, where Λ = n2^{n−1} via a triangular-inverse bound (pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Experiments**: Setup:
  - Problems: 14 nonsmooth problems (Lukšan–Vlček and Haarala; n = 5–50) (Table 6.1, p. 12).
  - Method: coordinate search with [I −I], opportunistic polling and no search step; "basic" vs "order" (store-all, Λ = 100, sample sets of size (n+1)/2 to 2n+1) (pp. 12–13).
  - Reported: evaluations and final values per problem (Table 6.2, p. 13).

  The average reduction in evaluations is about 27%, against about 50% on smooth problems in [8]. Increases are small (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: theory (estimates of Clarke derivatives from simplex gradients, and convergence to the gradient under strict differentiability) with numerical confirmation (p. 13).
  - **Limits, stated**:
    - Theorem 3.3 is only "a first step" (p. 7).
    - The underdetermined case is "not of great interest" (p. 5).
    - Theorem 3.5 cannot hold with fewer than n + 1 points (p. 9).
    - The simplex-gradient stopping test behaves like the mesh-size test (p. 9).
    - Nonsmooth practice is less well understood (p. 13).
  - No future work. SID-PSM is not named in this paper.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 1, 10, 13). The ordering heuristic sits in the poll-ordering slot, which the GPS/MADS convergence theory does not use. The paper supplies the theory for why the heuristic stays informative when f is nonsmooth. This is also evidence for Taste #4 (theory that explains the numerics) ✅.
- Research Heuristic 7 ✅ (p. 13): every previously evaluated point is a candidate for the simplex gradient (store-all).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 12–13): a stated collection and an ablation against the same method without ordering. There are no profiles, no external solver and no code mention.
- c05 candidate *state where you lose* ✅ (pp. 9, 13): the new stopping test is no better than the mesh-size test, the gain is 27% rather than 50%, and increases are reported.
- c03 candidate *import model-geometry theory into direct search* ✅ (pp. 3, 10): Λ-poisedness is linked to the cosine measure.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To show that a sample-based gradient surrogate is meaningful at nonsmooth points, take its limit along refining subsequences and compare it with Clarke's generalized directional derivative: f°(x*; v) ≥ ∇_s f*ᵀv (pp. 5–7).
- Bound the poisedness of a poll set by its cosine measure, ‖Σ⁻¹‖ ≤ 1/(d_min κ(D)) (Proposition 1, p. 10). Any method with a cosine-measure guarantee can then reuse interpolation error bounds.
- For finite-difference-type quantities of a Lipschitz f, use the Lipschitz mean value theorem f(y) − f(x) = s(z)ᵀ(y − x), s(z) ∈ ∂f(z), together with the closedness and local boundedness of ∂f, to pass to the limit (pp. 5, 8–9).
- Run the ablation as the same method with and without the add-on, and give per-problem evaluations *and* final values, so that cheaper-but-worse runs are visible (Table 6.2, p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "Theorem 3.3 should be primarily regarded as a first step to understand the use of overdetermined simplex gradients in the nonsmooth setting." (p. 7)
- "Our numerical experience has shown, however, that the use of this stopping criterion has an effect similar to the use of a stopping criterion in which the algorithm would stop if the size of αk follows below a prescribed tolerance." (p. 9)

### S059 · SID-PSM: A pattern search method guided by simplex derivatives for use in derivative-free optimization (Departamento de Matemática, Universidade de Coimbra 2008, no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. L. Custódio (FCT-UNL) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the user manual of the SID-PSM software, version 1.3. It packages S008 (SIAM J. Optim. 2007, ref. [10]) and S012 (COAP 2010, ref. [9]) and uses S030 (ref. [8]) for a stopping rule (pp. 1, 7, 15). **Read**: full (22 pages). The output logs in Appendices A–C (pp. 17–19) were skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: min f(x) over Ω = Rⁿ or {c_i(x) ≤ 0}, without derivatives of f. In the constrained case the user must supply the constraint gradients (p. 2). The package is for black-box codes and physical experiments (p. 2). What sets it apart from NOMAD/NOMADm and APPSPACK is the reuse of past function evaluations to make pattern search more efficient (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Fig. 2.1 (p. 5) is generalized pattern search with the additions marked in italic (p. 4):
  - **Simplex derivatives**: a Λ-poised sample set is taken from stored points within Δ_k = σ_k α_{k−1} max‖d‖. Its simplex derivatives give a descent indicator: the negative simplex gradient or a simplex Newton direction (pp. 4–5).
  - **Search step**: minimization of a minimum-Frobenius-norm quadratic model in a trust region (pp. 5–6).
  - **Poll**: ordered by the angle to the descent indicator (Fig. 2.2, p. 6).
  - **Mesh update options**: including a sufficient-decrease ratio ρ_k on the simplex model (Fig. 2.3, pp. 6–7).
  - **Other components**: simplex-gradient stopping tests (Fig. 2.4, p. 7), pruning (pp. 7–8), store-all or store-successful lists (p. 8), a cache (p. 8), and cheaper block or QR poisedness checks (pp. 8–9).
  - **Defaults**: order_option 5, search_option 1, pss 2 = [e −e I −I], store_all 1, Λ = 100, tol_alfa 10⁻⁵, fevals_max 1500 (App. D, pp. 20–22; p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Global convergence to stationary points is supported by theory "except in the nonlinear constrained case" (p. 2). Other stated caveats:
  - The model minimizer should be projected onto the mesh for convergence, but the projection is not implemented (p. 6).
  - The conditions that justify pruning are never tested (p. 8).
  - mesh_option 1 may cause premature convergence (pp. 6–7).
  - Equality constraints are discouraged (p. 2).
  - With bounds, the poll set must contain the coordinate directions (pp. 11, 13).
  - Degenerate constraint gradients halt the run (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis**: None. The manual defers to refs. [8]–[10] (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**: No benchmark. Demonstration runs on (x₂ − x₁²)² from (−1.2, 1): unconstrained, 37 iterations and 145 evaluations (Apps. A–B, pp. 17–18); bound-constrained, 31 iterations and 129 evaluations (App. C, p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: software. MATLAB, free for research, educational or commercial use under the GNU LGPL, with a request to cite [9] and [10] (pp. 1–2, 9).
  - **Limits, stated concretely**: MATLAB only (p. 9), constraint gradients required (p. 2), and the theory gaps listed in D3 (pp. 2, 6, 8).
  - No future work.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 1–2, 9–15, App. D). This is the released artefact itself: LGPL, installation notes, drivers, a parameters file, documentation of each file, and a citation request. It also matches Step 1: every strategy is a switchable option (order_option 0–9, search_option, mesh_option, pruning, stop_grad; pp. 20–22), so turning heuristics on and off becomes an ablation.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence with the c03 correction (pp. 3–7). The search step is "free of any rules" and hosts the MFN model; the poll carries convergence. The glue is still the mesh (rational lattice) with simple decrease. Sufficient decrease appears only as a mesh-update *option* (mesh_option 1 or 2, pp. 6–7), as the c03 card found for S008.
- Research Heuristic 7 ✅ (pp. 3–4): past evaluations feed the simplex derivatives and the MFN models at no extra cost in evaluations.
- c04 candidate *disclosed theory–implementation gap* ✅ (pp. 2, 6, 8): no theory for nonlinear constraints, the mesh projection is not implemented, and the pruning conditions are untested.

**Transferable techniques**:
- In the algorithm box, typeset in a distinct style (here italic) exactly what differs from the base method whose theory you inherit (Fig. 2.1, pp. 4–5).
- Expose each heuristic as a numbered option in one parameters file, with its default and range, so that users and later papers can run ablations (App. D, pp. 20–22).
- Put the citation request, naming the method papers, in the abstract of the software manual (p. 1).
- Before each evaluation, look the point up in a cache: first filter with an ℓ1-norm tolerance, then compare with ℓ∞ (pp. 8, 14). This avoids re-evaluating lattice points.
- Ship an unconstrained and a constrained toy driver, and print their expected output in appendices, so that users can verify their installation (pp. 11, 17–19).

**Quotes**:
- "The search step is free of any rules as long as only a finite number of (feasible) trial points in the current mesh is considered." (p. 3)
- "Note that for global convergence purposes the minimizer should be projected onto the mesh. However, this projection is not implemented in SID-PSM." (p. 6)

## Batch synthesis (s3-01)

- **Method 5 starts as infrastructure.** The earliest items of this corpus (1993–1994), which come before any solver, are benchmark generators with Fortran code, a solution checker, an installation test and a separate TOMS software paper (S053 p. 14; S026 p. 16; D001 pp. 1–4). The existing c01 candidate *controlled-difficulty test-problem generators* now has four papers (S002 plus S053, S026, D001). It still lacks four-fold validation: these papers are from one collaboration (Calamai) and one period.
- **The same construction serves two problem classes.** The separable-blocks + Householder–diagonal disguise is built for bilevel QPs (S026) and reused for single-level QPs (S053 p. 2). This is a construction-level analogue of Method 4.
- ***Bridge inequality to a classical proof* recurs** in S084 (pp. 7–10). With s01 (S037, S031, S039) and s2-03 (S085), the candidate now has five papers, all derivative-based and published 1996–2008.
- **SID-PSM, theory and artefact.** S030 supplies the nonsmooth theory for the ordering heuristic (Method 1, Taste #4). S059 is the released artefact (Method 5) and discloses its gaps (pp. 2, 6, 8). Both keep the mesh with simple decrease as the glue, which confirms the c03 correction to Method 1.
- **S109 is a stated source.** It says in Vicente's own words that industrial applications come from one's own research network and that formulating the problem is part of the work (pp. 4–6). It could be added to "Stated methodology (primary)" in the SKILL.md appendix.
