# Paper cards · batch c04 (core)

**Batch intent**: Check, against full texts, how Vicente's group carried the directional direct-search skeleton (optional search step, poll step, step-size update) into four new settings between 2009 and 2012: global optimization with linear constraints (PSwarm), model-based search steps (MFN models in SID-PSM), multiple objectives (DMS) and discontinuous objectives (Rockafellar-derivative analysis). The main questions are (a) how much of the implemented solver the convergence theory actually covers, (b) which object each paper changes and which it leaves alone (Methods 1 and 4), (c) which benchmarking protocol each paper builds (Method 5), and (d) whether SKILL.md's claim that sufficient decrease, not a mesh, is the glue holds for this period.
**Focus dimensions**: D2 (what changed vs. the base direct search), D3 (what the theory assumes vs. what the code does), D4 (proof skeleton), D5 (benchmark protocol), D8 (stated limits). D1, D6 and D7 are also filled because all four papers are core.
**Material roles**: S018 core · S012 core · S003 core · S024 core. All four text files were readable and match the named paper. All four are preprint versions: S018 is dated 16 March 2009, S012 is dated 31 July 2009, S024 is dated 24 November 2010, and S003 carries no date in the text. The published journal versions were not read. The S012 text file has lost its "fi"/"ff" ligatures (e.g. "signicant" for "significant"), so the quotes below were chosen to avoid affected words.

### S018 · PSwarm: A hybrid solver for linearly constrained global derivative-free optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2009, DOI 10.1080/10556780902909948)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. I. F. Vaz · **Position in trajectory**: the follow-up to the bound-constrained PSwarm paper (Vaz & Vicente, J. Global Optim. 2007, cited as [34], p. 2). This paper extends the solver to general linear inequality constraints. It belongs to the direct-search-made-efficient period of SKILL.md's trajectory and is later cited by DMS as a possible swarm search step (S003, p. 27). **Text read**: preprint dated 16 March 2009, pp. 1–22, read in full, including the one-paragraph appendix on performance ratios (p. 20).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Many applied problems (e.g. regression fitting of stellar parameters, p. 2) are nonconvex, derivative-free and need a global minimizer. The authors say mathematical-programming contributions there have been "limited and mostly directed to the application of heuristic techniques", with too little benchmarking (p. 2). The bound-constrained PSwarm worked well (p. 2), but some applications have general linear constraints, often *unrelaxable* ones (f can be evaluated only at feasible points), which the old version could not handle (pp. 5, 18). The problem is posed as min f(z) s.t. Az ≤ b, ℓ ≤ z ≤ u, with bounds deliberately kept separate to exploit them (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: state the constraint class together with its *evaluation status* (relaxable or unrelaxable). That status then justifies a feasible-iterate design (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The skeleton is coordinate search. A particle-swarm step is the search step, and the poll is performed around the best particle (pp. 2, 5). Algorithm 3.1 (pp. 6–7) marks in bold what changes from the bound version. Four things change. (i) The initial swarm is sampled inside a maximum-volume inscribed ellipsoid, x = q + ϱ^{1/n}Eς (pp. 7–8). Unbounded regions get fictitious bounds (6), and the Zhang–Gao interior-point code was rewritten in C (p. 8). (ii) Search feasibility is kept by damping each velocity component: a ratio test against the bounds (8), then a ratio test against the linear constraints along the damped velocity (9) (p. 9). (iii) The poll uses positive generators of the tangent cone of the ε-active constraints with ε = O(α) (p. 10). Algorithm 3.2 halves ε until the active matrix C̄ has full row rank, then takes a QR of C̄ᵀ, B = QR^{-T}, N = I − BC̄, D = [B −B N −N], and falls back to D⊕ otherwise (p. 11). (iv) The poll uses an extreme-barrier f̂, while the search needs none because it is feasible by construction (p. 6). Unchanged: the PSO velocity update (4), dropping of particles that come within α of a better one, and the step-size rule (φ = 2 after two consecutive poll successes in the same direction, θ = 1/2) (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: when a population heuristic must respect constraints, keep its velocity formula and only *scale* the displacement by a ratio test per component and per constraint (eqs. 8–9, p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Only linear inequalities are handled, and equalities converted to inequalities are said to introduce degeneracy (p. 3). f may be nonsmooth or noisy (p. 3). The ellipsoid problem is well posed only if the feasible set is bounded, A has full rank and a strictly feasible point exists (p. 8). **No convergence theorem is stated or proved in this paper** (checked pp. 1–22). Global convergence is claimed only for the bound-constrained version by reference to [34] (pp. 2, 5). For the linear-constraint version the text itself records three departures from what the theory needs. The projection of particles onto the mesh is omitted (p. 7). The sufficient-decrease condition that accompanies ε = O(α) in Kolda–Lewis–Torczon is ignored "to avoid rejecting points which yield a (simple) decrease" (p. 10). The D⊕ fallback in the degenerate case is admitted to be possibly not "aligned to the basic requirements needed for global convergence of the overall algorithm" and is defended by numerical experience and the randomness of the search (pp. 10–11).
  - Transferable technique: list each place where the implementation deviates from the convergence theory's hypotheses, next to the reason for the deviation (pp. 7, 10–11).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: None. There are no lemmas or theorems. The only derivations are constructive: the ratio-test step lengths (8)–(9) (p. 9) and the QR construction of tangent-cone generators (Algorithm 3.2, p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: The test set has 110 linearly constrained problems, extracted from 1564 problems in Vanderbei/CUTE, GLOBALlib and a few papers, all coded in AMPL: 23 with linear, 55 with quadratic and 32 with non-quadratic objectives (p. 11). It also has 10 "highly nonconvex" problems generated with random linear constraints following Parpas et al. and Pintér's objective (Table 1 and Algorithm 4.1, p. 12). The baselines are ASA (simulated annealing), NOMADm (MADS, with a maximal positive basis) and DIRECT (fixed penalty 10⁶, after a check that other values changed little) (pp. 12–13). To be "as fair as possible", no solver gets an initial guess; guesses are drawn at random inside the fictitious bounds (p. 13). PSwarm uses s = 40 and the swarm parameters from [34] (p. 13). Budgets are 2000 evaluations for the 110 problems and 10000 for the 10 hard ones. Stochastic solvers get 10 runs, and performance profiles are drawn separately for the minimum, average and maximum final value (Figs. 3–8, pp. 14–16). NOMADm is restarted from 10 random guesses on the hard set, and it fails when linprog cannot find a feasible start (p. 15). Instrumentation: a poll was attempted in about 70% of iterations, and about 56% of polls succeeded (p. 14). Per-class result: PSwarm is best on linear and non-quadratic objectives, and DIRECT has a small advantage on quadratics (p. 17). A new **function profile** counts evaluations to reach f_{p,s} − f_{p,L} ≤ τ|f_{p,L}| with τ = 0.1, averaged over runs. On the 110 problems NOMADm solves about 40% within 1000 evaluations against PSwarm's 20%, while PSwarm solves about 70% at 2000 and is the most robust (pp. 17–18, Figs. 10–11). DIRECT solves none of the hard problems to τ = 0.1 (p. 18). Data profiles were considered and rejected because some solvers are stochastic and non-monotone and because small budgets matter less for global optimization (p. 17). The appendix modifies the performance ratio to 1 + t − min t when min t < 0.001 (p. 20). There is no comparison with a raw, unwrapped particle swarm.
  - Transferable technique: for stochastic solvers, draw separate profiles for the best, average and worst of 10 runs rather than a single mean (Figs. 3–5, pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable technique: when final values can be near zero, switch the performance ratio from t/min t to 1 + t − min t below a threshold (Appendix, p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: when data profiles do not fit (non-monotone best values, global goal), define a profile of evaluations-to-relative-accuracy against the best value found by any solver, and state the τ used (pp. 17–18).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper criticizes the field's reliance on untested heuristics (p. 2). It builds on its own [34] and places its polling rule inside the linearly constrained direct-search literature: Lewis–Torczon (all generators for ε ∈ [0, ε*]), Lucidi–Sciandrone–Tseng (ε reduced plus projection) and Kolda–Lewis–Torczon (ε = O(α) plus sufficient decrease). It takes KLT's ε-rule but drops its sufficient decrease (p. 10). It calls its degeneracy handling "a simplified version" of NOMADm's (p. 13) and follows Lewis–Torczon and Abramson et al. for degeneracy (p. 10). Moré–Wild data profiles are acknowledged and set aside for stated reasons (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The algorithm box highlights in bold the differences from the earlier version (p. 6). The contributions are summarized in the conclusion in three layers: the extension, "a comprehensive numerical comparison" with a reusable collection, and the new function profiles (pp. 18–20). The authors are candid about process and doubt: the paper "is the result of intensive testing" (p. 18); on nonlinear constraints "It is not clear to us how to proceed toward this goal", and the planned penalty/augmented-Lagrangian version "might not be the way to proceed" (p. 20). There are no theorems and hence no theorem-to-intuition balance to assess.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are software and algorithmic (linear-constraint PSwarm, including a C version, pp. 8, 17), a benchmark collection offered to others (p. 18) and a benchmarking tool (function profiles). Stated limits: nonlinear constraints remain open (p. 20); degenerate handling relies on a fallback that theory does not cover (pp. 10–11). A limit that is visible but not stated as one: the linear-constraint solver has no convergence guarantee in this paper (see D3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (design level). A heuristic (PSO) sits in the optional search step, and the poll around the best particle provides the local guarantee (pp. 2, 5).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. In this paper the globalizing device is simple decrease on a mesh (p. 5), not sufficient decrease. The implementation then relaxes even the mesh requirement (no projection, p. 7) and explicitly declines sufficient decrease (p. 10). Step 3 of Method 1, and the line in its *Different from standard practice* paragraph that makes sufficient decrease rather than a mesh the glue, do not describe this 2009 paper. The globalizing condition is traded for efficiency and defended numerically (pp. 10–11).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. To reach linear constraints only the poll set (tangent-cone generators) and the search feasibility are changed; the step logic is kept (pp. 6–7, 9–11). Heuristic 6 ✅: unrelaxable constraints lead to a feasible method with an extreme barrier (pp. 5–6).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. There is a collected and shared problem set (pp. 11, 18), stochastic solvers are run repeatedly, and a new profile is tailored to global optimization (pp. 17–18, 20). Method 5 ⚠ on step 4: there is no comparison against the unsafeguarded heuristic (baselines listed pp. 12–13).
- New-pattern candidate: **disclosed theory–implementation gap**. The solver departs from the theory's hypotheses and the text says where and why (pp. 7, 10–11). See also S012 (p. 8) and S003 (p. 20, footnote).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Sample an initial population in a polytope from its maximum-volume inscribed ellipsoid: x = q + ϱ^{1/n}Eς, with ϱ ~ U(0,1) and ς normalized. Add fictitious bounds only for this purpose when the region is unbounded (pp. 7–8).
- Obtain tangent-cone generators robustly: shrink ε from min(ε_init, 10α) until the ε-active rows have full row rank, then set D = [B −B N −N] from a QR of C̄ᵀ, and fall back to D⊕ (Algorithm 3.2, p. 11).
- Benchmark fairness move: withhold initial guesses from all solvers and generate them at random inside common bounds (p. 13).
- Report instrumentation of the hybrid (fraction of iterations reaching the poll, poll success rate) to show which component does the work (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "However, our numerical experience has shown us that this is a robust and efficient way of handling degeneracy." (p. 11)
- "It is not clear to us how to proceed toward this goal." (p. 20)

### S012 · Incorporating minimum Frobenius norm models in direct search (Computational Optimization and Applications 2010, DOI 10.1007/s10589-009-9283-0)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. L. Custódio, H. Rocha · **Position in trajectory**: it upgrades the search step of SID-PSM (Custódio & Vicente 2007; Custódio, Dennis & Vicente 2008, refs [5, 6], p. 6), whose earlier search step was "very crude", a diagonal-Hessian quadratic (p. 6). It imports Powell/Scheinberg model technology into direct search. DMS later cites it as a possible DMS search step (S003, p. 27). **Text read**: preprint dated 31 July 2009, pp. 1–17, read in full. There is no appendix. The data-profile figures are captions only in the text file (pp. 11–14).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is someone else's benchmark. Moré–Wild [10] showed interpolation-based trust-region methods to be more efficient and robust than direct search, and the result for piecewise-smooth and noisy problems was "relatively surprising" (p. 2). Direct search keeps convergence properties for nonsmooth problems and is easy to code and parallelize (p. 2). "It is therefore natural to ask for a combination of both techniques" (p. 2). The goal is a hybrid that competes with model-based methods on smooth problems and perhaps beats them on noisy or nonsmooth ones (pp. 6–7).
  - Transferable technique: start from a published benchmark in which your method family loses, and ask which ingredient of the winner can be transplanted into your family's optional component (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Keep the directional direct-search structure (search plus poll) and build quadratic models from points *already evaluated* during the run, so that minimizing the model in a trust region becomes the search step (abstract, p. 1; pp. 6–7). Design choices, each reported as the survivor of tried alternatives: store-all rather than store-successful (p. 7); a search attempted as soon as a first model exists, reusing the last model if no new one is formed (p. 7); up to (n+1)(n+2) points (p. 7); trust-region radius Δ_k = σ_k α_{k−1} max‖d‖, with σ = 1 after an unsuccessful and 2 after a successful iteration and floor 10⁻⁵ (p. 7). For n+2 to (n+1)(n+2)/2 points it uses an MFN model (2); Powell's least-updating MFN (3) "performed worse in our context" (p. 8). Above (n+1)(n+2)/2 points there are two variants: discard down to complete interpolation, or unregularized least-squares regression, since ρ = 0 was best (p. 8). 80% of the points are nearest to x_k and 20% farthest, with P = 20 found by trial (p. 8). Geometry control is deliberately "extremely loose": singular values below machine eps are replaced by eps instead of a poisedness test (pp. 8–9). The poll is [e −e I −I] (adding ±e "allows to change all the components at once"), opportunistic because complete polling "performs clearly worse", ordered by the negative simplex gradient when a Λ-poised subset exists and cyclic otherwise (p. 9). The step size is kept on success and halved on failure (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: tie the search trust-region radius to the direct-search step size of the previous iteration, so that no separate radius update is needed (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: §2 recalls error bounds for C¹ functions with a Lipschitz gradient: Theorem 2.1 (underdetermined quadratic interpolation, with the constant containing ‖H‖) and Theorem 2.2 (the MFN Hessian bounded by C_{p,q}C_fΛ_F). Together they show that MFN models are fully linear under Λ-poisedness (pp. 4–5; both taken from the CSV book [4, Thms 5.4, 5.7]). The algorithm, however, **does not enforce** Λ_F- or Λ_L-poisedness in the search (p. 8). The paper also states **no convergence result** for the resulting method (the word appears only in the introduction, p. 2). Acceptance is simple decrease (p. 6). The paper describes search points as lying "in the current mesh" (p. 6), but it does not say how the trust-region minimizer is placed on the mesh (not stated, pp. 6–8). Constraints are out of scope (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No new proofs. The one analytic argument is a design justification: since the gradient-error constant in Theorem 2.1 grows with ‖H‖, the Frobenius norm of the model Hessian is the quantity to minimize (p. 4). Combining this with the Hessian bound gives the fully-linear error bound (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: read your error bound's constant, identify the quantity the designer controls (here ‖H‖), and make minimizing it the model-selection criterion (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: The paper uses Moré–Wild data profiles with a budget of 1500 evaluations counted in units of n+1 ("natural in derivative-free optimization", p. 10). There are two accuracies (τ = 10⁻³, 10⁻⁷) and four classes of 53 problems with n ∈ [2, 12]: smooth, nonstochastic noisy, piecewise smooth, stochastic noisy (pp. 9–10). The baselines are NEWUOA, NMSMAX (Nelder–Mead) and APPSPACK in serial mode with random ordering (p. 10). Moré and Wild supplied the NEWUOA and APPSPACK results and the profile code (pp. 15–16). Results, including against the authors' own method: NEWUOA is best on smooth problems. SID-PSM wins only on noisy or nonsmooth problems at larger budgets, and there it is the best of the four. The regression variant becomes competitive with NEWUOA even on smooth problems, and the gap to NEWUOA shrinks at high accuracy (p. 10). Both variants beat APPSPACK and NMSMAX at τ = 10⁻⁷ (p. 10). Ablations appear in prose only (least-updating MFN, complete polling, DGQT vs trust.m, regularization, P%) (pp. 7–9). No plot compares against the pre-MFN SID-PSM.
  - Transferable technique: report the four Moré–Wild problem classes separately at two accuracy levels, and state explicitly the regime (smooth, small budget) where the strongest baseline still wins (pp. 2, 10).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper frames model-based methods (DFO by Scheinberg, NEWUOA by Powell, MNH by Wild) as the efficiency benchmark (pp. 2, 5). It tests Powell's least-updating alternative and reports it as worse *in this context* (p. 8). It justifies loose geometry by analogy with Fasano–Morales–Nocedal's observations on badly poised sets (p. 8). It builds directly on its own SID-PSM papers (pp. 6–7). It claims, "to our knowledge", to be the first attempt to use MFN-model search steps in directional direct search (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper is short, at 17 preprint pages. The framing sentence "what might be considered as a natural way" (p. 2) and the reporting convention "We will report the various possibilities and describe the ones which we found to be the most successful" (p. 2) set up a design-by-elimination narrative. Hedges are frequent ("seemed to indicate", p. 8; "perhaps better", p. 7). The negative result is stated in the introduction, not buried: performance "is still below" model-based methods on smooth problems at small budgets (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are algorithmic and software (SID-PSM with an MFN search step is available, p. 15) plus numerical evidence. Stated limits: inferior to NEWUOA on smooth problems with small budgets (pp. 2, 10); the constrained MFN search step is "still subject of future research" (p. 15). Unstated but visible: no convergence statement, and the geometry is uncontrolled by design (D3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. Models go in the search step, and optionality licenses loose geometry: "Since the search step is optional, this is an attempt to explore all model information independently of the quality of the underlying sample sets" (p. 8).
- Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence. All previously evaluated points are reused (store-all) and simplex gradients order the poll (pp. 7, 9). This is the case SKILL.md cites.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. Acceptance is by simple decrease (p. 6) and no convergence result is given, so the convergent skeleton is inherited by reference to direct search and never checked for the modified method (pp. 2, 6–9).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. The paper uses a standard collection with four classes, profiles in evaluation units of n+1, and the strongest available model-based baseline, and reports where it loses (pp. 9–10). The code is released (p. 15).
- Inner Tension *Direct-search skeleton vs borrowing derivatives and models* ✅ evidence. The paper states its goal as competing with interpolation-based trust region by importing its models (pp. 2, 6–7).
- New-pattern candidate: **disclosed theory–implementation gap**. The paper recalls a fully-linear theory (pp. 4–5) and then turns off the geometry control it requires (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the model from a mix of 80% nearest and 20% farthest stored points, to keep locality while preserving some geometry (p. 8).
- Replace poisedness management in an optional step by SVD thresholding at machine precision (pp. 8–9).
- Add ±e (all-ones) to the coordinate poll set so that one poll direction changes all variables at once, and use cyclic polling when no simplex gradient is available (p. 9).
- Measure data-profile budgets in units of n+1 to pool problems of different dimensions, and say why n+1 (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "The approach taken here is to maintain the structure of directional direct-search methods, organized around a search and a poll step, and to use the set of previously evaluated points generated during a direct-search run to build the models." (p. 1)
- "The geometry control of the sample set used for the model computation is extremely loose." (p. 8)

### S003 · Direct multisearch for multiobjective optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2011, DOI 10.1137/10079731X)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. L. Custódio, J. F. A. Madeira, A. I. F. Vaz · **Position in trajectory**: the opening of SKILL.md's new-problem-classes period. It reuses the algorithm template and the convergence text of the companion discontinuous-functions paper S024, which it cites as [47], "to appear" (pp. 27, 33). It lists S018 and S012 as future search-step add-ons (p. 27). Most of it was written while Vicente visited the Courant Institute (p. 1). **Text read**: preprint (undated in the text), pp. 1–33, read in full, including Appendix A (pp. 28–31). Figure axes are extracted as bare numbers and were not interpreted.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Several conflicting black-box or noisy objectives rule out derivative-based techniques (p. 1). A-priori aggregation needs weights or goals, returns one point and must be rerun if preferences change (p. 2). Varying weights may not spread points evenly, especially on nonconvex fronts, and normal-boundary intersection can return dominated points (p. 2). The paper enters by extending direct search itself to MOO without any aggregation, in the a-posteriori class that otherwise contains mainly genetic algorithms and simulated annealing (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The single incumbent becomes an **iterate list** L_k of nondominated pairs (x; α), and **success means the list changed** (pp. 4–5). Each iteration picks a poll center from the list, runs an optional search step and a poll step, forms a temporary list, filters out dominated points (Algorithm 2.2) and selects a trial list (Algorithm 2.3 keeps all; Algorithm 2.4 keeps a single dominating point) (pp. 4–7). For the convergence theory the trial list "must necessarily include" all nondominated points of the previous list (p. 4). One statement covers both globalizations through ρ̄ ∈ {0, forcing function}. Sufficient decrease is recast as F(x) ∉ D(L; ρ̄(α)), with D(L; a) the ℓ∞-neighbourhood of the region dominated by L (p. 4). Unchanged from single-objective direct search: the search/poll structure, the step-size update per point, and extreme-barrier constraints F_Ω (pp. 3–5). With m = 1 DMS reduces to direct search (p. 2). Opportunistic and complete polling are both covered because the theory only uses unsuccessful iterations (p. 6). The later improvements are a cache with an ℓ∞ tolerance of 10⁻³ and a poll center chosen as the list point with the largest gap Γ (p. 25).
  - Transferable technique: to lift a single-point method to a set-valued goal, make the iterate a list, define success as *the list changed*, and write down which list elements must be kept for the proof to survive (pp. 4–7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumption 4.1 requires the union of the objectives' level sets to be compact and F bounded (p. 9). Theorem 4.8 needs F Lipschitz near x* and a refining direction in the hypertangent cone (p. 13). Theorem 4.9 needs the refining directions to be dense in the Clarke tangent cone. The authors flag this as "stronger than just considering that the normalized set of directions D is dense" (p. 13) and argue it is needed even without constraints, because the cone of simultaneous descent can be arbitrarily narrow (p. 14). The lattice route needs Assumptions A.1–A.4 (integer structure, rational step updates, a mesh-restricted search) (pp. 28–29). The sufficient-decrease route needs only A.5, α‖d‖ → 0 ⇔ α → 0 (p. 30). **Theory–practice gap disclosed**: the numerics use D_k = [I −I], which is not dense, with ρ̄ = 0. A footnote says a randomized orthogonal poll [Q −Q] was tried and "the results were not better" (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The main text gives intuition and the appendix gives rigour (pp. 9–10). (i) **Lattice route** (Thm A.1, p. 30): if α is bounded below, all points lie on an integer lattice intersected with a compact set, which is finite. A point removed from the list can never re-enter "by transitivity" of dominance, and each success adds a new point, so there are only finitely many successes, which is a contradiction. (ii) **Sufficient-decrease route** (Thm A.2, pp. 30–31): each success places a new point that carves a hypercube of side ≥ ρ* out of the region nondominated by the list, and no later point can land there. Infinitely many successes would contradict compactness. (iii) **Stationarity** (Thm 4.8, p. 13): the Clarke–Jahn lim sup is bounded below along a refining subsequence after adding and subtracting ρ̄(α_k‖d_k‖), which vanishes relative to α_k‖d_k‖. For each k some objective j(k) fails to improve, and a pigeonhole over the finitely many objectives fixes one j(d) (p. 13). Reading note (my observation, not the authors'): the step from x_k + α_k d_k being not nondominated relative to L_k to a per-component inequality *against x_k* is stated without argument. It follows from transitivity of dominance plus the nondominance of L_k (p. 13). Density then gives Pareto–Clarke criticality (Thm 4.9, p. 14). Sanity corollaries: m = 1 recovers Clarke stationarity (Cor. 4.10), and an eventually singleton list gives an ideal point (Cor. 4.11) (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: for list-based methods, prove lim inf α_k = 0 with a *packing* argument in objective space. Each success removes a ρ*-hypercube from a bounded region (pp. 30–31).
  - Transferable technique: when a per-iteration witness j(k) varies, pass to a subsequence on which it is constant by pigeonhole over a finite index set (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: The test set has 100 bound-constrained MOO problems from the literature, recoded in AMPL and made public at mat.uc.pt/dms. Table 5.1 gives 69 bi-objective, 30 tri-objective and 1 four-objective problem (FES3), with n up to 30. Some literature formulations were found to contain errors (pp. 15–16). Eight public solvers were tested, and only the best three (AMOSA, BIMADS for m = 2 only, NSGA-II in C) are reported, the rest online (p. 15). The metrics are chosen to work without the true front (p. 15). **Purity** is used only in pairwise comparisons, because similar solvers depress each other's purity (p. 17). Stochastic solvers are reported as the best and worst of 10 runs (p. 17). **Spread Γ** is the maximum gap including extreme points, and **Δ** extends Deb's measure to m ≥ 2 (pp. 17–18). Extreme points come from all runs of all solvers (pp. 18–19). **Data profiles for MOO** count a problem as solved when |F_{p,s} ∩ F_p| ≥ (1 − ε)|F_p|/|S| (p. 19). Spread metrics are excluded from data profiles because they are not monotone in the budget (pp. 19–20). A DMS ablation compares four list initializations. DMS(n,line) is best, but its gain over DMS(1) is "not overwhelming" (pp. 20–21). The budget is 20000 evaluations; AMOSA and NSGA-II always use it, while DMS may stop at α < 10⁻³ (p. 22). Results are reported with their losses: NSGA-II is slightly more efficient on Δ (p. 23) and slightly more robust than DMS in its best-run front (p. 23), BIMADS wins clearly on ZDT4 (p. 24), and on the true fronts of ZDT1–6 DMS reaches purity 0.80–0.99 on four problems but 0.029 on ZDT4 (Table 6.1, p. 25). The worked example on SP1 shows three iterations in detail (pp. 7–9).
  - Transferable technique: when a metric is corrupted by similar competitors (purity), restrict it to pairwise comparisons and say why (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: exclude from data profiles any metric that is not monotone in the budget, since a problem could otherwise flip back to "unsolved" (pp. 19–20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the a-posteriori class and against weighted sums and normal-boundary intersection (Das–Dennis), citing their known drawbacks (p. 2). It claims to generalize "all" directional direct search: GPS, GSS and MADS (pp. 1–2). The nonsmooth analysis follows Audet–Dennis MADS, with the definitions "taken from [2]" (pp. 11–12). The lim-inf results are credited to Torczon, Audet–Dennis and Kolda–Lewis–Torczon (p. 30). The competitors are evolutionary codes and BIMADS. The tone toward them is neutral and data-driven (pp. 22–25).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper coins a name, "direct multisearch", justified by the reduction to direct search for m = 1 (p. 2). The abstract states the gap between theory and practice openly: the proof gives only "at least one limit point" in a stationary form of the front, while "extensive computational experience" shows an "impressive capability" to generate the whole front (p. 1). The test set and the MOO profiles are framed as "by-products" (p. 1). A worked-example section sits before the theory (§3). The intuition for lim inf α = 0 is given in the main text and the rigour in the appendix (pp. 9–10). The conclusion calls the principles "extremely simple" (p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are an algorithmic framework, a convergence analysis, a public test set, and metrics and profiles for MOO. Stated limits: the theory guarantees only a limit point in a stationary form of the front, "as no aggregation or scalarization technique is incorporated" (p. 26); the density assumption is stronger than a dense direction set (p. 13); the implementation uses a non-dense poll (p. 20). Future directions named: swarm or other dissemination search steps, MFN and simplex-gradient models, parallel polling (m|D_k| evaluations), user preferences inserted at any of five places, and a discontinuous extension via S024 (pp. 27–28).

**Method links**:

The proofs carry over nearly verbatim from single-objective direct search (pp. 13, 30–31). Step 6 (native metrics: purity, Γ, Δ) is confirmed (pp. 17–18).

### S024 · Analysis of direct searches for discontinuous functions (Mathematical Programming 2012, DOI 10.1007/s10107-010-0429-8)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. L. Custódio · **Position in trajectory**: the regularity-weakening companion of DMS. It shares DMS's algorithm template and globalization text almost word for word (compare pp. 5–9 here with S003 pp. 4–5, 28–31), and DMS cites it for a discontinuous generalization (S003, p. 27). Part of it was also developed during Vicente's Courant Institute visit (p. 1). SKILL.md records that a later paper (Audet, Bouchet & Bourdin 2024) gave a counterexample to this paper's result. That paper was not read here. **Text read**: preprint dated 24 November 2010, pp. 1–31, read in full, including the Appendix on constrained upper subderivatives (pp. 23–29).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The known result (Audet–Dennis) is that the Clarke–Jahn derivative is nonnegative along refining directions *if f is Lipschitz near the limit point* (p. 3). "not too much is known" about direct search on discontinuous f beyond the fact that limits are limits of unsuccessful poll centers on ever finer meshes (pp. 3–4). The applications named are the Omega portfolio ratio, algorithm-parameter tuning, automatic error analysis and aircraft design (p. 2). The entry point is to weaken the regularity assumption of a known stationarity theorem while keeping the algorithm.
  - Transferable technique: motivate a regularity-weakening paper with concrete, cited application classes where the stronger assumption fails (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: No new algorithm. Algorithm 2.1 (p. 6) is the generic directional method with an optional search, an opportunistic poll and ρ̄ ∈ {0, forcing function}. The one design change is to apply the forcing function to the actual displacement, **ρ̄(α_k‖d_k‖) instead of ρ̄(α_k)**. This makes MADS and sufficient decrease "compatible" (p. 6; footnote 1, p. 12). The analytic idea is to replace the Clarke derivative by Rockafellar's upper subderivative f↑, extended here to the constrained case (p. 4). There are two parts: (i) nonnegativity of f↑ along refining directions with respect to which f is directionally Lipschitz (§3); (ii) identification of the locally best "step" when f is piecewise Lipschitz with two pieces (§4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The level set is bounded and f is bounded below (Assumption 2.1, p. 7). The lattice route needs Assumptions 2.2–2.5; the sufficient-decrease route needs only α‖d‖ → 0 ⇔ α → 0 (Assumption 2.6, p. 9). Theorem 3.1 requires f lower semicontinuous at x*, f directionally Lipschitz with respect to the refining direction v ∈ H_Ω(x*), **and lim f(x_k) = f(x*) along the refining subsequence**. This last condition is assumed, not proved, in §3 (p. 11). Theorem 3.2 (v in the tangent cone) additionally requires refining directions dense in a ball around v intersected with H_Ω, and directional Lipschitzness on that set (pp. 12–13). §4 assumes a finite partition near x* into sets with nonempty interior, the exterior-cone property, and f Lipschitz inside each set with a continuous extension (Assumption 4.1, p. 13), plus density of refining directions for every infinite subsequence (Thm 4.1, p. 15). The strong conclusion needs exactly two pieces (Cor. 4.1, p. 17). A claimed by-product is a nonsmooth analysis of GSS-type methods, not previously available "even for the continuous case" (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: (i) Theorem 3.1 (pp. 11–12) is the Audet–Dennis lim-sup argument with the base points constrained to x_k →_f x*, which is where lim f(x_k) = f(x*) enters. The ρ̄ term is added and subtracted and vanishes for both globalizations. It gives f⁺(x*; v) ≥ 0. (ii) The identification f↑ = f⁺ = f°_R on the directionally Lipschitz set D_{f,Ω} is Theorem A.2 (pp. 27–29). It is obtained by extending Rockafellar 1980 to Ω ≠ ℝⁿ: the epigraph of f↑(x; ·) equals the tangent cone of the restricted epigraph (Prop. A.1 and eq. (7), pp. 25–26), and directional Lipschitzness is equivalent to hypertangency of (v, β) to the epigraph (Thm A.1, p. 27). (iii) Theorem 3.2 then follows from the lim-inf representation (9) (p. 13). (iv) §4 uses a **proof by extension**. A continuous extension of a Lipschitz function to the closure is Lipschitz with constant 3L + 2 (Prop. 4.1, p. 14). Using the exterior cone and McShane's extension, f is extended to a Lipschitz function that strictly decreases into an exterior cone (Prop. 4.2, p. 15). If all poll points stayed in the closure of one piece, the Clarke derivative of the extension would be ≥ 0 along a dense set of refining directions, which contradicts that strict decrease. Hence infinitely many poll points leave the piece (Thm 4.1, pp. 15–17). With two pieces, monotonicity of {f(x_k)} and lower semicontinuity give f(x_k) → f(x*) along the better piece (Cor. 4.1, p. 17). Which step the 2024 counterexample targets was **not determined** from this text.
  - Transferable technique: to analyse behaviour at a discontinuity, extend the function from one piece to a Lipschitz function with a designed property (strict decrease on a cone). Apply the known Lipschitz theory to the extension, and derive a contradiction about where the poll points must go (pp. 15–17).
- **D5 Experiments**: Four 2-D lower-semicontinuous functions on [−1, 1]² with a unique minimizer at the origin, each built to satisfy or violate a specific hypothesis (pp. 17–21). f1 has two pieces separated by a constant gap. f2 is continuous but not Lipschitz. f3 has a piece with empty interior, so it is not directionally Lipschitz in any direction. f4 has four pieces, violating n_B = 2. Each example states its directionally Lipschitz cone (pp. 18–21). The solvers are NOMADm 4.6 (LTMADS, 2n directions) and a simple sufficient-decrease implementation with the random orthogonal poll [Q −Q] and ρ(t) = t². The search step is empty, the poll order is fixed because "(proper) convergence rather than efficiency" is the concern, and runs stop at α = 10⁻⁷ (p. 21). Protocol: 10 runs from x0 = (−0.4, −0.5), reporting failures and mean evaluations (Table 1, p. 22). Then a 100-point grid of starting points × 10 runs = 1000 runs per function at tolerances 10⁻⁷ and 10⁻¹⁰ (Table 2, p. 22). Findings: f3 fails 1000/1000, and f4 fails 61 then 44 times. The two f1 failures at 10⁻⁷ vanish at 10⁻¹⁰ and are attributed to "not enough directions" (pp. 22–23). The failures of f4 persist.
  - Transferable technique: build a test suite in which each function violates exactly one hypothesis of your theorem, and rerun at a tighter tolerance to separate slow asymptotics (too few directions generated so far) from a violated assumption (pp. 22–23).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues the Clarke-calculus analysis of Audet–Dennis for GPS and MADS [4, 5], together with [2] and [11] (p. 5). It generalizes Rockafellar [20] to the constrained case and notes that Rockafellar's hypertangency definition differs from Clarke's, which "later became the standard" (p. 11). It credits Bogani–Gasparo–Papini for GSS under a specific nondifferentiability, as a contrast to its own general by-product (p. 3). Its acknowledgements credit a colleague for pointing to the McShane extension result (p. 29).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract states the generalization with its hypotheses in three sentences (p. 1). The paper openly guides the reader: "Reading the Sections 2.4 and 2.9 of the book [9] will definitely help", followed by the geometric meaning of f↑ through the tangent cone of the epigraph, so that readers can compute examples in 1-D and 2-D (pp. 10–11). The final remarks are modest ("we tried to shed some light", p. 23) and give a precise, mechanistic reason why the result stops at two pieces (p. 23). The numerical section is framed as illustration and closes with a strong claim of agreement with theory (pp. 22–23).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is purely theoretical (nonsmooth-analysis extension plus the best-branch result), with 2-D illustrations. Stated limits: only two pieces (p. 23); results hold at limit points of refining subsequences under density of refining directions (pp. 10, 13, 15). Visible but unstated limits: lim f(x_k) = f(x*) is a hypothesis in §3 (p. 11); the numerics are 2-D only (pp. 17–22). No future work is named beyond the two-piece barrier (p. 23). External fact from SKILL.md (not verified here): a 2024 counterexample to the paper's result.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The algorithm skeleton is untouched, and the stationarity notion is changed to fit the new class (Clarke to Rockafellar upper subderivative) (pp. 4, 6, 11).
- Method 4 limitation / Inner Tension *Generality vs correctness* ⚠. The text shows *where* the reach is extended: lim f(x_k) = f(x*) is assumed (p. 11), density is required in every subsequence (p. 15), and the constrained Rockafellar calculus is rebuilt in an appendix (pp. 23–29). Whether the 2024 counterexample hits one of these steps could not be checked from this text.
- Heuristic 10 ⚠ variant. The paper does test its own hypotheses adversarially: f3 and f4 are built to violate assumptions (pp. 19–21), and a 1000-run grid is used (p. 22). This probes the *assumptions*, not the *proof*. Numerical agreement (first quote below, p. 23) cannot detect a gap in a limit argument. This refines Heuristic 10: counterexample search must target the proof steps, not only the hypotheses.
- Method 2: no link (no complexity content, checked pp. 1–31).
- New-pattern candidate: **unified ρ̄ statement**, here with the refinement ρ̄(α_k‖d_k‖) that makes the lattice and sufficient-decrease routes compatible in one proof (p. 6; footnote 1, p. 12). See also S003 (pp. 4, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
