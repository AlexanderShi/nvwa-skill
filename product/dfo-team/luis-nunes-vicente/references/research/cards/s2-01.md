### S081 · A comparison between line searches and trust regions for nonlinear optimization (Investigação Operacional 1996, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: single author; thanks J. Dennis, M. El-Alem, M. Heinkenschloss, R. Tapia and V. Torczon (p. 7) · **Position in trajectory**: written while a graduate student at Rice (footnote, p. 1). It expands the thesis discussion of line searches vs trust regions and of the trust region as regularization (S040, card b01, thesis pp. 36–38). Derivative-based. **Read**: full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is an asymmetry between two classical theorems.
  - Line searches with Armijo–Goldstein–Wolfe conditions need a uniform bound on the condition number cn(Hk) to keep the angle between −gk and the quasi-Newton direction away from π/2 (Theorem 2.1, pp. 2–3).
  - Trust regions need only ‖Hk‖ bounded for lim ‖gk‖ = 0 (Theorem 2.2, p. 4).
  - The author notes the comparison is unfair, because the trust-region step is left unspecified while the line-search step lies along the quasi-Newton direction (§3, p. 4).
  - The claim to be supported is that the trust region has a built-in regularization of ill-conditioned second-order approximations (abstract, p. 1). The problem is stated as two questions: under what conditions does the scaled quasi-Newton step satisfy fraction of Cauchy decrease (8), and fraction of optimal decrease (9)? (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Take away the trust region's freedom to choose a direction. The step is the quasi-Newton step −Hk⁻¹gk, scaled back to the radius when it is longer than ∆k, formula (10) (p. 4). The ratio test and the radius update stay as in the classical method (pp. 3–4). Answer: a uniform bound on cn(Hk) makes the scaled step satisfy (8) (Theorem 4.1, p. 5) and (9) (Theorem 4.2, p. 6). The restricted trust region therefore inherits the line-search assumption, so the weaker trust-region assumption is bought by the freedom of direction.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: unconstrained; f continuously differentiable, bounded below, ∇f uniformly continuous; Hk symmetric positive definite; ℓ2 norm (pp. 1–2, 4). The only new hypothesis is cn(Hk) ≤ κ (pp. 2, 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 4.1: when the step is cut to the boundary, its model decrease is at least a half of ∆k‖gk‖ times the cosine of the angle (11). The Cauchy decrease is bounded in each of the two branches of (7), and the cosine bound (6) closes the argument (p. 5).
  - Theorem 4.2: bounds the optimal model decrease using ‖s*k‖ ≤ ∆k < ‖Hk⁻¹gk‖ (p. 6). It could also be obtained from Theorem 4.1 and a result of Byrd, Schnabel and Shultz [2] (p. 6).
  - Both constants depend only on κ. Their exact form is not legible because the extraction drops κ (pp. 5–6).
  - Example 4.1, taken from [2], is a 3×3 diagonal H with a small parameter (the symbol is dropped in the extraction). For a small radius, the quasi-Newton model decrease is of higher order (O(·⁶)) than the Cauchy decrease (O(·⁵)), so (8) fails in the limit (p. 5).
  - Final remarks: the Armijo condition is rewritten as a ratio (12) and set next to the trust-region ratio, which contains the curvature term s^T Hk s (p. 6). A line search can be regularized by adding µI to Hk, but µ must then be chosen, whereas in the trust region µ is fixed implicitly by ∆k through (Hk + µI)s*k = −gk and the complementarity condition (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: a theoretical clarification (two theorems and one example) of a claim about trust regions (p. 1). No limitations or future directions are stated (pp. 6–7). The scope is unconstrained problems with positive definite Hk (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 4–6): the trust-region skeleton accepts a foreign step (the quasi-Newton direction), and the fraction of Cauchy decrease is what connects that step to the convergence theorem. Unlike Method 1, the foreign step gets no free pass: it needs an extra assumption (bounded condition number). Derivative-based precursor.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (p. 6): the two globalizations are compared through their acceptance tests written in a common ratio form. The paper compares acceptance tests; it does not generalize one.
- new-pattern candidate: *bridge inequality to a classical proof* (pp. 4–5): prove that the new step satisfies the classical sufficient-decrease condition, then cite Powell's theorem.
- new-pattern candidate: *negative example for the rejected alternative* (p. 5): the borrowed diagonal example shows the forced trust region failing without the condition-number bound.
- new-pattern candidate: *handicap the flexible method to isolate what its freedom buys* (pp. 4–6). New.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To compare two globalization strategies fairly, restrict the more flexible one to the other's step (here, the trust region along the quasi-Newton direction) and ask what assumption the restricted version needs. The difference names what the flexibility buys (pp. 4–6).
- To show that a new step inherits a classical theorem, prove that it satisfies the classical decrease condition (fraction of Cauchy decrease) with a constant depending only on your assumption, then cite the theorem (Theorem 4.1 → Theorem 2.2, pp. 4–5).
- Pair the positive theorem with a small diagonal counterexample in which a parameter goes to zero, showing that the assumption cannot be dropped (Example 4.1, p. 5).
- Rewrite competing acceptance tests in one common ratio form, so the reader sees which quantity (here, curvature) each uses (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "The question we ask and then answer in this short paper supports this claim." (p. 1)
- "One can see that trust regions use curvature to accept or reject a step but line searches do not." (p. 6)

### H007 · Métodos de optimização para controlo óptimo e projecto de engenharia (Actas da I Conferência Nacional de Telecomunicações 1997, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: single author; member of the Applied Mathematics for Telecommunications line of the Instituto de Telecomunicações, Coimbra; funded by Praxis XXI (footnotes, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: first Coimbra years. Proceedings pp. 437–440. A Portuguese-language dissemination note for a telecommunications audience on the TRICE project with Heinkenschloss (p. 1, [11] p. 4). **Read**: full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The class is min f(y,u) subject to C(y,u) = 0, y ≥ 0, u ≥ 0, with states y and controls or design variables u. Such problems arise in optimal control, engineering design and parameter identification, which the note links to telecommunications (p. 1). The entry is a view of the field: optimization researchers now look for problem classes with special structure coming from applications, because that structure lets existing algorithms be adapted and lets them profit from computing advances (§I, p. 1). The discretized problem should keep its infinite-dimensional functional structure in view (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - TRICE composes three existing techniques (p. 2): SQP; trust regions, for global convergence and to regularize ill-conditioned quadratic subproblems; and interior-point affine scaling (Coleman–Li [4]) for the bounds on u.
  - Structure used: the user's solvers for the linearized state equation (5) and the adjoint equation (6); the adjoint gives the reduced gradient (7) (pp. 1–2).
  - Both y and u are independent variables (all-at-once). The reduced formulation (8)–(9) would require computing y(u), which can be very expensive when the state equation is highly nonlinear (p. 2).
  - The implemented algorithms cover only the case without state bounds, (10)–(12) (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and C once or twice continuously differentiable; m < n; the reduced formulation assumes the implicit function theorem applies (pp. 1–2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no proofs; results are cited from [5], [13] and [10].
  - The properties come in three tiers (p. 2): global convergence to first-order points using first derivatives; to second-order points if second derivatives are used; and a local q-quadratic rate near points satisfying second-order sufficient conditions, where the trust regions become inactive.
  - The inexactness analysis [10] computes tolerances for the linearized state solves, the adjoint solves and all derivative computations from quantities known at each iteration (pp. 2–3).
- **D5 Experiments**: none reported. The interface is said to have been tested successfully on a wide range of application problems (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: software and dissemination.
    - Fortran 77 and Matlab implementations; the Matlab version has a GUI (p. 3).
    - Dynamic problem scaling; limited-memory secant options [2]; a choice of algorithms according to ill-conditioning, desired rate and the number of linearized or adjoint solves (p. 3).
    - The user supplies f, C and first derivatives, plus either (i) linearized state and adjoint solves, or (ii) linearized state solves and products with the sensitivity matrix S and its transpose. Option (ii) is efficient only when there are few controls (p. 3).
    - Code and user's guide at http://www.caam.rice.edu/trice (p. 3).
  - **Future work (§V, p. 3)**: the state-bounded problem (1)–(3), with preconditioners [1], interior-point algorithms [14] (= H003), local convergence under degeneracy following Wright [15], and global convergence with trust regions plus an efficient implementation.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (p. 3): the solver is released in two languages with a user's guide at a public URL and a GUI, and its interface is presented as a modular, almost object-oriented design that differs from most optimization software.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (p. 2): global convergence comes from a trust-region skeleton wrapped around proven components (SQP steps, affine scaling), not around a heuristic.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pp. 2–3): inexact linear solves and finite-difference derivatives are allowed, with tolerances computed each iteration. The control is deterministic; Method 3 later makes such requirements probabilistic.
- new-pattern candidate: *design around the user's existing oracle* (pp. 1–3): the algorithms consume the application's own linearized-state and adjoint solvers.
- new-pattern candidate: *implementable accuracy conditions* (pp. 2–3): tolerances computed from quantities known at the start of each iteration.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Offer the user two equivalent oracle options (adjoint solves, or sensitivity-matrix products) and state when each is efficient (sensitivities only for few controls) (p. 3).
- In a short overview, list convergence properties in tiers keyed to the derivative information the user supplies (first derivatives → first-order points; second derivatives → second-order points; local q-quadratic rate) (p. 2).

**Quotes**: none. The accented Portuguese letters are encoded as control characters in the extraction, so no content-bearing sentence can be copied verbatim.

### H003 · On interior-point Newton algorithms for discretized optimal control problems with state constraints (Optimization Methods & Software 1998, DOI 10.1080/10556789808805679)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: single author; thanks Dennis and Heinkenschloss and two referees (p. 19) · **Position in trajectory**: published in OMS 8 (1998) 249–275. Coimbra; circulated as preprint 96-18 (H007 ref. [14], p. 4). It extends the TRIP line (S011 = [10], S013 = [17], thesis S040 = [28]) from bounds on controls to bounds on states. **Read**: full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The TRIP class (1.1) gains bounds y ≥ 0 on the states (p. 1).
  - The entry point is a structural failure. Regularity (full row rank of the Jacobian stacked with the active bounds) fails whenever |A(y) ∪ A(u)| > n − m. With bounds on controls only, regularity always holds if Cy is nonsingular (p. 4).
  - In the reduced formulation (2.2), state bounds become nonlinear inequality constraints y(u) ≥ 0 (p. 3).
  - So the paper first secures optimality conditions without regularity: Proposition 2.1 gives a condition (2.8) under which the first- and second-order KKT constraint qualifications hold (pp. 4–5; Figure 2.1, p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Apply Newton's method, in an interior-point way, to three equivalent forms of the first-order conditions (Table 1.1, p. 2):
  - **Affine scaling** on D(x,λ)²∇ℓ = 0, C = 0 in (x, λ): Algorithm 4.1 (p. 11). It extends the Coleman–Li scaling D, and its derivative term E, from u to y (pp. 5–7).
  - **Primal-dual** on ∇ℓ − z = 0, C = 0, XZe = µe in (x, λ, z): Algorithm 5.1 (p. 14).
  - **Reduced primal-dual** on W^T(∇f − z) = 0, C = 0, XZe = µe in (x, z), with the adjoint multiplier λ(x,z) of (5.10): Algorithm 5.2 (pp. 15–16).

  Each step can be computed in the null space as s = s^n + W(x)s_u with W = [−Cy⁻¹Cu; I]. All three reduced systems have the form W^T H^a W s_u = …, where H^a augments the Hessian of the Lagrangian with a diagonal bound term, (4.10), (5.7), (5.15) (pp. 10, 13, 16). A fraction-to-the-boundary rule with parameter σ is used (pp. 11, 14, 16).

  Use of the correspondence: at stationary points D ↔ X^{1/2} and E ↔ Z (Remark 5.1, p. 13). This is used to add a centralization term to the affine-scaling algorithm (p. 14). It also explains the multiplier update (4.15) as an approximation of the reduced primal-dual adjoint update (Remark 5.2, p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumptions 2.1: f and C twice continuously differentiable with Lipschitz second derivatives, and Cy(x) nonsingular (p. 3). The author notes that the analysis needs second derivatives but the algorithms may use first derivatives only (p. 3). The rate theorems assume convergence to a nondegenerate regular point satisfying second-order sufficient conditions (pp. 12, 14, 16).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: local only.
  - **Well-posedness**: nonsingularity of the scaled KKT matrix (3.2) under regularity and positive definiteness on the null space (Proposition 3.3, pp. 7–8). The reduced matrix W^T H^a W is nonsingular by contradiction: a null vector would lift to a null vector of the full matrix (Proposition 4.1, p. 11). For Algorithm 5.2, a rank argument premultiplies the full primal-dual matrix by diag(W^T, I, I) (p. 17).
  - **Rates**: q-quadratic if |σ − 1| = O(‖F‖); for the primal-dual algorithms a second parameter must also be O(‖F‖) (its Greek letter is dropped in the extraction). The rate proofs are borrowed: from Coleman–Li [7] and Dennis–Heinkenschloss–Vicente [10] for Theorem 4.1 (p. 12), and from El-Bakry et al. [12] for Theorems 5.1–5.2 (pp. 15, 17). Multipliers computed from (x, λ) or (x, z) converge r-quadratically through a Lipschitz bound (pp. 12, 17).
  - **General NLP** (§6): full-space versions extend directly; the reduced versions need a QR-based null-space basis and least-squares multipliers (p. 18).
- **D5 Experiments**: none shown. The introduction reports implemented instances tested on problems from [17] with encouraging preliminary results, but gives no data (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contributions**: a constraint-qualification analysis, three local algorithms, a null-space reduction and a unified view (p. 18).
  - **Limits and future work, stated concretely**: globalization with trust regions is under study; the local rate in the singular case is open; for problems of the form (1.1), regularity and strict complementarity are likely to be absent (pp. 18–19).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 1, 4–7, 18–19): the affine-scaling Newton skeleton of [10] is kept and only the scaling object changes (D and E now cover the states). The price is stated: regularity and nondegeneracy are likely to fail. Local and derivative-based.
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (p. 2): numerics are asserted as encouraging with no data, collection or profiles.
- new-pattern candidate: *nest the known theories as special cases* (pp. 2, 18): the affine-scaling algorithm extends [10], the full-space primal-dual algorithm is the one of [12], and §6 recovers general NLP.
- new-pattern candidate: *correspondence between sibling algorithms to port a missing component* (pp. 13–14, 17). New.
- new-pattern candidate: *state where you lose* (pp. 4, 18–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive a family of algorithms by applying Newton's method to equivalent forms of one optimality system, and tabulate which variables each iterates on (Table 1.1, p. 2). Then use the variable correspondence at stationary points (D ↔ X^{1/2}, E ↔ Z) to move a feature (centralization) from one algorithm to another (pp. 13–14).
- When a standard constraint qualification fails for structural reasons in your class, prove a class-specific sufficient condition by building an explicit feasible arc x(t) = (y(u + tp_u), u + tp_u) from the implicit function (Proposition 2.1, pp. 4–5).
- Prove nonsingularity of a reduced (null-space) matrix by contradiction: lift its null vector to the full KKT matrix, which is known to be nonsingular (Proposition 4.1, p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "The preliminary numerical results are encouraging." (p. 2)
- "One of our claims in this paper is that looking at these three interior{point Newton algorithms in a comprehensive manner is a tool to gain insight about their individual components and properties." (p. 17)

### H004 · Derivative computations for a class of optimal control problems (School of Finite Elements and Applications, Centro Internacional de Matemática, Coimbra 1998, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: single author · **Position in trajectory**: CIM school series n. 6 (September 1998). Teaching material for his lecture on optimization methods for control and design at the CIM school (footnote, p. 1). The footnote says it is motivated by [3] (= S011) and [7] (= S048). **Read**: full (Figure 1 on p. 7 is dot-art; caption read).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: How to compute first and second derivatives, by sensitivity and adjoint equations, for (1) min f(y,u) s.t. c(y,u) = 0 and its reduced form (3) f̂(u) = f(y(u),u) (abstract, pp. 1–2). The two formulations are related but not necessarily equivalent, because the implicit function theorem selects one solution branch (p. 2). The motivation is practical (p. 2): the discretization inherits a scaling from the infinite-dimensional problem, handled through scalar products (e.g., an H¹ product via a stiffness matrix), and much of the information exists only as derivative-times-vector operations (e.g., finite elements without assembled matrices).
- **D2 Core idea**: localize every sensitivity or adjoint computation in applications of W(y,u) and its adjoint W* to vectors, W = [−c_y⁻¹c_u; I].
  - W d_u costs one generalized linearized state solve; W* d costs one generalized adjoint solve (p. 6).
  - On this basis: ∇f̂(u) equals ∇_uℓ evaluated at the adjoint multiplier, (8) (p. 4); ∇²f̂(u) equals the reduced Hessian Ĥ(y(u),u), (13), with its proof (pp. 4–5).
  - Approximations: a Gauss–Newton form when ∇_y f ≈ 0 (least-squares tracking), (14) (p. 5); second-order sensitivities that avoid multipliers (p. 5).
  - With sensitivities only, the Lagrangian term ⟨λ, c⟩ is obtained from one linearized state solve (10) (p. 4).
  - A reduced-Hessian approximation is embedded in the full space as H = [0 0; 0 Ĥ], (15) (p. 6).
  - §2.3 contrasts the oblique projector P_obl = WW* with the orthogonal projector P_ort = W(W*W)⁻¹W*, (16), and characterizes P_obl geometrically (Proposition 2.1, from the thesis [13]) (pp. 6–8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces Y, U, Λ with their own scalar products (p. 1); the implicit function theorem assumptions hold throughout (p. 3); f and c once or twice differentiable (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: implicit differentiation of ∇_yℓ(y(u),u,λ(u)) = 0 gives λ′(u) and hence (13); the proof is credited to [6] (pp. 4–5). The projector uniqueness is shown by a short orthogonality argument (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: consolidation for teaching. No limitations or future work are stated (pp. 1–8).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (p. 6): the reusable artefact here is an oracle abstraction (W and W* applications) that any algorithm can call, not a solver with profiles.
- new-pattern candidate: *minimal application oracle / matrix-free interface* (p. 6).
- new-pattern candidate: *research section recycled as teaching notes* (p. 1; compare pp. 3–6 with S048 pp. 5–10). New.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Express every derivative computation through two primitives, W v and W* d, each costing one linearized-state or adjoint solve. Algorithms then need only those primitives (p. 6).
- When only sensitivities are available (no adjoint solver), obtain the Lagrangian term ⟨λ, c⟩ from one linearized state solve via ⟨∇_y f, c_y⁻¹c⟩ (p. 4).
- Store a reduced-Hessian approximation as the full-space operator [0 0; 0 Ĥ], so that full-space code paths stay valid (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "It also localizes the use of the sensitivity equation method and the adjoint equation method in the derivative calculations." (p. 6)
- "Written as part of the teaching material used to support the lecture Optimization Methods for Control and Design Problems given by the author at the School of Finite Elements and Applications, CIM, Coimbra 1998." (p. 1)

### H002 · An analysis of Newton's method for equivalent Karush-Kuhn-Tucker systems (Investigación Operativa 1999, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: single author · **Position in trajectory**: published in Investigación Operativa 7 (1999) 17–28. Coimbra; dated January 25, 1999 (p. 1). It abstracts the reformulations used in S011 ([3]) and H003 ([9]). **Read**: full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Many methods apply Newton's method to the first-order conditions after eliminating variables or equations. Two equivalent systems give two Newton methods, so how close are their steps? (p. 2). The two running cases are:
  - equality constraints: the full system (1.1)–(1.2) vs the null-space form Z(x)^T∇f = 0, g = 0 with least-squares multipliers (Goodman [7]), (1.3)–(1.4) (p. 2);
  - simple bounds: primal-dual Newton (1.8) vs Coleman–Li affine scaling (1.9)–(1.10) (pp. 2–3).

  The stated goal is a model that answers the question for several reformulations at once (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: an abstract model.
  - The original system is F(x,y) = 0; the reformulation is G(x̄)H(x̄) = 0, with a defining condition GF = GH at a solution (2.3) and a relating condition U(x, x̄, y) = 0 (p. 4).
  - Premultiply the original Newton equation by G and subtract the reformulated one. This gives an identity for R(x̄)(∆x − ∆x̄) (p. 4).
  - The discrepancy splits into three terms (p. 5): derivative consistency ‖R − S‖; the y-derivative reduced by G, ‖G ∂F/∂y‖; and function-value consistency ‖GH − GF‖.
  - Theorem 2.1: if each term is at most κi‖U‖, then ‖∆x − ∆x̄‖ ≤ κ‖U‖ (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: the entries of F, G, H have Lipschitz first derivatives (p. 4); under the standard Newton assumptions near a solution the constants are uniform (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: the paper instantiates U three times.
  - Equality constraints: G ∂F/∂y = 0 and U = (x − x̄, y − y(x̄)), so ‖U‖ → 0 at stationary points and the steps coincide in the limit, (3.1) (p. 6).
  - Simple bounds: U contains terms (X − D(x̄)²)e, whose limits need not exist or vanish, so the steps need not coincide (p. 7).
  - Discretized optimal control with control bounds: the same conclusion (pp. 8–10).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an abstract step-comparison bound with three instances. The limit is stated concretely: in the interior-point examples the bound does not imply that the steps coincide (pp. 3, 7, 10). Future work: the same analysis for the primal-dual, affine-scaling and reduced primal-dual algorithms of [9] (= H003) for general NLP (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 4–10): Newton's method is kept and the system is reformulated; the price of the reformulation is measured by ‖U‖, and the paper says where it does not vanish. Local, derivative-based.
- new-pattern candidate: *abstract assumptions, then verify per instance* (pp. 5–10): Theorem 2.1 has abstract hypotheses that §§3–5 verify case by case.
- new-pattern candidate: *nest the known theories as special cases* (pp. 1–2).
- new-pattern candidate: *state where you lose* (pp. 3, 7, 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To compare two algorithms derived from equivalent formulations, premultiply one Newton equation by the reformulation operator G and subtract the other. The identity splits the step discrepancy into function-value consistency, derivative consistency and a cross-derivative term (pp. 4–5).
- State the comparison theorem with abstract hypotheses (each term ≤ κi‖U‖), verify them per instance, and report the instances where ‖U‖ → 0 fails (pp. 5–10).
- After the key inequality, give one paragraph interpreting each term of the bound (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "The goal of this work is not to provide the answer only for this particular reformulation." (p. 2)

### S048 · An interface between optimization and application for the numerical solution of optimal control problems (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 1999, DOI 10.1145/317275.317278)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Heinkenschloss (Rice) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the TRICE software period. It was circulated as Coimbra report 98-08 (H004 ref. [7], p. 8). The interface was used to implement the affine-scaling interior-point algorithms of Dennis–Heinkenschloss–Vicente 1998 (S011) in Fortran 77 and Matlab (p. 24). **Read**: full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Discretized optimal control problems (1)/(2) could be given to general NLP solvers (LANCELOT, LOQO, SNOPT), but that approach has severe limitations (p. 2):
  - memory runs out under refinement if Jacobians are stored (p. 2);
  - the sparse formats are hard to produce for PDEs discretized with finite-element packages, and application solvers such as multigrid cannot be plugged in (p. 2);
  - treating the problem as plain finite-dimensional NLP ignores the infinite-dimensional structure, so improper scaling and artificial ill-conditioning make convergence deteriorate under refinement (pp. 2–3).

  In addition, new optimization methods reach optimal control late or not at all, and the gradient method is still common (p. 3). Claim: an interface can narrow, and often close, this gap (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & design**:
  - **Structure**: Hilbert spaces Y, U, Λ with user-supplied scalar products, for scaling and mesh independence (p. 3; §2, pp. 4–5); the state/control split; partial-Jacobian solves left to the application; inexact solves and evaluations allowed; Jacobians and Hessians only as operator applications (matrix-free) (p. 3).
  - **Routines**: Table 1 lists the user routines (p. 10). Common to both approaches: fval, cval, lcval, state, linstate, yprod, uprod, lprod, hs_exact, xnew. Adjoint approach: adjoint, adjval, grad. Sensitivity approach: sens, sensa, fgrad.
  - **Calling conventions** (pp. 10–13): a return flag (0 = done; > 0 = failed; < 0 = done but not ideal, e.g., near singularity); a `tol` input on every routine except xnew, to control inexactness; `user_parms`, which the optimizer never reads; and a `job` argument for cheap special cases (c = 0 or s_u = 0).
  - **Iterate lifecycle**: xnew tells the application when the iterate changes, with the states `init`, `current_it`, `react_it`, `trial_it`, `new_it` and `temp`. The states follow the trust-region trial/accept/reject cycle, and they let the application cache per-iterate work (stiffness matrices, grids), log, or checkpoint (pp. 13–14).
  - **Language**: the description is language-neutral, with Matlab syntax used only for illustration (pp. 4, 10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and c at least once differentiable, with second derivatives in parts; the implicit function theorem for the reduced formulation (pp. 3, 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no convergence theory.
  - **Consistency tests**: the adjoint identities must hold to O(tol) when solvers are inexact, and finite-difference checks (24)–(27) use only interface routines (p. 17).
  - **Appendix A**: gradients under weighted inner products are ∇f = T⁻¹∇̃f; adjoints are c_y* = T_y⁻¹c_y^T T_λ; the BFGS update changes under a weighted product; a weighted CG equals preconditioning (pp. 25–27).
- **D5 Experiments**: none in this paper.
  - Code fragments: steepest descent with Armijo on the reduced problem (pp. 18–19), reduced SQP without globalization (pp. 19–20), an augmented-Lagrangian merit value (p. 20), and the L-BFGS two-loop recursion with split inner products (pp. 21–22).
  - A duct-flow problem with a shock (from Cliff et al.) shows how linstate, the scalar products and adjoint are realized (pp. 22–24). The computations in that earlier work showed that the choice of scalar product noticeably affects performance and solution quality (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: a software-design framework. Its stated theoretical role is to force algorithm designers to separate optimization from application tasks (p. 25).
  - **Limitations (§6)**: the interface is not sufficient for all algorithms (p. 24). Pointwise bounds live naturally in L∞, so the Hilbert space U = L² is questionable, although numerics were good and are supported by Ulbrich et al. (p. 24). Vectors held in core are a problem for time-dependent PDEs or seismic inversion (pp. 24–25).
  - **Deferred**: how to control inexactness and what to do when a routine fails are declared beyond the paper's scope (pp. 17–18).
  - **Extension**: general inequality constraints g ≤ 0 enter (1) through slack variables (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 1–4, 24): the artefact itself is published as the paper, and implementations in Fortran 77 and Matlab solved a variety of problems.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 17–24): no problem collection, baseline or profiles; the examples are code fragments and one worked application.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (p. 11): the paper states the rationale that inexact but cheaper information, properly controlled, can make algorithms more efficient, and supplies the `tol` hook for it. The control is deterministic.
- new-pattern candidates:
  - *minimal application oracle / matrix-free interface* (pp. 3, 10–16);
  - *design around the user's existing oracle* (p. 3);
  - *infrastructure as a stand-alone citable publication* (pp. 1–4);
  - *disclosed theory–implementation gap* (pp. 17–18: the fragments ignore the flags and tolerance control);
  - *state where you lose* (pp. 24–25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give every user routine a three-state status flag (0 success, > 0 fatal, < 0 performed but suspicious) and a tolerance input, so the optimizer can report where a problem arose and can later control inexactness (pp. 10–11).
- Add an iterate-lifecycle callback (init / trial / accept / reactivate / temporary) so the application can cache per-iterate work, log iterates, or checkpoint and restart (pp. 13–14).
- Ship consistency tests with the interface: adjoint identities that must hold to O(tol), and finite-difference derivative checks built only from interface routines (p. 17).
- Put problem scaling into user-supplied inner products rather than variable transformations, and rederive gradients, adjoints and BFGS under the weighted products (pp. 4–5, 25–27).
- Add a `job` argument to user routines so the application can exploit special cases the optimizer knows about (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "We believe that the gap between optimization methods and their application to optimal control problems can be narrowed and in many cases even be closed by the provision of an interface between optimization algorithms and optimal control applications." (p. 3)
- "By using this interface or some of its features, optimization algorithm designers are forced to separate optimization and application tasks within the algorithms." (p. 25)

### S099 · Characterization of the smoothness and curvature of a marginal function for a trust-region problem (Mathematical Programming 1999, DOI 10.1007/s10107980027a)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: D. A. Andrews (Department of Statistics, Rice) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: Rice PhD period (TR96-13, May 1996, revised July 1997, p. 1; NATO/Fulbright support, p. 2). It extends the thesis's use of the Gay–Sorensen characterization (S040, card b01) to a value function. **Read**: full; figures as captions.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - **Application**: two-group Gaussian discriminant analysis needs a root of an equation involving v(∆) = min{q(s) : ‖s‖ ≤ ∆}, to be found by Newton's method, so v′(∆) is needed (p. 3; §5, pp. 13–14).
  - **Wider question**: a complete characterization of the smoothness and curvature of v, going beyond what the application requires (p. 3).
  - **Gap in the general theory**: NLP sensitivity theory (Gauvin, Rockafellar, Janin) gives Lipschitz continuity, one-sided derivatives, and v′ equal to the multiplier (up to sign) when the solution and multiplier are unique (Theorems 2.1–2.2, pp. 3–4). The second-order results of Seeger and Shapiro are omitted as too general and not covering all cases (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: explicit calculation through the Gay–Sorensen optimality characterization (Proposition 3.2, p. 4) and the eigendecomposition of H. There are three cases (pp. 5–8):
  - (I) H positive definite, with a threshold ∆* = ‖H⁻¹g‖;
  - (II) H not positive definite, easy case;
  - (III) the hard case (g orthogonal to the eigenspace of the smallest eigenvalue), with a threshold ∆#.

  All formulae are collected in Table 1 (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Euclidean norm; H symmetric; any g (p. 2). Nothing else.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **Key device**: implicit differentiation of the secular equation g^T(H + λI)⁻²g = ∆², (3.7), gives λ′(∆), (3.8). From it, v′ equals the multiplier with a negative sign (the minus sign is lost in the extraction; the v′ axes of Figures 1, 3 and 4 run over negative values, pp. 7–10), and v″ is given by (3.9) (pp. 5–6).
  - **Limits at zero**: the asymptotics of h(λ) as ∆ → 0⁺ give v″₊(0) = g^T Hg / g^T g, (3.10) (pp. 9–10).
  - **Breakpoints**: one-sided limits (3.11) show v is not twice differentiable at ∆* and ∆#, but has a Schwartz second derivative there (pp. 11–12).
  - **Curvature**: v″ is monotone decreasing, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to g^T(H + λI)⁻³g (p. 12).
  - **Theorems**: C^∞ except at ∆*, ∆# (Theorem 3.1, p. 8); C¹ on [0, ∞) (Theorem 3.2, p. 11); not C² at ∆*, ∆# (Theorem 3.3, p. 12). Theorem 4.1 (p. 13): v is convex if H is positive definite; otherwise it is concave, or convex and then concave, depending on the sign of v″₊(0) and on λ1.
- **D5 Experiments**: v, v′, v″ are plotted for 3-D diagonal examples of each case, using Matlab with MINPACK-2 dgqt.f through a MEX interface (pp. 6–10). The application is implemented in S-PLUS: Newton's method from an interpolated starting point (5.7) needs 7, 10 and 7 iterations on the UCI datasets iris, wdbc and wine (dimensions 4, 10, 13) to reach 10⁻¹⁴ accuracy (Table 2, p. 15). An iris projection is used for visualization (Figure 5, p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: theory (a complete characterization) plus one statistics application. Stated extensions: an ellipsoidal norm ‖Ws‖ ≤ ∆ by change of variables, and Moré's quadratic constraint c(s) ≤ ∆, which could be treated the same way (p. 15). No limitations are discussed.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (p. 15): the validation is one application on three real datasets with iteration counts; there is no baseline and no collection.
- new-pattern candidate: *reverse field import* (§5, pp. 13–15): a trust-region object is exported, with a statistician coauthor, into discriminant analysis.
- new-pattern candidate: *name where the proof technology breaks* (pp. 3–4): general sensitivity theory is applied first, then declared insufficient for all scenarios.
- new-pattern candidate: *exploit the problem's own geometry* (pp. 5–8): the eigenstructure and the easy/hard dichotomy of the trust-region subproblem organize the whole proof.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To differentiate a value function whose multiplier is defined by a scalar secular equation, differentiate that equation implicitly to get λ′, then substitute into the KKT expression of v (pp. 5–6).
- Collect an exhaustive case analysis into one table of formulae per case and per interval of the parameter (Table 1, p. 11).
- Build a Newton starting point by interpolating linearly between two parameter values where the function is known in closed form (5.7) (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "But the mathematical questions we answer in this paper go far beyond this point." (p. 3)
- "These results have some implications for problem (1.1), but we will omit them since they are too general and they do not cover all possible scenarios in (1.1)." (p. 4)

### S055 · Bilevel programming: Introduction, history and overview (Encyclopedia of Optimization 2001, DOI 10.1007/978-0-387-74759-0_45)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: single author · **Position in trajectory**: Coimbra; the file is the manuscript dated December 1, 1997 (p. 1). It condenses his early bilevel work: the Calamai–Vicente problem generator [5], the Vicente–Savard–Júdice discrete linear BP [20], and the Vicente–Calamai bibliography review [21] (= S002, card c01) (p. 3). **Read**: full. The extraction drops the minus signs in the worked example.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry defines bilevel programming (BP) as a hierarchical problem in which some variables must solve an optimization problem parameterized by the others, and lists applications in transportation, management, planning and optimal design (p. 1). Generalizations mentioned: integer BP [20], and a variational-inequality lower level [14] (p. 1). It then defines Ω(x), M(x) and the induced region (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea (structure of the overview)**:
  - **Worked example**: one two-variable example has a nonconvex but connected induced region; one added upper-level constraint makes it disconnected; there are two local minimizers and one global minimizer (pp. 1–2). Compactness of the induced region matters for existence [8] (p. 2).
  - **History**: Bracken–McGill 1973; Candler–Norton coined the term; Stackelberg games as motivation; attention from the early 1980s (p. 2).
  - **Theory** (p. 2):
    - optimality conditions via reformulations, nondifferentiable analysis or geometry;
    - linear BP is strongly NP-hard [9];
    - linear BP problems with exponentially many local minima can be constructed [5];
    - minimax, linear, integer, bilinear and quadratic programs are special cases;
    - multiobjective optimization and static Stackelberg problems are related but different.
  - **Algorithms** (pp. 2–3):
    - extreme-point methods for linear BP, which find a global minimizer;
    - branch-and-bound and complementarity-pivot methods, which exploit the lower-level complementarity conditions (linear or convex quadratic lower level) and also find a global minimizer;
    - descent-direction and penalty methods for nonlinear BP, which are expected to find a local minimizer.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: for the branch-and-bound and complementarity classes, the lower level is convex in y with a constraint qualification, so that the necessary conditions are sufficient (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis**: none (worked example only).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an encyclopedia entry with no new results. It points to the Shimizu–Ishizuka–Bard book, surveys, and the author's own bibliography review (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- No Method 1–5 link. The entry is background for the later bilevel DFO work (S047, card s2-03; S070, card c2-05).
- new-pattern candidate: *survey as a problem-feature routing table* (pp. 2–3): algorithm classes are keyed to the problem class and to the kind of guarantee (global or local).
- new-pattern candidate: *controlled-difficulty test-problem generators* (p. 2): the author's own generator result is cited as a complexity fact.
- new-pattern candidate: *one toy example exhibiting every pathology* (pp. 1–2). New.

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a survey or tutorial, use one two-variable example plus a one-constraint modification to exhibit every structural pathology of the problem class (nonconvex, then disconnected feasible region, several local minimizers) (pp. 1–2).
- Organize an algorithm overview by (problem class, guarantee type: global or local minimizer) rather than chronologically (pp. 2–3).

### Batch synthesis (s2-01)

1. **The acceptance condition already connects a foreign step to a theorem in 1996** (Method 1 precursor). S081 lets the trust-region skeleton take the quasi-Newton step and asks only whether it passes fraction of Cauchy decrease (pp. 4–6). Unlike the later DFO wrappers, the foreign step pays an extra assumption (bounded condition number).
2. **The artefact habit predates DFO, but profiles do not** (Method 5).
   - TRICE was released with code, a user's guide and a GUI (H007 p. 3).
   - The interface is itself a TOMS paper (S048), and its derivative framework was reused as lecture notes (H004 p. 1).
   - Yet no paper in the batch has a problem collection, a baseline or profiles. H003 only asserts that preliminary numerics are encouraging (p. 2); S099 reports iteration counts on three datasets (p. 15).
3. **Inexactness is an explicit, deterministic parameter.** Examples: the `tol` input on every interface routine, with the stated rationale that cheaper, controlled information may give more efficient algorithms (S048 pp. 10–11); tolerances computed each iteration from known quantities (H007 pp. 2–3). These are deterministic precursors of Method 3.
4. **Unify sibling methods through one abstract model.** H002 uses an abstract reformulation model whose hypotheses are verified per instance (pp. 4–10). H003 derives three algorithms from equivalent optimality systems, with a correspondence that ports centralization between them (Table 1.1, p. 2; pp. 13–14). This adds evidence to *nest the known theories as special cases* and *abstract assumptions, then verify per instance*.
5. **Losses are stated concretely**: regularity fails structurally and the singular case is open (H003 pp. 4, 18–19); the Newton steps need not coincide (H002 pp. 7, 10); the interface has limits for L∞ bounds and out-of-core data (S048 pp. 24–25).
6. **No evaluation counts anywhere** (Method 2). All results are asymptotic (S081), local-rate (H003, H002) or structural (S099), consistent with the era effect noted under Method 2.
