The batch has two papers: the bilevel stochastic gradient (BSG) framework for constrained and unconstrained lower levels (J. Glob. Optim. 2025), and its trilevel extension (arXiv 2025). Four questions:
- (a) Is Method 4 (*keep the skeleton, change one object*) still the design move when the skeleton is stochastic gradient descent rather than direct search?

### S042 · Inexact bilevel stochastic gradient methods for constrained and unconstrained lower-level problems (Journal of Global Optimization 2025, DOI 10.1007/s10898-025-01502-8)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. Giovannelli, G. D. Kent (both Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period.
- Timeline: arXiv v1 dates from 2021 (id 2110.00604); the file is v3, dated 8 Nov 2023 (p. 1); publication is 2025. Funded by AFOSR award FA9550-23-1-0217 (p. 37).
- It is the base paper of the group's bilevel line. S070 (c2-02) and S091 (this batch) build on it. S091 cites it as Lehigh ISE Technical Report 21T-025, Dec 2022 (S091 ref. [18], p. 10).
- Self-citations: 2 of 66 references. One is the 1994 bibliography with Calamai ([63], S002 in c01), cited as a survey (p. 1). The other is Liu & Vicente's stochastic multi-gradient paper ([36]), cited for a mini-batch bound (p. 21). Vicente's own 1990s bilevel algorithm papers are not cited (reference list, pp. 37–41).

**Read**: full text, pp. 1–37. Appendices A–D (pp. 42–45) were skimmed at the level of proof structure. The reference list was checked for self-citations.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry combines an application pull, an uncovered constraint class and a cost of second-order information.
  - **Applications**: continual learning, neural architecture search (NAS), adversarial learning and hyperparameter tuning (p. 3). f_u and f_ℓ are expectations over random variables with i.i.d. samples (p. 3).
  - **Uncovered class**: BSG methods have mainly treated the lower-level (LL) unconstrained case (p. 4). The few constrained approaches handle only linear constraints or constraints that depend only on y [31, 33, 57, 62, 64] (p. 4).
  - **Cost**: the truncated Neumann series needs ‖∇²_yy f_ℓ‖ < 1, or knowledge of the Lipschitz constant C0, "which is typically unknown in practice" (pp. 3–4). Second-order derivatives prevent large-scale use (p. 22).
  - **Problem form**: the bilevel problem (BLP) has upper-level (UL) constraints x ∈ X only, with X closed and convex. The authors say UL constraints involving y could make BLP "intractable due to a disconnected feasible region" (p. 2). The LL solution is assumed unique, so BLP reduces to min f(x) = f_u(x, y(x)) over X (1.1) (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: In a crowded ML-optimization area, pick the cell by a constraint class nobody covers: nonlinear LL constraints that depend on x. Then show an application where that constraint arises naturally. Here it is the GEM-style *do not forget old tasks* constraint in continual learning (p. 33).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: BSG is the SG method applied to the reduced problem (1.1). The stochastic gradient is a sampled adjoint gradient (hypergradient) evaluated at an approximate LL solution ỹ_k (p. 4).
  - **Constrained LL**: the LL KKT system (2.5) is written as G(x, v(x)) = 0 with v = (y, z_I, z_E). The Hessian ∇²_yy f_ℓ in the adjoint formula is replaced by the KKT Jacobian ∇_vG, which gives ∇y = −∇_xG ∇_vG⁻¹ L (2.7) and the adjoint gradient (2.8) (pp. 7–8).
  - **Unified notation**: d(D) = −(a − AB⁻¹b) with D = (a, b, A, B) (2.12). One formula covers the unconstrained and constrained cases and the deterministic and stochastic ones (p. 8).
  - **Algorithm 1 (p. 9)** has three steps:
    - an approximate LL solve, by "any arbitrary optimization method";
    - a stochastic direction;
    - a projected step.

    There is no stopping criterion, "as is usual in the literature related to SG methods" (p. 9).
  - **Inexact B⁻¹**: either an adjoint solve with residual r̃ (2.14), or a truncated Neumann series with remainder R̃ (2.15) (pp. 9–10).
  - **What is kept**: the SG skeleton and its decaying-step theory from the Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal survey [5] (pp. 16–18).
  - **Practical variants (§5.1, pp. 22–25)**:
    - **BSG-N-FD**: solves the adjoint equation with CG (unconstrained) or GMRES (constrained). Each Hessian-vector product is a central finite difference, (5.2) and (5.4), with ε = 0.1 (p. 23).
    - **BSG-1**: uses Gauss–Newton-style rank-1 outer products for the second derivatives, (5.6)–(5.7). In the unconstrained case this gives the closed form ∇f ≃ ∇_xf_u − (∇_yf_ℓ^⊤∇_yf_u / ∇_yf_ℓ^⊤∇_yf_ℓ) ∇_xf_ℓ (5.8), with no matrix-vector products (p. 24).
    - **Constrained LL in practice**: the exact ℓ1 penalty (5.1) is minimized by stochastic subgradient. The multipliers come from CG on the KKT system (p. 22).
    - **LL accuracy in practice** ("inc. acc."): one more LL step is added whenever the UL objective changes by less than a threshold, and each LL solve is warm-started from the previous one (p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: Write the direction as a function of a data tuple, d(D) = −(a − AB⁻¹b). The constrained case then changes only B (Hessian → KKT Jacobian), and the stochastic case changes only how D is sampled. One proof covers all four cases (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: Ship two practical variants side by side, one covered by the theory (BSG-N-FD) and one cheaper heuristic (BSG-1), and say in the contributions paragraph which one is which (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Base (p. 2)**: f_u is C¹ and f_ℓ is C². The LL solution y(x) exists and is unique. X is closed and convex.
  - **LL unconstrained (pp. 10–11)**:
    - Assumption 3.1: the gradients and Hessians are Lipschitz, and they *and their stochastic estimates* are bounded at all points.
    - Assumption 3.2: ∇²_yy f_ℓ is positive definite everywhere, and so is its stochastic estimate.
    - Assumption 3.3: uniformly bounded inverse Hessians, which the paper calls "equivalent to saying that fℓ(x, ·) is strongly convex".
  - **LL constrained (p. 11)**:
    - Assumption 3.4: smoothness, bounded Jacobians and Hessians, and bounded multipliers.
    - Assumption 3.5: LICQ, strict complementarity and SOSC. This makes y(x) a strict local minimizer; global uniqueness needs extra convexity-type conditions, which are listed.
    - Assumption 3.6: the inverse KKT matrices are uniformly bounded.
  - **Direction and error budgets (pp. 12–14)**:
    - Assumption 3.7: bounded second moment of the exact-LL direction.
    - Assumption 3.8: sensitivity. The direction map is Lipschitz in its data and residual (3.2), and the data are Lipschitz in the LL error (3.3).
    - Assumption 3.9: E‖w(x_k) − w̃_k‖² ≤ (C_w α_k)².
    - Assumption 3.10: sampling error E‖D − D(ξ)‖ ≤ C_D α_k.
    - Assumption 3.11: residual ‖r_k‖ ≤ C_e α_k.
  - **Rates (pp. 18–20)**: f bounded below and Robbins–Monro steps (Assumptions 4.1–4.2); bounded iterates and strong convexity (4.3–4.4); convexity (4.5).
  - **Admitted as strong**:
    - Strong convexity of f amounts "in practice" to an LL that is a QP: "Still, we cover this case for completeness" (p. 19).
    - The same "lack of practicality" applies to the convex case (p. 20).
    - The Neumann condition ‖∇²_yy f_ℓ‖ < 1 is "a strong assumption" (p. 4).
  - **Claimed mild**: the sensitivity assumption "is not restrictive" (p. 12). It holds under a finite-sum structure with Lipschitz terms, where the randomness is drawing a batch (p. 13).
  - **Reader's note**: Assumption 3.1 asks the *stochastic* estimates to be bounded at all points (p. 10). But §4.5 assumes normally distributed estimates (p. 21), and the experiments add Gaussian noise (p. 26); Gaussian estimates are unbounded. Also, Assumption 3.3 is printed with (H^ℓ_xy)⁻¹ where (H^ℓ_yy)⁻¹ is presumably meant (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: State each assumption with a one-sentence purpose. Where an assumption is strong, say so on the spot and name the practical case it forces (LL = QP) (pp. 10–13, 19–20).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Proof skeleton**:
    1. **Sensitivity (Prop. 3.1, p. 13; App. A, pp. 42–43)**: the direction is Lipschitz in its data for both inexact schemes. The proof uses three facts: sums are Lipschitz; products are Lipschitz when the factors are bounded; inversion is Lipschitz when the singular values stay away from zero (p. 13). Parts of the proof "have been published elsewhere [13]" (Couellan & Wang) (p. 13).
    2. **Smoothness of f (Prop. 3.2, p. 16; App. B, pp. 43–44)**: bounded ∇y makes y(x) and w(x) Lipschitz, and then ∇f is Lipschitz.
    3. **Error budget (pp. 14–15)**:
       - Lemma 3.1 bounds the second moment of the inexact direction by G_d = 2(U_d + V_d) (p. 14).
       - Lemma 3.2 bounds the bias: E‖−∇f(x_k) − d(x_k, w̃_k, ξ_k)‖ ≤ C_d α_k. The proof adds and subtracts the deterministic direction at the approximate LL point d(x_k, w̃_k). This splits the error into an LL-inexactness part and a sampling part, and each part is O(α_k) by Assumptions 3.9–3.11 (p. 15).
    4. **Descent (Lemma 4.2, p. 17)**: E f(x_{k+1}) − f(x_k) ≤ −α_k‖P_X∇f‖² + α_k²C_∇fC_d + ½α_k²L_∇fG_d. The bias enters at order α_k², the same order as the variance term.
    5. **Rates**:
       - Theorem 4.3 (nonconvex): Σα_k E‖P_X∇f(x_k)‖² < ∞, so the α-weighted average goes to 0. "The proof follows [5, Theorem 4.10] closely" (p. 18).
       - Theorem 4.4 (strongly convex): with α_k = γ/k and γ ≥ 1/(2c), both E‖x_k − x*‖² and E f(x_k) − f* are O(1/k) (p. 19; App. C, p. 44).
       - Theorem 4.5 (convex): with α_k = ᾱ/√k, min_s E f(x_s) − f* = O(1/√k) (p. 20; App. D, p. 45).
    6. **Enforcing the budgets**:
       - Assumption 3.9: take i_k = k² inner SG steps with β_i = γ/i on a strongly convex LL, using Nemirovski et al. [43, (2.9)] (p. 20).
       - Assumption 3.10: grow the mini-batch sizes n_a, n_b, n_A, n_B as α_k shrinks, assuming Gaussian estimates (4.10) (p. 21).
       - Assumption 3.11: tighten the adjoint-solve tolerance or raise the Neumann order q (p. 13).
  - **Form of the complexity**: rates are per UL iteration, with constants. The paper gives no total count of LL steps or samples. **Reader's note**: with i_k = k², reaching UL iteration K costs Σ k² = O(K³) LL steps, and the batch sizes grow too. The paper does not state or discuss this.
  - **Reader's note**: the proof of Lemma 4.1 writes P_X(x_k + α_k d) − x_k as α_k P_X d, and uses P_X = P_X^⊤ = P_X² and ‖P_X‖ ≤ 1 (p. 17). These are properties of a linear orthogonal projector, which requires X to be a subspace. X is only assumed closed and convex (p. 2). The experiments all have X = ℝⁿ (p. 22), so this is never exercised.
  - Transferable technique: Give every error source (inner-solve error, sampling error, linear-algebra residual) a budget of O(α_k). The direction bias is then O(α_k), it enters the descent lemma at O(α_k²), and it is summable under Σα_k² < ∞. The textbook SG proof then goes through unchanged (pp. 13–18).
  - Transferable technique: For each budget, add a short subsection showing how an algorithm enforces it: inner iteration count i_k = k², a growing batch size, a solver tolerance (pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable technique: Prove a Lipschitz-in-data property of a matrix formula once, from the three facts about sums, bounded products and well-conditioned inverses. It then holds for every approximation scheme of the inverse (p. 13; pp. 42–43).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Setup and code**: Python on a desktop machine, averaged over 10 seeds (p. 22). Code on GitHub (BSG_Methods_Con_Unc) (p. 22).
  - **Test problems**:
    - a synthetic quadratic bilevel problem (5.11) with n = m = 300 (p. 26), run with three LL regimes:
      - unconstrained (p. 26);
      - 50 random linear inequalities in y (5.12) (p. 28);
      - 5 random quadratic constraints coupling x and y (5.13) (p. 30);
    - continual learning on CIFAR-10 with 5 incremental tasks and a CNN (19,392 hidden and 163,840 output weights), with and without the no-forgetting constraints (5.15) (pp. 32–33).
  - **Baselines**:
    - DARTS and StocBiO in the unconstrained case (p. 26);
    - SIGD for linear constraints in y, the only class SIGD handles (p. 28);
    - none for quadratic constraints: "To the best of our knowledge, there do not exist any other bilevel stochastic algorithms that can handle general nonlinear constraints in the LL problem" (p. 30);
    - BSG-H (true stochastic Hessians) as a reference only (p. 22).
  - **Noise**:
    - Gaussian noise with std 5 on gradients and 0.05 on Hessians (p. 26);
    - linear case: Hessian std in {0.005, 0.05}, 0.5 on everything else (p. 28);
    - quadratic case: Hessian std in {0.005, 0.5} (p. 30).
  - **Metrics**: UL iterations and time. The synthetic plots show the true f(x_k) = f_u(x_k, y(x_k)) with the optimal value marked (pp. 26–28). Continual learning plots show the approximation f_u, "as it is typically done in bilevel ML" (p. 36).
  - **Tuning**: fixed UL and LL step sizes chosen by a grid over powers of 10. Each algorithm gets its own grid bounds of two or three values, because "Using a single arbitrary grid to test all of the algorithms would have led to stepsizes biased toward one set of algorithms over the others" (p. 26). Continual learning uses a decaying UL step (constant/k) and a fixed LL step (p. 36). StocBiO's C0 and q are set to the values that "led to the best results" (p. 27; p. 36).
  - **Results (synthetic)**:
    - Unconstrained, deterministic: BSG-N-FD and BSG-H are best; StocBiO is slightly worse; BSG-1 is worse because the problem lacks Gauss–Newton structure; DARTS is worst (pp. 27–28).
    - Unconstrained, stochastic: BSG-N-FD is best, closely followed by StocBiO. BSG-H degrades because stochastic Hessians need more samples than gradients [5, §6.1.1] (p. 28).
    - Linear constraints: BSG-N-FD and BSG-H beat SIGD. At high Hessian noise, SIGD and BSG-H stop converging and BSG-N-FD is best (pp. 28–30).
    - Quadratic constraints: BSG-H cannot be tuned to converge at high noise (p. 32).
  - **Results (continual learning)**:
    - Unconstrained: BSG-N-FD, BSG-1 and StocBiO are similar. DARTS is best on the first two tasks (p. 36).
    - Constrained: BSG-N-FD is better in iterations, and BSG-1 is slightly better in time (p. 36).
  - **Failures reported**:
    - no step size made BSG-1 and BSG-H converge in the high-noise linear case (p. 28);
    - the same for BSG-H in the quadratic case (p. 32);
    - BSG-H is not run on continual learning because of its cost (p. 36).
  - **Not tested**: no instance has UL constraints, so the projection is never exercised (p. 22). There is no problem collection and there are no profiles.
  - Transferable technique: Give each algorithm its own step-size grid window, and write down why a shared grid would bias the comparison (p. 26).
  - Transferable technique: When a method cannot be tuned to converge at some noise level, say so in the text rather than dropping its curve silently (p. 28, p. 32).
  - Transferable technique: On synthetic bilevel tests, choose an LL solvable in closed form, so you can plot the true f(x_k) = f_u(x_k, y(x_k)) instead of the surrogate f_u(x_k, ỹ_k) (p. 26).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Taxonomy**: implicit vs iterative differentiation. The paper focuses on implicit differentiation, citing [29] for the finding that automatic differentiation can be less efficient (p. 4).
  - **Critique by restrictive assumption**: prior methods "either focus on a specific problem structure or require the LL problem to be solved to optimality at each iteration or rely on a truncated Neumann series" (p. 4).
  - **Named critiques**:
    - the DARTS direction "may not be a descent direction" even with full batches (p. 4);
    - Savard & Gauvin [55] need an auxiliary linear–quadratic bilevel problem, and their direction "does not yield the exact size of the gradient" (p. 8).
  - **Unification claim**: the analysis unifies the adjoint-equation class and the Neumann class, which were "studied separately [24, 46]" (p. 5). It "comprehensively generalizes all existing approaches in the literature" (p. 10).
  - **Templates**:
    - the Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal SG theory [5] (pp. 16, 18);
    - Fiacco and McCormick sensitivity theory [18–20, 42] for the KKT route (p. 7);
    - Couellan & Wang [13] for parts of Props. 3.1–3.2 (pp. 13, 16).
  - Transferable technique: Criticize prior work through the assumption it needs (solve to optimality; ‖H‖ < 1; linear constraints only), and then show a single analysis in which each of those is one case (pp. 4–5).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Abstract order**: applications, then the difficulty (high dimension, constraints), then "we introduce", then a "comprehensive convergence theory", then the practical methods (p. 1).
  - **Contributions (§1.3)**: "The first main contribution …", "The second main contribution …", "Moreover …". Each carries a first or unify claim (p. 5).
  - **Priority claims**: p. 5, p. 30 ("To the best of our knowledge"), p. 36 ("first of their kind").
  - **Hedging**: sparse, mostly "seem to" in the results (p. 28, p. 36).
  - **Notation**: a defended notational choice: overlapping notation for d(·) "because we believe it is more powerful and effective than using a different letter" (p. 6).
  - **Proof placement**: rates are stated with explicit constants in the body, and the longer proofs (Props. 3.1–3.2, Thms. 4.4–4.5) are in the appendices (pp. 42–45).
  - **Admitted weaknesses**: stated where they arise (p. 19, p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution levels**:
    - algorithmic framework: the first for nonlinear, possibly nonconvex LL constraints (p. 5);
    - theory covering all kinds of inexactness in three convexity regimes (pp. 16–20);
    - two Hessian-free practical methods (pp. 22–25);
    - released code (p. 22).
  - **Limits written**:
    - the strongly convex and convex regimes are impractical (pp. 19–20);
    - BSG-1 has no theory (p. 5);
    - on continual learning, BSG-N-FD "is slightly outperformed in terms of time" (p. 37).
  - **Limits not written**:
    - there are no UL-constrained experiments;
    - there is no total-work complexity;
    - the experiments use fixed or c/k steps with capped inc. acc. (at most 30 LL iterations), while the theory uses Robbins–Monro steps and i_k = k² (pp. 18, 20, 26, 36);
    - the fixed FD parameter ε = 0.1 (p. 23) gives an error that does not shrink with α_k, while Assumption 3.11 needs one that does (p. 14). The conclusion nonetheless calls BSG-N-FD "consistent with the theory" (p. 37).
  - **Future work**:
    - NAS formulations with LL constraints, for example against overfitting [48] or instance-specific constraints [54];
    - variance reduction [8, 66] (p. 37).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (outside DFO). The SG skeleton and its proof [5] are kept. The object that changes is the direction: an adjoint gradient built from a data tuple D. LL constraints change only one block of D (B becomes the KKT Jacobian) (pp. 7–8). The nonconvex theorem is the SG theorem with a bias term (p. 18).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. The yardstick is the single-level SG method: the same three regimes, with 1/k and 1/√k rates (pp. 18–20). But the unit is UL iterations. The inner work (i_k = k² LL steps, growing batches) is never added up into an oracle count (pp. 20–21).
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction with its *different from standard practice* clause. Here Vicente works inside the stochastic-approximation tradition: unbiased (Gaussian) estimates, Robbins–Monro steps and bounds in expectation (pp. 18, 21). No requirement is relaxed to *holds with probability p*. What survives of Method 3 is the per-component accuracy requirement, but each requirement is a deterministic-in-expectation O(α_k) budget (pp. 13–14).
- Method 1 ✗ contradiction (disclosed). BSG-1 is released as a heuristic "not grounded on theoretical principles" and is not wrapped in any safeguard (p. 5). This is Taste warning sign 1. It is openly compared with DARTS, which has the same status.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant:
  - present: GitHub code (p. 22); the strongest class-specific baselines (StocBiO, DARTS, SIGD) (pp. 26, 28); the theory-covered variant tested against the heuristic variant;
  - absent: a problem collection and profiles; the only non-synthetic instance is one continual-learning setting (pp. 32–36).
- New-pattern candidate: *abstract assumptions, then verify per instance* (candidate already in the pool). Here Assumption 3.8 is posed as a property "any stochastic algorithm for solving bilevel problems needs to satisfy". Prop. 3.1 then verifies it for both inverse schemes (pp. 12–13), and §§4.4–4.5 show how to enforce Assumptions 3.9–3.10 (pp. 20–21).
- New-pattern candidate: *deterministic inexactness tied to the step*, here in a stochastic form: the LL error, the sampling error and the residual are each O(α_k) (pp. 13–14).
- New-pattern candidate: *nest the known theories as special cases*. The adjoint-solve and Neumann classes become two residual types of one assumption (p. 5, p. 10, p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate: *import a formalism and claim the first stochastic version*. The variant here is to claim the first method for a constraint class (p. 5, p. 30, p. 36).
- New-pattern candidate: *disclose omitted experiments* (p. 28, p. 32, p. 36).
- New-pattern candidate: *state where you lose*. DARTS is best on the first two continual-learning tasks, and BSG-N-FD is slightly slower in time (p. 36, p. 37).
- New-pattern candidate: *disclosed theory–implementation gap* ⚠. Only partly disclosed: the experiments test "practical implementations … as opposed to the standard BSG method (Algorithm 1)" (p. 22). The step-size and FD-parameter mismatches are not discussed (pp. 23, 26, 37).
- New-pattern candidate (process evidence): *supervisor-role authorship on student-led artefacts*. The code lives in the student's GitHub account, GdKent (p. 22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Parametrize the direction by a data tuple, d(D) = −(a − AB⁻¹b). Handle constraints by swapping B for the KKT Jacobian under LICQ, SCS and SOSC, so one proof covers the constrained and unconstrained cases and the deterministic and stochastic ones (pp. 7–8).
- [D4] Give each inexactness source a budget of O(α_k). The bias then contributes α_k² to the descent lemma, and the standard SG theorem [5, Thm 4.10] applies almost verbatim (pp. 13–18).
- [D4] Pair each abstract budget with an enforcement recipe: i_k = k² inner SG steps with β_i = γ/i, batch sizes that grow as α_k shrinks, or a solver tolerance (pp. 13, 20–21).
- [D4] Prove Lipschitz sensitivity of a formula like a − AB⁻¹b once, from three facts: sum; product of bounded factors; inverse with singular values bounded away from zero (p. 13; pp. 42–43).
- [D2] Hessian-free adjoint: run CG or GMRES with central-difference Hessian-vector products. If the loss is a sum of nonnegative terms, a Gauss–Newton rank-1 surrogate gives a closed-form adjoint (pp. 23–24).
- [D5] Give each method its own step-size grid window and state why (p. 26). Report the noise levels where a method could not be tuned (pp. 28, 32).

### S091 · A stochastic gradient method for trilevel optimization (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2505.06805)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. Giovannelli (now at the University of Cincinnati) and G. D. Kent (Lehigh) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period.
- It is the third paper of the Giovannelli–Kent–Vicente multilevel line, after S042 ([18]) and S070 ([19]) (p. 10).
- Funded by AFOSR award FA9550-23-1-0217 and ONR award N000142412656 (p. 9).
- The sharper rate and the proofs of the Lipschitz properties are deferred to Kent's PhD thesis, "in preparation" ([26]; p. 6, p. 11, p. 41).
- Self-citations: [18], [19] and the 1994 bibliography with Calamai ([42], p. 12). Unlike S070, it cites the bibliography, listing it among the multilevel surveys (p. 2).

**Read**:
- main text, pp. 1–9: in full;
- Appendix A (pp. 13–14), Appendix B (pp. 14–16) and Appendix F (pp. 41–47): read;
- Appendix C (pp. 17–30): read at step level (the prose of C.1, C.2 and C.4, and the structure of C.5);
- Appendix D (pp. 31–41) and Appendix E (p. 41): statements only.

The format (a 9-page body followed by "Technical Appendices", marked "Preprint. Under review.", p. 1) looks like an ML-conference submission. That is the reader's inference.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is extending a mature bilevel toolkit one level down, with an ML motivation. The gap is that no stochastic trilevel method exists: "As far as we know, this is the first work that addresses the stochastic setting of a trilevel problem, both theoretically and numerically" (p. 1).
  - **How prior work is sorted (p. 2)**, by the ingredient each one lacks:
    - an evolutionary strategy [40]: "without convergence guarantees";
    - a proximal gradient method [39]: guarantees but "lacking numerical validation, leaving the method’s practicality uncertain";
    - Sato et al. [38], BETTY [13] and federated trilevel learning [23, 24]: deterministic only.
  - **Problem**: TLO has x ∈ X, y ∈ Y(x) and z ∈ Z(x, y) (p. 1). Only the unconstrained middle-level (ML) and LL cases are treated (p. 2). Algorithm 3 has no projection, so X is effectively ℝⁿ (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: Place the new method between the two failure modes of prior work, *guarantees without numerics* and *numerics without guarantees*. Then deliver both (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Reduce by one level at a time.
  - **Reduction**: the unique LL solution map z(x, y) turns TLO into a bilevel problem (BLO). The unique ML solution y(x) then turns it into a single-level problem, f(x) = f1(x, y(x), z(x, y(x))) (2.1) (pp. 2–3).
  - **Gradients**: the trilevel adjoint gradient (2.2) is "essentially a classical adjoint gradient calculation applied to problem BLO" (p. 3). The ML gradient (2.3) is the bilevel adjoint gradient (p. 3). Third-order derivative tensors of f3 enter through ∇²_xy f̄ and ∇²_yy f̄, (A.5)–(A.6) (pp. 7, 13).
  - **Algorithm (pp. 3–4)**: three nested SG loops with warm starts:
    - Alg. 1 runs K LL steps;
    - Alg. 2 runs J ML steps, each preceded by a call to Alg. 1;
    - Alg. 3 takes a UL step after Alg. 2 and one more LL pass.
  - **What is kept from S042**: an approximate lower-level solve, an inexact adjoint gradient and an SG step (p. 2).
  - **What is new**:
    - the composite step size θ = αβγ (3.1), used as the single scale of all bias bounds (p. 4, p. 6);
    - constant, horizon-dependent step sizes in the theorem (p. 6).
  - **Practical variants (p. 7; pp. 41–44)**:
    - TSG-N-FD: CG with finite-difference Hessian-vector products, ε = 0.1;
    - TSG-AD: truncated Neumann series on (1/C0)∇²_zz f3, with Hessian-vector products by automatic differentiation (Algs. 5–7);
    - TSG-H (true Hessians and tensors): "not suited for practical optimization problems" and included only for completeness (p. 7).

    J and K are set by an increasing-accuracy rule, with thresholds 10⁻² on f1 and 10⁻¹ on f2 (p. 7).
  - **New formulation (F.18)**: a trilevel model of adversarial hyperparameter tuning. The UL tunes a regularization weight, the ML trains the model and the LL chooses worst-case data perturbations. The ML and LL are swapped relative to [38], because this "more accurately reflects the original minimax formulation" (p. 8; p. 46).
  - Transferable technique: To go from k to k+1 levels, eliminate the lowest level through its solution map and reuse the k-level direction formula unchanged. Only the new derivative terms (here third-order tensors) need fresh treatment (p. 3; p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model (pp. 5–6)**:
  - **Assumption 3.1**: f1 is C¹, f2 is C² and f3 is C³. The functions f1, ∇f1, f2, ∇f2, ∇²f2, ∇f3, ∇²f3 and ∇³f3 are all Lipschitz.
  - **Assumptions 3.2–3.3**: f3 is µ_z-strongly convex in z, and f̄ is µ_y-strongly convex in y. "In practice", f̄ is strongly convex when f2 is strongly convex in (y, z) and z(x, y) is affine (p. 5).
  - **Assumption 3.4**: unbiased i.i.d. estimates with bounded variances, including those of the third-order tensors.
  - **Assumption 3.5**: bounded inverses of the stochastic Hessians. The motivation is that regularization can enforce it (p. 6).
  - **Assumption 3.6**: the bias of the inverted stochastic Hessians is O(θ). It can be met with a truncated Neumann series and increasing sample sizes. The authors note that [11] instead "leave the number of samples as a parameter" (p. 6).
  - **Admitted as limiting**: strong convexity at the ML and LL is named as a limit that "constrain the applicability" of TSG (p. 9).
  - **Reader's note**: Assumption 3.1 makes f2 itself Lipschitz (p. 5), and z(x, y) is Lipschitz (E.2, p. 41). Then f̄(x, y) = f2(x, y, z(x, y)) is Lipschitz in y, which cannot hold together with µ_y-strong convexity on all of ℝᵐ (Assumption 3.3). The synthetic f2 (F.14) is quadratic, hence not Lipschitz (p. 44). The paper does not discuss this. Whether the proofs need the property only along the iterates was not checked.
  - **Reader's note**: the quartic test problem (F.16) "has two optimal solutions" at the LL (p. 45), so Assumption 3.2 fails for it. The authors pick the initial points so that the methods converge to one of the two solutions (p. 45). The mismatch with the assumptions is not commented on.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Lyapunov function**: V^i = f(x^i) + ‖y^i − y(x^i)‖² + ‖z^i − z(x^i)‖² + ‖z^i − z(x^i, y^i)‖² (B.1). The paper says "There is no particular property that is required from Lyapunov functions for our analysis"; the choice only serves the telescoping (p. 14).
  - **One lemma per difference in V^{i+1} − V^i ((B.2), p. 15; statements p. 16)**:
    - **B.1 (UL descent)**: a bias term ω̃α_i², plus a coupling term α_i L²_Fyz (E‖y(x) − y‖² + E‖z(x) − z‖²).
    - **B.2 (LL error)**: contraction factors (1 − γρ_f3)^K per ML step and (1 − γρ_f3)^{JK} per UL step, plus Kγ²σ² or JKγ²σ² noise terms. This is the SGD step on a strongly convex function via [33, Thm 2.1.12], applied by induction (pp. 16–18).
    - **B.3**: auxiliary LL errors relative to the moving ML iterate (p. 16).
    - **B.4 (ML error)**: contraction (1 − ψ_iβ_i)^J under the inexact bilevel gradient; a geometric-series and L'Hôpital argument bounds the accumulated terms (p. 16; App. C.4).
  - **Bias and moment bounds (App. D, pp. 31–41)**: the UL direction has bias ≤ ωθ_i and a bounded second moment (Lemmas D.3–D.6).
  - **Theorem 3.1 proof (C.5, pp. 25–30)**:
    1. Choose α_i = 1/√I, β_i = α_i/√J and γ_i = α_i/(√J√K) (p. 26).
    2. Sign analysis of the coefficients A1 (≥ 0) and A2–A4 (≤ 0) yields lower bounds on I, J and K (pp. 26–29).
    3. Consolidate the bounds into ς ≤ J, ϖ ≤ I and K ≥ Ξ(I, J) = O(J³I) (p. 29).
    4. Telescope (p. 30).
  - **Results**:
    - Theorem 3.1: (1/I) Σ E‖∇f(x^i)‖² = O(J/√I) (p. 6).
    - Remark 3.1: with step sizes that use unknown problem constants, O(1/√I) with K ≥ O(J⁴I). This matches the best general nonconvex bilevel rate [11]. The formal statement is only in the thesis [26] (p. 6).
    - The proofs are called "a non-trivial adaptation of the proofs in [11]" (p. 15).
  - **Interpretation**: the paper reads K ≥ O(J³I) as evidence that errors propagate upward: higher accuracy at a middle level needs much higher precision at the LL (p. 6).
  - **Reader's note**: the J in the numerator of O(J/√I) means that, for fixed I, more ML iterations weaken the bound. The paper ties this J to the extra J in the K requirement of Remark 3.1 (p. 6).
  - **Form of the complexity**: per UL iteration, with loop-length conditions. No total oracle count (I·J·K) is stated.
  - Transferable technique: For nested loops, build a Lyapunov function with one squared-error term per inner level (plus one auxiliary cross term). Prove one lemma per term. Then choose the loop lengths so that every coefficient except the descent term is non-positive, and telescope (pp. 14–16, 26–30).
  - Transferable technique: Express every bias assumption in one composite step θ = αβγ, so that the conditions of all levels scale together (pp. 4, 6).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Setup and code**: machine specs and code on GitHub (TSG) (p. 7).
  - **Synthetic problems**:
    - a quadratic trilevel problem, n = m = t = 50 (p. 44);
    - a quartic one (quartic LL), n = m = 5 and t = 1 (p. 44).

    Gaussian noise is added to gradients and Hessians but not to the third-order tensors, because the practical variants never use them (p. 7). Results are 95% t-distribution confidence intervals over 10 runs (p. 7).
  - **Step sizes**: decaying, ᾱ/i, β̄/j and γ̄/k, "carefully chosen … (without conducting extensive, time-consuming grid searches at all levels, as our goal is not to compare our algorithms against others)" (p. 45, Table 2).
  - **Synthetic results**:
    - Quadratic, deterministic: TSG-H is best (p. 7).
    - Quadratic, stochastic: TSG-H is very sensitive to noise in the f3 Hessian above 0.1 and is dropped from the high-noise plots. TSG-N-FD degrades with noise in ∇f3, and TSG-AD is the most robust (pp. 7–8).
    - Quartic: TSG-H is the slowest in time, and TSG-AD is again the most robust. Finite differences lose accuracy when the third derivatives are nonzero (p. 8).
    - Level-by-level diagnostic plots of f, f̄ and f3 with their optimal values are in the appendix (Figs. 8–9, p. 45).
  - **Adversarial hyperparameter tuning**:
    - data: red and white wine quality, and California housing; split 70/15/15;
    - model: linear, with MSE loss; minibatch 64; 10 runs;
    - step constants from a grid over {0.1, 0.01, 0.001} (p. 47).

    The metric is the test MSE after adding Gaussian noise (std 5) to the test features, averaged over 100 noise realizations (p. 8).
  - **Comparators (formulation ablation, not an algorithm race)**:
    - the bilevel problems obtained by deleting the UL (minimax adversarial training) or the LL (hyperparameter tuning without an adversary), solved by BSG-AD, which the paper describes as essentially equivalent to StocBiO (p. 8);
    - the ML/LL-swapped formulation of [38] (p. 47, Fig. 10).

    The algorithm of [38] is not run, because it is deterministic (p. 8). TSG-N-FD is dropped on (F.18) because it performs poorly. The explanation given is noise amplification: δ has one entry per data entry (rows × columns) (p. 8; p. 47).
  - **Results**: the trilevel formulation is "the most consistently effective". The bilevel variants have competitive runtimes, and their relative ranking is read as a diagnosis of each dataset: red and white wine depend more on hyperparameter tuning, California housing more on adversarial learning (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: Evaluate a new multilevel model against the models obtained by deleting each outer or inner level, and against prior work's level ordering. Read the ranking of the reduced models as a diagnosis of the data (pp. 8–9, 47).
  - Transferable technique: For robustness claims, report the test error under a fixed perturbation of the test features, averaged over many perturbation draws (100 here) (p. 8).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Claim**: the first stochastic trilevel method (pp. 1–2, 9).
  - **How prior work is sorted**: by the ingredient each one lacks: guarantees, numerics, or stochasticity (p. 2).
  - **Proof and rate lineage**: the proof adapts Chen, Sun & Yin [11] ("Closing the gap"), and the rate is benchmarked against theirs (pp. 6, 15).
  - **Modelling disagreement with Sato et al. [38]**: argued in words (p. 8), then tested (p. 47).
  - **Borrowed conventions**: from the SG survey [8], the rule that Hessians need lower noise (larger batches) than gradients (p. 8). From [22], the Neumann scaling by C0 (p. 43).
  - Transferable technique: When you disagree with a prior formulation, state the modelling reason in one sentence, then run your solver on *their* formulation as well as yours, so that the solver is held fixed (p. 8, p. 47).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Priority claims**: "first-ever" in the abstract, "As far as we know" (p. 1), and "the first" (p. 2, p. 9).
  - **Main theorem**: stated in its simplest implementable form, "due to the intuitive choice of its step-sizes and the appeal to its direct implementability". The sharper, constant-dependent version is a remark (p. 6).
  - **Interpretation paragraph**: after the theorem, a paragraph turns a proof condition into a claim about hierarchy and ends with an open question (p. 6).
  - **Limitations**: a paragraph headed "Potential limitations" names three specific items (p. 9).
  - **Proportions**: about 35 of the 47 pages are appendices (pp. 13–47).
  - Transferable technique: After the main theorem, write one paragraph on what its parameter coupling says about the problem class. Here: LL errors propagate upward. End that paragraph with the open question it raises (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contributions**:
    - the TSG algorithm;
    - a nonconvex rate covering all kinds of inexactness (pp. 1, 9);
    - two practical Hessian-free variants (p. 7);
    - a new trilevel formulation of adversarial hyperparameter tuning (p. 8);
    - code (p. 7).
  - **Limits written (specific, p. 9)**:
    - strong convexity at the ML and LL;
    - bilevel formulations "remain competitive in terms of running time";
    - only regression tasks on tabular data.
  - **Future work**:
    - how the accuracy at one level relates to the precision needed at lower levels in general multilevel problems (p. 6, p. 9);
    - penalization approaches for nonconvex lower levels (p. 9).
  - **Not written**:
    - no total oracle complexity;
    - the experiments use 1/i-type decaying steps and adaptive J and K, not the theorem's step sizes (p. 6 vs p. 7, p. 45);

### Batch answer to the intent questions
- **(a) Method 4 carries over to SGD.** Both papers keep a textbook SG proof and change only the direction object:
  - S042: the constrained LL changes only the B block of d(D) (pp. 7–8, 18);
  - S091: one extra level adds one Lyapunov term (pp. 3, 14).
- **(b) Method 3 does not.** Both papers use the stochastic-approximation tradition that SKILL.md contrasts Vicente with: unbiased estimates, decaying or horizon-dependent steps, bounds in expectation (S042 pp. 18, 21; S091 pp. 5–6). The Method 3 contrast clause should be scoped to the DFO work.
- **(c) Only UL iterations are counted.** Inner-loop costs appear as conditions, i_k = k² and K ≥ O(J³I), but are never added up into a total oracle count (S042 p. 20; S091 p. 6).
- **(d) The theory–practice gap is partly disclosed.** Both papers label the heuristic or reference variants (S042 p. 5; S091 p. 7). Neither discusses the mismatch between the step sizes of the theorems and those of the experiments (S042 pp. 18, 26; S091 pp. 6, 45).
