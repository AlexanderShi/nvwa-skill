**Batch intent**: The 2009 book *Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization* (Conn, Scheinberg & Vicente) has no open full text; the skill holds it only as an abstract-level card (S001, batch a3-01), and 08 §11 lists it as the first open gap. This batch reads the open material around the book, six items: the authors' table of contents (B001), the two author-maintained errata lists for the first and second printings (B002, B003), the authors' addendum deriving the quadratic-regression error bounds (B004), and two external reviews (B005, Mathematics of Computation; B006, SIAM Review). ### B001 · Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization — table of contents (book front matter; Conn, Scheinberg & Vicente, MPS-SIAM Series on Optimization 8, SIAM 2009; book DOI 10.1137/1.9780898718768)
**Read**: full (every line of the three pages) · **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, K. Scheinberg · **Voice**: the three authors' own organization of the field. It is stated methodology at the level of what comes first and what gets space, and it is joint, not Vicente's alone · **Position in trajectory**: the "DFO book" of the 2006–2010 row of the SKILL.md trajectory. The pages carry the typesetting stamp "2008/11/17" (pp. 1–3). The book is contemporary with the geometry papers [S010; S020] and the general derivative-free trust-region paper [S006], and it comes before the complexity line [S023, 2013].

**Architecture** (starting pages from the TOC; lengths are differences of starting pages):
- Front: Preface (book p. xi) and Ch. 1 Introduction (book pp. 1–12) with five sections: why DFO (§1.1), examples of problems where derivatives are unavailable (§1.2), limitations of DFO (§1.3), how DF algorithms should work (§1.4), a short summary of the book (§1.5) (p. 1).
- Part I "Sampling and modeling" (book pp. 13–112; five chapters, about 100 pages) (pp. 1–2):
  - Ch. 2 Sampling and linear models (book pp. 15–34): positive spanning sets and positive bases (§2.1), gradient estimates used in direct search (§2.2), linear interpolation and regression models (§2.3) and their error bounds (§2.4), other geometrical concepts (§2.5), simplex gradients (§2.6).
  - Ch. 3 Interpolating nonlinear models (book pp. 35–56): basic concepts, Lagrange polynomials, Λ-poisedness and the condition number as a measure of well poisedness (§§3.1–3.4).
  - Ch. 4 Regression nonlinear models (book pp. 57–72): the same notions "in the regression sense" (§§4.1–4.4).
  - Ch. 5 Underdetermined interpolating models (book pp. 73–88): the choice of an underdetermined model (§5.1), Lagrange polynomials and Λ-poisedness (§5.2), minimum Frobenius norm models (§5.3).
  - Ch. 6 Ensuring well poisedness and suitable derivative-free models (book pp. 89–112): fully linear and fully quadratic models (§6.1), well poisedness via Lagrange polynomials (§6.2) and via pivotal algorithms (§6.3), practical considerations of geometry improvement algorithms (§6.4), regression and minimum Frobenius norm models (§6.5).
- Part II "Frameworks and algorithms" (book pp. 113–226; five chapters, about 114 pages) (pp. 2–3):
  - Ch. 7 Directional direct-search methods (book pp. 115–140): coordinate search (§7.1) before the general framework (§7.2); global convergence in the continuously differentiable case (§7.3) and the nonsmooth case (§7.4); then three globalization sections: simple decrease with integer lattices (§7.5), the mesh adaptive direct-search method (§7.6), imposing sufficient decrease (§7.7).
  - Ch. 8 Simplicial direct-search methods (book pp. 141–162): Nelder–Mead (§8.1), its properties (§8.2), and a globally convergent variant (§8.3, book pp. 149–160). At 12 pages, §8.3 is the longest section of the book by page span (next section's starting page minus its own).
  - Ch. 9 Line-search methods based on simplex derivatives (book pp. 163–172): a line-search framework (§9.1), first-order global convergence (§9.2), analysis for noise (§9.3), the implicit-filtering algorithm (§9.4), other simplex derivatives (§9.5).
  - Ch. 10 Trust-region methods based on derivative-free models (book pp. 173–206; at 34 pages the longest chapter): framework basics (§10.1), conditions on the trust-region models (§10.2), first-order methods and their global convergence (§§10.3–10.4), second-order methods and their global convergence (§§10.5–10.6), model accuracy in larger concentric balls (§10.7), the trust-region subproblem (§10.8).
  - Ch. 11 Trust-region interpolation-based methods (book pp. 207–226): common features (§11.1), the "DFO" approach (§11.2), Powell's methods (§11.3), wedge methods (§11.4).
- Part III "Review of other topics" (book pp. 227–250; two chapters, 24 pages) (p. 3): Ch. 12 on surrogate model management (surrogate modeling §12.1; rigorous optimization frameworks to handle surrogate models §12.2). Ch. 13 reviews constrained and other extensions, organized by framework (directional direct search §13.1; trust-region interpolation-based methods §13.2), plus one section on global optimization, mixed-integer programming and other problems (§13.3).
- Back matter: Appendix "Software for derivative-free optimization" (book p. 251), Bibliography (book pp. 255–270), Index (book p. 271) (p. 3).
- Apparatus: exercises close every chapter except Chs. 1, 11 and 13. A notes-and-references section closes Chs. 4–11 (titled "Other notes and references" in Chs. 6–11) but not Chs. 2, 3, 12 or 13 (pp. 1–3).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The book enters through *why* and *when*, before any tool: Ch. 1 has sections on why DFO, examples where derivatives are unavailable, and the limitations of DFO (§§1.1–1.3, p. 1). The first normative title is "How derivative-free algorithms should work" (§1.4, book p. 7; p. 1). What these sections say is not read (title only). B005's reviewer reports that the introduction runs the key algorithms on Rosenbrock's function and a perturbed quadratic (B005 p. 1). Errata items 1–2 fall on book pp. 9–10, inside §1.4 (B002 p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: open a monograph or long survey with separate sections for motivation, examples, limitations and how the algorithms should work, all before the first definition (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & architecture**: Tools first, algorithms second, extensions last.
  - Part I (about 100 pages) builds the sampling and modeling tools before any algorithm framework (pp. 1–2): positive spanning sets, interpolation and regression models, poisedness and its condition-number measure, and algorithms that ensure well poisedness.
  - Part I serves both method families. Ch. 2 opens with positive spanning sets and "Gradient estimates used in direct search" (§§2.1–2.2, p. 1), the direct-search tools, before the interpolation chapters (title only).
  - Part II gives each family its own chapters: two for direct search (directional; simplicial), one for simplex-derivative line search, and two for trust regions (a framework chapter, then a chapter of named practical methods) (pp. 2–3).
  - Constrained problems, surrogates, global and mixed-integer problems are reviewed, not developed (Part III, p. 3).
  - What gets a whole chapter: each model type (interpolation, regression, underdetermined; Chs. 3–5), the maintenance of the sample set (Ch. 6), Nelder–Mead-type simplicial search (Ch. 8) and surrogate management (Ch. 12) (pp. 1–3).
  - Transferable technique: when two algorithm families share an analytical core, write the core once (here, sample-set geometry). Then give each family a framework chapter that points back to it (pp. 1–3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: not stated in a table of contents. The titles show only the scope. The nonsmooth case appears only in direct search (§7.4), noise only in simplex-derivative line search (§9.3), and second-order results only for trust regions (§§10.5–10.6) (pp. 2–3). Title only.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The convergence titles all read "Global convergence ..." (§§7.3, 7.4, 9.2, 10.4, 10.6; pp. 2–3). No title mentions rates, complexity or evaluation counts (pp. 1–3). Error bounds are titled for linear interpolation and regression (§2.4, p. 1). Proof content not read.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a. No section title mentions numerical comparisons, test sets or benchmarking. The only software item is an appendix (p. 3). According to B006's reviewer, the introduction holds the book's only comparison between methods (B006 p. 1).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Other authors' methods appear as section titles: the mesh adaptive direct-search method (§7.6), Nelder–Mead (§§8.1–8.3), implicit filtering (§9.4), the "DFO" approach (§11.2), Powell's methods (§11.3) and wedge methods (§11.4) (pp. 2–3). The globalization alternatives sit side by side in Ch. 7: integer lattices with simple decrease (§7.5), MADS (§7.6), sufficient decrease (§7.7) (p. 2). This is a taxonomy, not a verdict (title only).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Some section titles are the question they answer ("Why derivative-free optimization", "How derivative-free algorithms should work", p. 1).
  - Chapters carry a regular apparatus of exercises and notes and references (pp. 1–3).
  - The simplest instance comes before the general framework: coordinate search (§7.1) before the directional framework (§7.2). The framework chapter (Ch. 10) comes before the chapter of named practical methods (Ch. 11) (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: put the simplest concrete method first, then the framework that contains it, then the named practical methods (pp. 2–3).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - What the table of contents leaves out or keeps short:
    - no title on complexity or rates (pp. 1–3);
    - no multiobjective, stochastic, probabilistic or randomized titles (pp. 1–3);
    - constraints, global optimization and mixed-integer problems only in review chapters (Ch. 13, §13.3, p. 3);
    - no title on one-dimensional derivative-free methods (B005's reviewer names this gap, B005 p. 3).
  - The limitations section sits in Ch. 1 (§1.3, p. 1), so the authors treat limits as introductory material (title only).
  - Reading path for a user of this skill (ordered by the skill's methods; title only):
    1. §§1.3–1.4 (limitations; how DF algorithms should work), to set the frame.
    2. §§2.1–2.2, then Ch. 7, especially the framework (§7.2) and the three globalization options (§§7.5–7.7). This is the background for Method 1 and its glue.
    3. §8.3 (the globally convergent Nelder–Mead variant): a practitioners' heuristic given a guarantee (Method 1).
    4. §2.6 (simplex gradients), then Ch. 9 (§9.3 noise; §9.4 implicit filtering): background for H7 (reuse evaluations) and Workflow D.
    5. Chs. 3–6 (poisedness; §5.3 minimum Frobenius norm models; §6.1 fully linear models), then Ch. 10 (§10.2 conditions on the models). This is the deterministic baseline that Method 3 relaxes [S014]. Ch. 11 is the model-based lens (Roundtable: Powell, Conn).
    6. Ch. 12 (§12.2, rigorous frameworks for surrogates) and Ch. 13 (extensions by framework): Method 1's search step with surrogates, and Method 4.
    7. The software appendix: a starting point for Method 5.

    B006's reviewer proposes a similar order for a course: a cursory reading of Part I, then Part II, referring back when needed (B006 p. 1).

- **D3 Assumptions & model**: One scope note matters. The old Theorem 2.13 would be valid if the regression model had the form m(y) = f(y⁰) + (y − y⁰)ᵀg, "and that such a g would then coincide with the regression simplex gradient of Page 33" (p. 1). The assumption named in the restated theorem is "Assumption 2.2", whose content is not in the list (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques** (the replacement proof of item 3, pp. 1–2; formulas as extracted):
  1. Assume y⁰ = 0 without loss of generality, by the argument at the end of the proof of Theorem 3.16 (p. 1).
  2. Write M = [1 0; e L] and its scaled version M̂ = [1 0; e L̂], with e the vector of ones (p. 1).
  3. The Taylor data satisfy M[f(y⁰); ∇f(y⁰)] − f(Y) = r with |r_i| ≤ (ν/2)Δ² (p. 1).
  4. The coefficient error is M†r, with M† the Moore–Penrose inverse, which is the left inverse (MᵀM)⁻¹Mᵀ when M has full column rank (footnote, p. 1).
  5. Scale: M† = diag(1, (1/Δ)I) M̂†, the new (2.9), stated without proof (p. 1). Showing (2.9) and concluding the proof is left to the reader as the rewritten Exercise 10: "Conclude the proof of Theorem 2.13 by first showing (2.9)." (item 6, p. 2). So the replacement is a proof outline, not a complete proof.
  6. The bounds are κ_eg = ν(1 + p^{1/2}‖M̂†‖/2) and κ_ef = 2κ_eg + ν/2 (p. 2).

  The device is the one used in the addendum (B004 pp. 1–2) and in the geometry paper's error bounds, where Taylor expansions are subtracted and the result is Δ-scaled [S010 pp. 12–16]. The regression bounds of the 2008 regression paper were stated without proof as "straightforward adaptations" [S020 pp. 14–16]. The book's regression results are the ones corrected here (items 3, 11) and derived in B004. The sources do not link the two; this is a sequence, not a stated cause.
  - Transferable technique: to bound a least-squares model's error, write the Taylor coefficients as solving the regression system up to a residual r. The coefficient error is then M†r. Factor the Δ-powers out of M† by scaling to the unit ball, so that the constant is the norm of the scaled pseudo-inverse (pp. 1–2).

- **D2 Core idea**: Treat the Taylor coefficients at y⁰ as an exact solution of the regression system up to a residual, so that the model's coefficient error equals M†r.   - the scaled set Ŷ ⊂ B(0; 1).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques** (formulas as extracted; the hat on M† is not always visible in the extraction):
  1. M[f(y⁰); ∇f(y⁰); vec(∇²f(y⁰))] − f(Y) = r, with each |r_i| bounded by a constant times Δ³; the extracted text prints the factor as (ν/2)Δ³ (p. 1).
  2. Subtracting the least-squares solution gives [f(y⁰); ∇f(y⁰); vec(∇²f(y⁰))] − [c; g; vec(H)] = M†r, display (0.1) (p. 1).
  3. Scaling: M† = diag(1, (1/Δ)I_n, (1/Δ²)I_p̄) M̂†, with p̄ = n(n + 1)/2, display (0.2) (p. 1).
  4. Hessian: ‖vec(∇²f(y⁰)) − vec(H)‖ ≤ p̄^{1/2}(ν₂/2)‖M†‖Δ. A factor √2 passes from the vector to the matrix, giving (0.3). Adding ν₂Δ moves the bound from y⁰ to y (pp. 1–2).
  5. Gradient: ‖∇f(y⁰) − g‖ ≤ n^{1/2}(ν₂/2)‖M†‖Δ², display (0.4). Taylor's theorem with a mean-value point t ∈ (0, 1), adding and subtracting ∇²f(y⁰)y, gives ‖∇f(y) − ∇m(y)‖ ≤ (ν₂ + ((n^{1/2} + √2 p̄^{1/2})/2)ν₂‖M†‖)Δ² (p. 2).
  6. Function values: the same expansion gives |f(y) − m(y)| ≤ (ν₂/2 + (1/2 + n^{1/2}/2 + √2 p̄^{1/2}/4)ν₂‖M†‖)Δ³ (p. 2).

The 2008 regression paper had stated its regression bounds without proof [S020 pp. 14–16].

Then move from the centre to any point in the ball by Taylor's theorem with a mean-value point. Each dimension factor (n^{1/2}, p̄^{1/2}, √2) then has a visible source (pp. 1–2).
