### S077 · A globally convergent primal-dual interior-point filter method for nonlinear programming: new filter optimality measures and computational results (Preprint 08-49, Dept. Mathematics, Univ. Coimbra 2008, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. Silva (Coimbra), M. Ulbrich (TU München), S. Ulbrich (TU Darmstadt) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the derivative-based NLP line. It is the sequel to Ulbrich–Ulbrich–Vicente, Math. Program. 100 (2004) [18] (p. 32; card S007), with support from an ESA contract (p. 1). **Read**: partial.
- Read: §1–§3 (pp. 1–17), including the proof of Lemma 2.1 (pp. 7–8) and the start of the proof of Lemma 2.5 (pp. 9–10); Assumption 4.1, Theorem 4.1 and every lemma statement of §4 (pp. 17–25); §5–§6 (pp. 25–31).
- Skimmed: the proofs of Lemmas 4.6–4.11 and Theorems 4.12–4.13.
- Not read: the rest of the proof of Lemma 2.5 and the proof of Lemma 2.7 (pp. 10–14), and the appendix (pp. 32–34).
- Not available: Figure 3.1 (caption only, p. 34).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is two defects of the authors' own 2004 method [18].
  - **Defect 1**: the optimality filter entry x^Tz/n + ‖∇xℓ‖² (2.10) "is based on first-order principles", so it may not tell minimization from maximization (p. 2, p. 4).
  - **Defect 2**: the method depends on exact Hessians of the Lagrangian (p. 2).
  - **Practical goal**: turn the method into a solver, ipfilter (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Kept from [18]**: the normal/tangential split of the Newton primal-dual step, (2.8)–(2.9); a step-control parameter ∆ that "plays a role comparable to a trust-region radius"; the central neighbourhood; the envelope filter; the restoration phase (pp. 4–5, 14–17).
  - **Change 1, the filter entry**: the second entry becomes objective plus a multiple of complementarity, either θg = ℓ(x,y,z) + (c+n)µ (2.11) or θg = f(x) + cµ (2.12). These are closer to the SLP/SQP filter entry f(x) (p. 4). The ratio test ρk ≥ η on θg against its linear model is enforced only when the model decrease is at least κθ(wk)². Otherwise wk is added to the filter (pp. 15–17).
  - **Change 2, inexact Hessians**: H is allowed if ‖[H − ∇²xxℓ]∇xℓ‖ ≤ N‖∇xℓ‖∆^{q−1} (2.21). The central neighbourhood is generalized to θh + θℓ^p ≤ Mµ with p ∈ [1, 2] (2.18) (p. 6). Condition (2.21) can be met by a finite-difference product r̃ ≈ ∇²xxℓ ∇xℓ plus the requirement H r̃ = ∇xℓ, for instance inside a quasi-Newton update, as the paper suggests (2.28) (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumption 4.1 (p. 17):
  - (A1) the iterates are bounded;
  - (A2) ∇h and ∇²xwℓ are Lipschitz;
  - (A3) {Hk} is bounded and allows the decrease condition (2.22);
  - (A4) Hk + ½Xk⁻¹Zk is PSD on the null space of ∇h(xk)^T;
  - (A5) ‖KKT(wk)⁻¹‖ ≤ C.

  The authors state that (A5) is what rules out infeasible problems. They also note that (A4) holds for PSD Hk, and locally under second-order sufficiency with strict complementarity (p. 17). The constant c in θg must satisfy (2.33) (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 4.1: lim inf θ(wk) + θℓ(wk) + µk = 0 (p. 17). The proof reuses [18] wherever it can: Lemmas 4.4, 4.5, 4.8 and 4.9 are each proved by a one-line pointer to [18], e.g., "See [18, Lemma 5]" (pp. 18–21). The new work is in two places:
  - Lemma 2.5 (p. 9) bounds the change of θg from above and the model decrease from below.
  - Lemma 2.1 (pp. 6–8) gives sufficient decrease of θℓ^p under (2.21). Its proof uses Hölder-type gradient estimates for ‖x‖^p (Lemma 2.2), which are proved in the appendix (not read).

  The endgame is the standard filter split: infinitely many filter additions (Theorem 4.12) or finitely many (Theorem 4.13) (pp. 24–25). The authors point out that Lemma 4.9 holds only for a particular subsequence. They give two repairs: never remove dominated entries, or modify the acceptance test (p. 21).
- **D5 Experiments**: ipfilter 0.2 (Fortran 90, MA27) against ipopt 3.5.1 on two collections: CUTEr of Sept. 2008 (631 problems) and an old CUTE set (469) (Tables 6.1–6.2, p. 30). Metrics are problems solved and average iterations (Table 6.3, p. 31):

  | Collection | ipfilter solved | ipopt solved | ipfilter avg. iterations | ipopt avg. iterations |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Old CUTE | 449 | 448 | 27.55 | 27.19 |
  | CUTEr | 532 (84.34%) | 549 (87.00%) | 47.44 | 38.58 |

  On QPs ipfilter is ahead (Table 6.4, p. 31). In tests not reported in detail, entry (2.12) was the best of the three filter entries (p. 31). No profiles, CPU times or evaluation counts are reported.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contributions**: theory (the new filter entries and inexact Hessians keep global convergence) and software. ipfilter is free for academic and research use and was selected for an ESA project on space-trajectory optimization (p. 2).
  - **Limits, stated concretely**: numerics for (2.21) without second derivatives are out of scope (p. 8); the code sets p = 1 and supports only exact second derivatives (p. 29); on current CUTEr it is less robust than ipopt (p. 31), which the introduction phrases as "already competitive" (p. 2).
  - The paper ends at §6 (p. 31), with no conclusions section.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 14–15): the skeleton of [18] is kept, and only the acceptance measure (the second filter entry) and the Hessian requirement change. The proof reuses [18] lemma by lemma (pp. 18–21).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 2, 30–31): the paper ships a free solver and compares it with the strongest available baseline (ipopt) on a large collection. It reports aggregate tables, not profiles, and does not hide the collection where it loses.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pp. 6, 8): an expensive requirement (the exact Hessian) is weakened to its action along one vector, with ∆-dependent accuracy. The weakening is deterministic, not probabilistic.
- new-pattern candidate: *weakest condition the proof touches*. Ask only for what the proof uses (here the product H∇xℓ). The same move appears in S085 (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ask a Hessian approximation only for its action along the single vector the proof uses: ‖[H − ∇²ℓ]∇ℓ‖ ≤ N‖∇ℓ‖∆^{q−1}. Meet the condition with one finite-difference Hessian–vector product plus the secant-type condition H r̃ = ∇ℓ (pp. 6, 8).
- To make a first-order optimality measure distinguish minima from maxima, add the objective to a constant multiple of complementarity (f + cµ). Then reprove only the lemma that bounds its change along the step (Lemma 2.5) (pp. 4, 9).
- When you modify a published convergent method, cite the unchanged lemmas one by one ("See [18, Lemma 9]") and reprove only those that touch the changed object (pp. 18–21).
- Report both the old and the current version of a test collection, together with the problem class where you win (QPs), next to the aggregate where you lose (pp. 30–31).

**Quotes**:
- "In fact, the optimality filter entry in [18] mentioned above is based on first-order principles and thus might have weaknesses in distinguishing well between minimization and maximization." (p. 2)
- "A numerical study on the imposition of (2.21) without second-order derivatives is out of the scope of this paper." (p. 8)

### S085 · Local analysis of the feasible primal-dual interior-point method (Computational Optimization and Applications 2008, DOI 10.1007/s10589-007-9075-3)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. Silva (PhD student, Coimbra) and J. Soares (Coimbra) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the derivative-based local-convergence line. It continues Vicente (COAP 2000) and Vicente–Wright (COAP 2002), refs [12] and [13] (p. 1, p. 16), and is a companion to S077. **Read**: full (pp. 1–16). The extraction is defective (see the batch header), so there are no quotes.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The existing local q-quadratic theory of primal-dual IPMs (El-Bakry et al. [5]; Yamashita–Yabe [14]) covers *infeasible* methods, in which feasibility of the inequalities is reached only in the limit (p. 1). Two feasible variants exist, but each needs something extra:
  - Gould–Orban–Sartenaer–Toint's log-barrier method is only superlinear, with a rate arbitrarily close to quadratic (p. 2).
  - Tits et al. update the inequality multipliers by a special formula (p. 2).

  The gap: a feasible primal-dual method with no special provision or correction formula for these multipliers (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Kept**: the standard Newton primal-dual IPM, whose other components are said to remain essentially the same (p. 2): a Newton step on the perturbed KKT system (5), and the parameter rules 1 − τk ≤ c2‖F0(wk)‖ and µk ≤ c3‖F0(wk)‖² (12)–(13) (pp. 3, 5).
  - **Changed**: no slack variables. Strict feasibility g(x) < 0 is kept by a step length (8) that includes ᾱᵢ, the first root of gᵢ(xk + α∆xk) (9)–(10). That root may be computed inexactly, provided the residual satisfies (11): −rᵢ ≤ min{σ(−gᵢ(xk)), −gᵢ(xk)c1‖∆wk‖/(1 + c1‖∆wk‖)} (pp. 4–5).
  - **In practice**: a warm-started scalar Newton iteration with a safeguard against negative α (Algorithm 5.1, pp. 11–12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - A1–A3 are the classical nondegenerate assumptions (p. 5): C² with Lipschitz second derivatives, LICQ, second-order sufficiency and strict complementarity (p. 5).
  - A4 requires the binding inequalities to be concave (p. 6).
  - In §4, A4 is replaced by an angle condition: lim inf ∇gᵢ(xk)^T∆xk/‖∆xk‖ = 4β > 0, i.e., the primal step is not asymptotically orthogonal to the binding-constraint gradients (28) (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The key result is Lemma 3.3: 1 − αk ≤ (1 − τk) + κζ(‖F0(wk)‖ + µk‖e‖) (p. 6). It is proved in four cases, according to whether the step length is set by a multiplier or by an inequality, and whether the corresponding constraint is active or inactive (pp. 7–9). The mean-value form of the root (23) and concavity (25) handle the active-inequality case (pp. 8–9). The q-quadratic Theorem 3.1 then follows Yamashita–Yabe's argument, described briefly for completeness (pp. 9–10). The nonconcave case replaces (25) with the bound (27), obtained from a Taylor expansion under (28) (pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Experiments**: Fortran 90 with dense LAPACK, on small CUTEr problems that have nonlinear inequalities and a strictly feasible x0. The feasible method is compared with the slack-based infeasible method, both without globalization. The feasible method converged on 15 problems, the infeasible one on 11, with similar iteration counts. The root finding costs about 2 extra evaluations per iteration. Table 1 lists every problem with its failure mode (pp. 13–14).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: a local-rate theory (q-quadratic under concavity, or under the angle condition) plus preliminary numerics.
  - **Limits, stated concretely**: a strictly feasible x0 is needed and finding one is out of scope; the method is efficient only when the inequalities are few or have easy structure; there is no globalization (pp. 13, 15).
  - **Interpretation**: the angle condition is linked to the notion of centrality (p. 15).
  - **Suggestion**: impose strict feasibility only on the inequalities that restrict the domain of f (p. 15).
  - No future-work list.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 4): the Newton primal-dual skeleton and its parameter rules are kept; only the treatment of the inequalities (strict feasibility through the step length) changes. The rate proof is borrowed (pp. 9–10).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 13–14): a head-to-head comparison against the natural baseline, with every failure listed. The problem set is small, there are no profiles, and no code release is mentioned.
- new-pattern candidate: *weakest condition the proof touches* (p. 10, p. 15). Concavity is replaced by the angle condition that the proof actually uses, which is then read in the field's own terms (centrality). Compare S077 (pp. 6, 8).
- new-pattern candidate: *deterministic inexactness tied to the step* (pp. 4–5). The root-finding residual is allowed to shrink with ‖∆wk‖ only as fast as the fast rate requires. Compare S047 (pp. 8–9) and S077 (p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To carry a local-rate proof over to a variant, isolate where the new feature enters (here the step length) and prove one bound of the form 1 − αk ≤ (1 − τk) + O(‖F0(wk)‖ + µk). The rest of the q-quadratic argument can be cited (pp. 6–10).
- Let a nonlinear sub-computation (the first root of gᵢ along the step) be inexact, with a residual tolerance proportional to −gᵢ(xk)·‖∆wk‖. A warm-started scalar Newton method then meets it in about 2 evaluations (pp. 4–5, 11–13).
- Put both methods and every test problem in one table, with the failure reason for each run (α < 10⁻¹⁰, it > 200, div) (p. 14).

**Quotes**: none (extraction defect; see the batch header).

### S083 · Implicitly and densely discrete black-box optimization problems (Optimization Letters 2009, DOI 10.1007/s11590-009-0120-2)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: single author (L. N. Vicente) · **Position in trajectory**: a short extension of directional direct search written during the 2008–2009 DFO-book period (ref. [5], p. 9). It sits next to MADS (pp. 5, 8). **Read**: full (pp. 1–9).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is an application-driven modelling gap: continuous models whose evaluation first rounds or projects the variables onto an unknown discrete set (p. 1). The problem is min f(x) over x ∈ L ∩ Ω. The set L is:
  - (L1) discrete;
  - (L2) closed;
  - (L3) such that the distance from any point to L is bounded (p. 2).

  Definition 1.1 gives a new notion of local minimizer with two radii σout > σin and point sets Rout and Rbetween (p. 2). The author reports up front a counterexample from Audet: for f = −|x|, every point of a sparse L is a local minimizer. He restricts the definition to densely discrete problems, for which it "suits the convergence purposes of a vast class of direct-search methods" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: This is directional direct search with an extreme barrier and simple-decrease acceptance (Algorithm 2.1, p. 4). The "only difference" from standard polling is one test (p. 5): if ‖PL(xk + αk d) − xk‖ ≤ c αk, the projection has collapsed back to xk. Polling then stops and αk is *increased* (step (B), p. 4). The directions satisfy ‖d‖ ≥ 1 and αk d → 0, and their union is dense in the unit sphere (D1–D3), which is compatible with MADS (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - L is unknown, and the user returns both the projected point and its value. If only the value is returned, the problem counts as noisy or dynamic-accuracy and is out of scope (p. 4).
  - PL is taken as the ℓ2 projection, but it may be any operator that respects (L3) (p. 3).
  - The level set is bounded (Theorem 3.1, p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 3.1 (pp. 5–6). Because L ∩ L(x0) is finite, the iterates are eventually constant at x*. The step size is then bounded:
  - Above, because otherwise (L3) is contradicted.
  - Away from zero, because otherwise (L1)–(L2) are contradicted.

  So αk oscillates. On one subsequence αk → αout, where polls fail along dense directions; on another αk → αin, where the projection collapses. The theorem covers condition (2), and condition (4) roughly. It does not cover condition (3) (the points in Rbetween); that "seems to require" dense sampling in the unit ball (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: A MATLAB illustration on one 2-D oscillatory quadratic over the lattice γZ² (γ = 0.1, 0.01), with a random orthogonal positive basis [Q −Q]. The plots of αk oscillate between two levels, as the theorem predicts (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Called a "first attempt" (p. 8). It leaves five open questions (p. 8):
  1. How to capture the points in Rbetween.
  2. Stopping rules that need no estimate of lim inf/lim sup αk.
  3. Aligning pattern-search lattices with the discrete structure of the application.
  4. Using ‖PL(x+αd) − (x+αd)‖ in the algorithm.
  5. Definitions that also work for sparse discrete sets.

  A referee suggested an application: caches of previously evaluated points (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 4–5): the poll/step-size skeleton is kept, and one test changes what an unsuccessful or "too fine" poll means.
- Heuristic 10 / warning sign 6 ✅ evidence (p. 3): the counterexample to the new definition, which Audet reported privately, is printed up front and the scope is narrowed rather than hidden.
- Method 2: no link (asymptotic result only, pp. 5–6).
- new-pattern candidate: *solution concept tailored to what the algorithm can certify* (pp. 2–3, 6). The stationarity notion is designed to fit direct search, and the theorem then states which parts of it are certified.

**Transferable techniques**:
- If the variable space has hidden granularity, add one test to the poll step that detects a mesh finer than the structure (‖PL(xk+αd) − xk‖ ≤ cα). In that case increase α instead of decreasing it (pp. 4–5).
- Bound the step size from above and from below by contradicting, in turn, each defining property of the hidden set: (L3) for the upper bound, (L1)–(L2) for the lower bound (p. 6).
- Design the numerical illustration to show the theorem's prediction (here the oscillating αk), not benchmark performance (p. 7).
- Close a short paper with a numbered list of specific open questions (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Many optimization problems are only apparently continuous." (p. 1)
- "This paper is a first attempt to shed some light on the solution of implicitly discrete black-box optimization problems." (p. 8)

### S051 · On partially sparse recovery (Preprint 11-13, Dept. Mathematics, Univ. Coimbra 2011, arXiv:1304.2809)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. S. Bandeira (Princeton PACM) and K. Scheinberg (Lehigh) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a compressed-sensing spin-off of the sparse-model DFO paper S029 (ref. [5], p. 5). The file is arXiv v1, dated 9 Apr. 2013 (p. 1). **Read**: full (pp. 1–6).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Recover x = (x1, x2), where x1 is sparse and x2 possibly dense, by solving min ‖x1‖₁ s.t. A1x1 + A2x2 = y (3) (p. 1). The motivating uses are image reconstruction and "sparse Hessian recovery", i.e., their own DFO models [5] (p. 1). Earlier sufficient conditions exist (Vaswani–Lu, Friedlander et al., Jacques). The authors say they "are not the simple extensions of the NSP and RIP properties" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Definition 3.1 (partial NSP): A2 has full column rank, and the NSP inequality holds over v1 with A1v1 ∈ R(A2) (p. 2).
  - Definition 3.2 (partial RIP): the RIP constant of PA1, where P = I − A2(A2^TA2)⁻¹A2^T projects onto R(A2)⊥. The projection reduces the problem to classical compressed sensing on PA1, followed by solving A2x2 = y − A1x1 (p. 3).
  - Theorems 4.1–4.2: NSP ⇒ partial NSP, and RIP ⇒ partial RIP with the same constant (p. 3).
  - Theorem 5.2: noisy recovery bounds (12)–(13) (p. 4).
  - Theorem 6.1: Gaussian matrices satisfy partial RIP when k exceeds a bound of order (s − r) log((N−r)e/(s−r)) + s log(12/δ) (16) (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - A2 has full column rank (pp. 2–3).
  - Bounded noise, ‖ε‖₂ ≤ η (p. 4).
  - Gaussian entries with variance 1/k (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Three devices.
  - **Projection** that eliminates the dense block (p. 3).
  - **Adapted NSP proof**: Rauhut's proof "with appropriate modifications" (p. 2).
  - **Union bound** over the C(N−r, s−r) sparsity patterns, with the Baraniuk et al. concentration estimate (p. 5).

  For the noisy case, the proof shows that problems (11) and (14) have the same x1-parts by mapping feasible points both ways (p. 4). The bounds on C1 and C2 are given without proof (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is analogues of NSP and RIP that are implied by the classical ones, plus a measurement count that can be an order smaller: if s − r = O(1), then k = O(s + log(N − r)) instead of O(s log(N/s)) (p. 5). The concluding remarks restate this and give no limitations or future work (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, outside DFO (p. 3): the classical framework (ℓ1 recovery, NSP/RIP) is kept and only the object is changed, from A to PA1. The proof then carries over.
- new-pattern candidate: *import a neighbouring field's certified tool through a specialist coauthor, driven by a DFO need* (p. 1; also S064 p. 4 and S087 p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Reduce a structured variant to the classical problem by projecting out the unconstrained block (P = I − A2A2†). Every classical guarantee then applies to PA1 with the same constant (p. 3).
- When you introduce new sufficient conditions, first prove that the classical conditions imply them, so no one loses. Then show where they are strictly cheaper, here in the number of measurements (pp. 3, 5).

**Quotes**:
- "We show that it is possible to guarantee recovery of a partially sparse signal using Gaussian random matrices with the number of measurements an order smaller than the one necessary for general recovery." (p. 1)
- "Note that the first step is now reduced to the classical setting of Compressed Sensing." (p. 3)

### S075 · Modelling nearby FGK population I stars: A new form of estimating stellar parameters using an optimization approach (Astronomy & Astrophysics 2011, DOI 10.1051/0004-6361/200811182)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. M. Fernandes (astronomy, Coimbra) and A. I. F. Vaz (Minho) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an application of PSwarm (S005/S018) in astrophysics. The file is the "Preprint online version: June 16, 2011", and its title spells Modeling with one l (p. 1). **Read**: full (pp. 1–10).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is the application. A star has six unknowns (M, age, X, Y, mixing length α, overshooting ov) but only four observables (Teff, L, Z/X, g) (pp. 1–3). Standard practice fixes α, ΔY/ΔZ and ov at solar-scaled or ad hoc values (pp. 1–2). The question is whether these parameters are universal (p. 2). The goal is to estimate all six at once by global derivative-free optimization (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Formulation**: minimize a sum of four squared, error-normalized misfits under bounds (1)–(2). Each evaluation runs the CESAM stellar-evolution code (pp. 2–3).
  - **Simulator failures**: a failure is treated like an infeasible point, value +∞ (p. 3).
  - **Solver**: PSwarm, with 42 particles, 2000 evaluations and ε = 10⁻⁵ (p. 3).
  - **Non-unique minimizers**: 25 runs per star, averaged. Twenty runs were enough for the average to stabilize (p. 5).
  - **Real-star filtering**: discard runs with f > 1 or with a variable at a bound; drop stars with fewer than 5 valid runs; fix ov and tie Z to [Fe/H] (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The physics choices are stated and justified: CESAM v3, OPAL opacities, the EFF equation of state (chosen for computational cost), and no rotation or diffusion (p. 2). The bounds are in Table 1 (p. 4). The optimization theory relies on PSwarm's convergence to stationary points, which is cited, not reproved (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none. The only convergence statement is the cited PSwarm result (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**: A validation ladder (pp. 4–9):
  1. The Sun (p. 4).
  2. Five fictitious stars with known parameters, each run 25 times (pp. 4–6).
  3. 196 real stars, reduced to 115 after filtering (p. 6).
  4. Comparison with literature values: masses from Torres et al., ages from Saffe/Holmberg, helium from Casagrande (pp. 6–8).

  The runs used a 130-node cluster (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contributions**: a methodology plus an online tool (pp. 2, 9), and astrophysical findings: planet hosts have similar helium, and mass and mixing length show no correlation (p. 9).
  - **Limits, stated concretely**: a non-zero overshooting for the Sun "without astrophysical meaning" (p. 4, p. 6); the Sun's age about 400 Myr low (p. 4); overshooting too poorly determined, so it is fixed for the real stars (p. 6).
  - **Future work**: a forthcoming paper on the chemistry of the solar neighbourhood and on planet hosts (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence of use, not design (p. 3): the PSwarm hybrid (swarm in the search step, poll for convergence) is the solver of choice for an expensive simulation.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 9): a public online tool for the application community; Taste #6 (anchored in an application) ✅.
- new-pattern candidate: *planted-truth validation ladder*: first a calibrated reference case, then synthetic instances with known answers, then real data compared with the literature (pp. 4–8). The same ladder appears in S087 (pp. 8–17).
- Method 2: no link.

**Transferable techniques**:
- For an inverse problem that is not identifiable, run the global solver many times (25), stop adding runs when the average stabilizes (after about 20), and report the averages with their spread (p. 5, pp. 8–9).
- Treat simulator crashes and physically invalid outputs exactly like infeasible points (+∞, the extreme barrier) (p. 3).
- Discard solutions that sit on a bound, because they would move if the bound were removed, and solutions whose misfit exceeds the error box (f > 1) (p. 6).

**Quotes**:

### S087 · Estimation of risk-neutral density surfaces (Computational Management Science 2011, DOI 10.1007/s10287-010-0126-3)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. M. Monteiro (Faculty of Economics, Coimbra) and R. H. Tütüncü (Goldman Sachs Asset Management; SDPT3 co-author, ref. [35]) (pp. 1, 21) · **Position in trajectory**: a finance application; it extends the single-maturity paper [33] (EJOR 2007; card S028) from a curve to a surface. **Read**: full text (pp. 1–21); the figure pages (22–34) were read as captions only.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Infer risk-neutral densities for *several* maturities at once, from option prices (p. 1). The paper positions itself against two alternatives:
  - Parametric fitting, whose least-squares problems are usually nonconvex (p. 2).
  - The closest work, Coleman–Verma–Li, which uses bicubic splines for the implied-volatility surface. Here the density surface is inverted directly (p. 2).

  The resulting problem is convex (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Representation**: a bicubic spline density p_y(ω, u) with 16 ns nt coefficients. The spline continuity conditions are linear, and the density integrates to 1 at the temporal knots (pp. 4–5).
  - **Objective**: option prices are linear in y, so the least-squares misfit E(y) is quadratic (pp. 5–6).
  - **Nonnegativity, two options**: (i) a QP that imposes it at the knots only; this is a heuristic, and small negative values between knots were observed (p. 6). (ii) An SDP that uses the Bertsimas–Popescu characterization of nonnegative polynomials on intervals along every knot line; the objective is moved into an SOC epigraph t ≥ E(y) so SDPT3 can solve it (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Nonnegativity is certified along the knot lines only. Inside the rectangles it was "never an issue" empirically (p. 16).
  - The data must be arbitrage-free. Violating prices are removed by monotonicity and convexity checks in the strike, after put–call parity (p. 15).
  - The method does not price path-dependent options (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no theorems. The modelling devices are:
  - prices linear in the spline coefficients (p. 6);
  - scaling by the average knot, against the ill-conditioning caused by ω⁵ terms (pp. 6, 8);
  - a Hessian shift ξ = |λmin| to remove spurious negative eigenvalues (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Synthetic data with known densities**: Black–Scholes, absolute diffusion, and Heston, the last with out-of-sample maturities (pp. 8–13).
  - **Market data**: S&P500 1995 (compared with [12]) and S&P500 2006 (pp. 13–15).
  - **Pricing**: CBOE binary options, July 2008, against Black–Scholes implied prices (pp. 16–18).
  - **Metrics**: residual, absolute and relative errors with a price floor of 0.01 (11), at-the-money errors, and CPU time for QP vs SDP (pp. 9, 16).
  - **Robustness**: checked against the number of knots (pp. 10, 12).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contributions**: modelling and computation (a convex SDP that certifies nonnegativity) and an application (binary prices closer to the market than Black–Scholes) (pp. 17–18).
  - **Limits**: the fit to the 2006 data is weaker, attributed to a smaller set with irregular strikes (p. 18); for the comparison with [12] the authors say it "is difficult to establish rigorous comparisons" without knowing how the error in [12] was computed (p. 14).
  - No future directions are given (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, outside DFO (pp. 3–7): the formulation of [33] (splines, least squares, QP vs SDP nonnegativity) is kept, and only the object changes, from one density to a surface.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 8–18): a stated set of data sets with known truth plus two market sets, and baselines (Black–Scholes, [12]). There are no profiles and, in the text read, no code release.
- Taste #6 ✅ (pp. 16–18): the pay-off is a market application.
- new-pattern candidate: *planted-truth validation ladder* (synthetic models with known densities, then market data, then out-of-sample instruments) (pp. 8–17); see S075.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Offer a cheap heuristic constraint (nonnegativity at the knots, QP) and a certified one (a polynomial nonnegativity certificate, SDP), and report both the accuracy and the CPU cost, so the reader can choose (pp. 6–7, 16).
- Put a floor (0.01) under prices in the relative-error metric, so that very cheap far-out-of-the-money options do not dominate it, and report at-the-money errors separately (p. 9).
- Remove arbitrage from market data before fitting (monotonicity and convexity in the strike): no nonnegative density fits prices that contain arbitrage (p. 15).
- Validate out of sample by fitting some maturities and predicting prices at held-out maturities and strikes (pp. 13–14).

**Quotes**:
- "Semidefinite programming is employed to guarantee the nonnegativity of the densities." (p. 1)
- "The SDP approach is computationally more expensive but not as much as one would think" (p. 16)

### S047 · Bilevel derivative-free optimization and its application to robust optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2012, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2010.547579)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn (IBM T. J. Watson) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: joins the bilevel line (S002, S022) and the model-based DFO line (the trust-region code of S029, ref. [1]). The file is the 15 Sept. 2010 preprint (p. 1). **Read**: full (pp. 1–20); the figure pages (21–24) were read as captions only.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Bilevel problems in which *both* levels are derivative-free (p. 1). The authors work with the reduced formulation (2), not the all-at-once formulation (3), because without convexity the lower level cannot be replaced by its first-order conditions (pp. 2–3). They restrict themselves to the unconstrained case, arguing that "a good understanding of the unconstrained case seems to be necessary" (p. 3). The motivating application is robust optimization of simulation-based functions, where min–max is rewritten as a bilevel problem (pp. 1, 16–17).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Both levels run the same practical interpolation trust-region method with minimum-Frobenius-norm models (Algorithm 5.1, from [1]) (pp. 10–12). Three expedients exploit the bilevel structure:
  1. **Inexact lower-level solve**: keep ‖∇ℓ mℓ‖ = O((∆u)²) and ∆ℓ = O(∆u), so the upper-level model stays fully linear (p. 8). A dynamic-accuracy variant ties the tolerance to min(‖su‖², ‖su‖‖gu‖) (p. 9).
  2. **Reuse of lower-level points** evaluated at perturbed upper-level variables, when ‖xu − xu_pert‖ = O((∆ℓ)³) (p. 9).
  3. **Lower-level starting points** from a linear surrogate xℓ_m(xu) (p. 12).

  Concrete rules are (10)–(11), with constants 10⁻² (p. 14). Linear constraints are handled with ℓ∞ trust regions, which turn the subproblems into QPs (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The lower-level solution set is a singleton (p. 2).
  - The reduced upper-level function is continuously differentiable (p. 3).
  - Near the solution, ∇²ℓℓ fℓ is nonsingular, as the inverse function theorem needs (p. 8).
  - fu is Lipschitz in the lower-level variables (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The authors add no new convergence theory ("no need to add further analysis", p. 3). The conditions come from chaining three bounds:
  - interpolation error bounds for perturbed function values: an error O(∆³) keeps a model fully quadratic (Thm 4.1), and an error O(∆²) keeps it fully linear (Thm 4.2) (pp. 6–7);
  - the inverse-function bound ‖xℓ − xℓ_dfo‖ ≤ c‖∇ℓ fℓ‖;
  - Lipschitz continuity of fu (p. 8).

  They also use the inexact-function-value device of Conn–Gould–Toint §10.6: if the errors are ≤ η0′(m(x) − m(x+s)), then ρ ≥ η0 − 2η0′ (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two synthetic bilevel problems (nu = nℓ = 5 and 20), the robust-optimization example of Bertsimas–Nohadani–Teo from starting points A and B, and an 8-asset Omega-function portfolio. The plots show the reduced upper-level function against the number of lower-level evaluations for three versions: basic, inexact, and inexact plus reuse (pp. 16–18; figure captions pp. 21–24). There is no comparison with other methods (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: a "first contribution" to derivative-free bilevel optimization (p. 18).
  - **Findings**: the inexact lower-level solve is promising; point reuse is less robust and recommended only when the lower level is expensive (pp. 18–19).
  - **Limits**:
    - The total number of lower-level evaluations "can still be considered high" (p. 19).
    - In robust optimization, a convex upper level produces a concave lower-level minimization, though the real difficulty seems to be the nonconvexity of the reduced function (p. 19).
    - Upper-level constraints on lower-level variables are excluded (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 3, 12–14): the derivative-free trust-region skeleton is kept unchanged. Only the evaluation of the reduced function changes, into an inexact inner solve, and the convergence theory is reused as is.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant, a deterministic precursor (pp. 5–9, 14): the accuracy of the inner solve is tied to the outer trust radius and to the predicted decrease, not to a probability.
- Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence (pp. 9, 14): lower-level evaluations already paid for, at slightly different upper-level variables, are reused.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 14, 19): a "relatively sophisticated Matlab implementation" is described, but no release is stated, there is no collection, and there are no baselines because none exist.
- new-pattern candidate: *deterministic inexactness tied to the step*; compare S085 (pp. 4–5) and S077 (p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive inner-solve tolerances by chaining error bounds so that the outer model keeps its accuracy class. Here an inner gradient tolerance O((∆u)²) and an inner radius O(∆u) keep the outer model fully linear (p. 8). Implement the tolerance as max(min(10⁻²(∆u)², 10⁻²∆u‖gu‖, 10⁻²), 10⁻⁵) (p. 14).
- Reuse points evaluated at perturbed parameters when the perturbation is below O(∆³), the level that keeps quadratic models fully quadratic (pp. 9, 14).
- Plot progress against evaluations of the expensive level, with one curve per expedient switched on (basic / inexact / inexact plus reuse). This is an ablation in a single figure (p. 21).
- If no competing method exists, say so explicitly rather than invent a straw baseline (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "An astute reader will notice that there are no comparisons with competing methods — this is because such methods appear not to exist." (p. 19)
- "However it does not appear to be as robust as the previous idea, and we recommend it only when the lower level function evaluations are relatively expensive." (p. 19)

### S064 · Optimizing radial basis functions by DC programming and its use in direct search for global derivative-free optimization (TOP 2012, DOI 10.1007/s11750-011-0193-9)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Le Thi Hoai An (Metz; developer of DCA with Pham Dinh Tao, p. 4) and A. I. F. Vaz (Minho) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a third search-step hybrid, after PSwarm (S005/S018) and the minimum-Frobenius-norm-model search step (S012). The file is the 8 March 2011 preprint (p. 1). **Read**: all text pages (pp. 1–13, 22–23, 34). The figure pages (14–21, 24–33) were read as captions only, and the plotted curves were not interpreted beyond what the text states.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Global derivative-free optimization under bound and linear constraints (p. 1). For direct search, the ability to find global minima "depends on" what is put in the search step. The earlier hybrids are particle swarm (Vaz–Vicente) and DIRECT (Griffin–Kolda) (p. 2). RBF models are known to be useful for global optimization (Regis–Shoemaker and others) (p. 2). The entry point is therefore RBF model minimization in the search step, with the subproblem solved by d.c. programming (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 5.1 (p. 8) has three parts.
  - **Search step**: minimize a cubic RBF model with a linear tail over Ω ∩ an ℓ∞ trust region of radius σkαk, so bounds intersected with the trust region still form a box. Accept on simple decrease.
  - **Poll step**: D = {±eᵢ, ±e}, optionally ordered by the RBF model values.
  - **Step-size update**: keep α on success, or double it after two consecutive poll successes along the same direction; halve it on failure.

  For linear constraints, the constraints are dropped from the subproblem and the point is pulled back along the segment towards xk (p. 8). There are two d.c. decompositions:
  - **DCA1** splits the terms by the sign of λ (p. 6).
  - **DCA2** uses g = ρ/2‖x‖² + t and h = ρ/2‖x‖² − (m − t). Each iteration is a projection, x_{k+1} = P(∇h(x_k)/ρ), with the a priori bound ρ = 12 np ‖λ‖∞ max{‖ℓ̄‖, ‖ū‖} (p. 7). DCA2b starts ρ small and doubles it on non-decrease (p. 13).

  Models are built from a cache: nmin = n+2 and nmax = 5n+1 points, 80% nearest to the iterate and 20% farthest (p. 22).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: No theorem is proved. The paper states that, for global convergence, the search step must terminate finitely and "yield a point in the underlying integer lattice" (p. 7). How the RBF minimizer x̄k is placed on the lattice is not stated in the algorithm box (p. 8) or elsewhere in the pages read. The DCA properties it relies on (descent, critical limit points, linear convergence) are quoted from [2–4] (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No new convergence analysis. The only derivation is the ρ bound, obtained from the Hessian formula for RBF models (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Test set**: 107 bound-constrained and 92 linearly constrained AMPL problems (pp. 9–11).
  - **Profiles**: performance profiles (a modified Dolan–Moré) and data profiles (Moré–Wild) at ε = 10⁻¹ and 10⁻⁵, normalized by n+1 (pp. 9, 12). Results are averaged over 10 runs (p. 13).
  - **Subproblem solvers**: DCA against fmincon (Section 7, pp. 12–13).
  - **Full method**: variants with and without poll ordering ("Sort"), against Pattern (empty search step) and PSwarm, with a budget of 1000 evaluations. A model-success-rate profile is included (pp. 22–23).
  - **Results**: DCA2b leads in the data profiles; there are no major differences in final objective values (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution**: an algorithmic hybrid plus a numerical study. RBF models improve efficiency as long as "relatively more than n + 1 points" are used (pp. 2, 34).
  - **Implementation**: inside the PSwarm solver, where a cache of evaluations is "extremely important" (p. 22).
  - No limitations or future work are stated (pp. 23, 34).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 7–8): a model-based global step in the search, with convergence left to the poll and step-size mechanism.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 7–8): acceptance is by simple decrease with an integer-lattice requirement, not the sufficient-decrease forcing function of Method 1, step 3.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 9–13, 22–23): a stated collection (with a URL, p. 9), performance and data profiles, and baselines that include the empty-search version and the previous heuristic (PSwarm).
- Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence (pp. 8, 22): cached evaluations are reused to build the model and to order the poll.
- new-pattern candidate: *import a neighbouring field's certified tool through a specialist coauthor* (p. 4); see S051 and S087.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use an ℓ∞ trust region for the search subproblem, with radius proportional to the direct-search step size, so that bounds ∩ trust region stays a box and any box solver applies (pp. 2, 8).
- Choose a d.c. decomposition whose DCA step is a closed-form projection (DCA2), with an a priori computable ρ. Then make ρ adaptive: start small and double it on non-decrease (pp. 7, 13).
- Build the interpolation set from a cache with 80% nearest points and 20% far points, to improve its geometry (p. 22).
- Report a model-success-rate profile: models built divided by models that led to a successful iteration (p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "Our main conclusion is that a direct-search method with an RBF model minimization in the search step offers a good compromise between global optimization and computational effort (i.e., number of function evaluations) as long as relatively more than n + 1 points are used in the corresponding sampling process." (p. 2)
- "The cache proved not to be useful in the particle swarm search step, as the number of hits in the cache was very low." (p. 22)

### Batch synthesis (s2-03)

1. **Keeping the skeleton and changing one object holds outside DFO** (Method 4). The same move appears in:
   - interior-point filter methods (S077 pp. 14–15);
   - interior-point local theory (S085 p. 2);
   - compressed sensing (S051 p. 3);
   - a finance spline model (S087 pp. 3–7).

   In S077, S085 and S047 the proof is explicitly borrowed or cited lemma by lemma (S077 pp. 18–21; S085 pp. 9–10; S047 p. 3).
2. **Inexactness is budgeted deterministically and tied to the step before 2014.** Three cases:
   - an inner-solve tolerance of O((∆u)²) (S047 p. 8);
   - a root residual that shrinks with ‖∆wk‖ (S085 pp. 4–5);
   - a Hessian error along ∇ℓ of order ∆^{q−1} (S077 p. 6).

   This is the deterministic precursor that Method 3 later makes probabilistic.
3. **Assumptions are cut down to what the proof touches.** Concavity becomes an angle condition (S085 p. 10), and the exact Hessian becomes its action on one vector (S077 p. 6). Two papers in this batch; a third instance is needed before this pattern can be promoted.
4. **Application papers validate on a ladder with known truth.** S075 goes from the Sun to fictitious stars to real stars (pp. 4–8). S087 goes from Black–Scholes, absolute-diffusion and Heston data to S&P500 data to binary options (pp. 8–18). Neither reports complexity or profiles; Method 2 has no counterpart there.
5. **No worst-case evaluation counts anywhere in the batch** (2008–2012). Every convergence result is asymptotic (S083 pp. 5–6; S077 p. 17) or local-rate (S085 pp. 9–10), consistent with the era effect noted under Method 2.
