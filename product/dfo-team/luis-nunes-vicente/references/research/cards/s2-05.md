# Paper cards · batch s2-05 (supplement)

Batch intent: Look at eight 2016–2022 papers from two groups:
- **Toulouse line with Gratton and Royer** (four papers). It extends the first-order worst-case analysis and the probabilistic-descent analysis in three directions: second-order direct search (S074), a decoupled second-order trust-region method (S060), and linearly constrained probabilistic direct search (S045). It also turns complexity bounds into a stopping signal (S104).
- **Lehigh-era papers** (four papers): a derivative-free trust-region method for nonsmooth black boxes (S058), two fairness-in-ML papers with his PhD student S. Liu (S094, S065), and a 3D-printing application (S078).

Three questions:
- (a) When the first-order complexity machinery of Method 2 is pushed to second order and to constraints, which proof devices carry over and what does the extension cost in n and ε?

- (c) In the fairness and application papers, what takes the place of theorems and collection profiles (Methods 2 and 5)?

Focus dimensions: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line), D8 — the supplement short card. D6 and D7 appear only in the digest, one line each.

### S074 · A second-order globally convergent direct-search method and its worst-case complexity (Optimization 2016, DOI 10.1080/02331934.2015.1124271)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Gratton (ENSEEIHT/IRIT) and C. W. Royer, then a Toulouse doctoral student (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: second-order sequel to Vicente's first-order worst-case analysis [36] (S023). The follow-up S060 cites it as [18]. **Read**: full (pp. 1–25; references skimmed).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Second-order results for direct search existed only as statements at limit points along refining directions: pattern search along a basis that appears infinitely often, MADS with dense refining directions, and GSS with curvature information (p. 2). The authors want a proof scheme "at the iteration level", analogous to the cosine measure for first order (pp. 2–3). They also want second-order complexity bounds, which existed only for derivative-based methods [9, 10] (p. 3). Three goals are announced in order (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Weak second order**: the Rayleigh measure rm(D, A) = min_{d∈D} dᵀAd (Def. 2.1, p. 6). Any sufficient-decrease direct search whose poll sets have a nonempty symmetric part V_k, and whose forcing function is o(α²), satisfies lim inf max{‖∇f(x_k)‖, −rm(V_k, ∇²f(x_k))} = 0 (Thm 2.1, p. 7).
  - **Strong second order, AHDS** (Alg. 3.1, pp. 10–11). The algorithm polls D_k, then −D_k, then d_i + d_j for a basis B_k ⊂ D_k. From the values already computed it builds H_k by central and mixed second differences, (3.4)–(3.5), and then polls ±v_k, the eigenvector for λ_min(H_k).
  - **Stated design rules** (p. 10): curvature search only after first-order polling fails, and O(n²) evaluations per iteration, in line with methods that build Hessian approximations.
  - **Kept**: the sufficient-decrease acceptance and the step-size update of the basic method (Alg. 2.1, p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f is C² with Lipschitz gradient (ν_g) and Hessian (ν_H), and bounded below on the level set (Asm. 2.1–2.2, p. 4).
  - ρ is nondecreasing with ρ(α) = o(α²) (Asm. 2.3, p. 4).
  - The poll sets are unit-vector PSSs with a nonempty symmetric part and cm(D_k) ≥ κ_g (Asm. 2.4, p. 5).
  - σ_min(B_k)² ≥ σ (Asm. 3.1, p. 13); σ = 1 for orthonormal bases.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **Key lemma** (Lemma 2.1, pp. 5–6): at an unsuccessful iteration, the two failed sufficient-decrease inequalities along d and −d are added. The sum is a second difference, which gives dᵀ∇²f(x_k)d ≥ −(2ρ(α_k)/α_k² + ν_H α_k/3).
  - **Proposition 3.1** (pp. 11–12): the entrywise error of H_k is at most (5ν_H/3)α_k (3.7). This passes to a Frobenius bound and then to an eigenvalue-perturbation bound, giving v_kᵀ∇²f v_k ≤ σ_min(B_k)²λ_k + (10ν_H n/3)α_k.
  - **Complexity** (§4) uses ρ(t) = c t^p/6 with p > 2:
    - Lower-bound α at unsuccessful iterations, and pass the bound to each successful iteration through the preceding unsuccessful one (Thm 4.1, pp. 14–15).
    - Count unsuccessful iterations from α_{l1} ≤ α_{k0} γ^{|S|} θ^{|U|} (Thm 4.2, pp. 15–16).
    - p = 3 gives the best power (p. 16).
  - **Result with D⊕**: O(n³ max{ε_g⁻³, ε_H⁻³}) iterations and O(n⁵ max{ε_g⁻³, ε_H⁻³}) evaluations (Cor. 4.1, p. 17). The weak criterion gives O(n^{3/2}) iterations and O(n^{5/2}) evaluations (p. 18).
  - **Tightness**: on f₁(x, y) = (9x − y)(11x − y) + x⁴/2 started at the origin with D⊕, the basic method cannot leave the saddle point (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**: 60 CUTEst problems flagged for negative curvature by Avelino et al.; 4 poll-set types × 10 orderings or rotations; budget 2000n evaluations; profiles on mean evaluations (pp. 18–19).
  - Results: the basic method is more efficient and AHDS more robust (p. 19). A stripped "sds" variant (Steps 3–4 removed) is generally the most efficient on non-symmetric sets (pp. 20–21). Only about 10% of AHDS successes come from the eigenvector step (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Claimed as the first second-order complexity bounds for a derivative-free method (p. 17).
  - The bound matches ARC and trust regions in ε_H when ε_H ≤ ε_g, but in ε_g it has ε_g⁻³ instead of ε_g⁻²ε_H⁻¹. The authors trace this to a single decrease formula in α_k³ (pp. 17–18).
  - Sharpness "remains to be proved" (p. 17). The n⁵ factor is admitted (p. 17, p. 24).
  - Future work: a probabilistic version, multilevel methods, curvilinear searches (p. 25). No code release was found in the text.

**Method links**:
- ✅ **Method 2 (evidence)**: bounds in both iterations and evaluations with n-dependence, placed next to the ARC and trust-region bounds, with the order gap explained and sharpness left open (pp. 16–18).
- ⚠ **Method 4 (variant)**: the skeleton and the notion of success are kept. What changes is the order of the forcing function (o(α) to o(α²)) and a staged poll set (pp. 4, 10).
- **Method 3**: appears only as future work (p. 25).
- ⚠ **Method 5 (partial)**: a problem subset with profiles and an ablation, but no solver release.
- **New-pattern candidates**:
  - *Iteration-level measure first*: pp. 6–8.
  - *Lazy escalation* (pay for curvature only after the cheap poll fails): p. 10.
  - *Mechanism-isolating control variant*: pp. 20–21.
  - *Trace an exponent gap to one decrease inequality*: pp. 17–18, cured in S060.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Add the failed-decrease inequalities at d and −d. This gives a curvature bound from an unsuccessful iteration at no extra cost (Lemma 2.1, pp. 5–6).
- Build the Hessian estimate only from points the poll has already evaluated. Bound the eigenvector error along the chain entrywise → Frobenius → eigenvalue perturbation (pp. 10–12).
- To count successful iterations, pass the step-size lower bound from the most recent unsuccessful iteration to each successful one (α_k ≥ θα_{l(k)}) (p. 15).
- Profile a stripped variant (here, symmetrized polling only) to test your own explanation of where the gain comes from (pp. 20–21).

**Quotes**:
- "We are interested in an other proof scheme that reasons at the iteration level." (p. 2)
- "The dependence on the problem dimension is however worsened compared to the first-order case." (p. 24)

### S104 · An indicator for the switch from derivative-free to derivative-based optimization (Oper. Res. Lett. 45(4) 2017, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2017.05.003)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Gratton (ENSEEIHT) and N. Soualmi (CERFACS) (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: turns the decrease inequalities from Vicente's complexity papers ([10] S023, [2] S046, [3] S032) into a run-time signal. The motivating application is acoustic full-waveform inversion (p. 1). **Read**: full (pp. 1–17).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: in some applications the gradient can be computed, but at a higher cost than f (p. 1). When to switch from a derivative-free to a derivative-based method is presented as never addressed (p. 2). The paper offers "a first contribution" in a smooth unconstrained setting where ∇f is L-Lipschitz (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Source inequalities** (p. 4): the per-iteration decrease lower bounds from complexity proofs. For a gradient method, the decrease is at least (G/L)‖∇f‖² (5). For a DFO method, a successful iteration decreases f by at least D₁t², and at a step reduction ‖∇f‖ ≤ D₂(n)L t (6).
  - **Indicators**: the ratio of (8) to (7), taken over the successful iterations since the last step reduction, gives I¹ = Σt_j²/Σ‖g_j‖² and I² = |C_k|‖g_{r_k}‖²/Σ‖g_j‖² (9) (p. 4). Both should increase, because the gradient goes to zero faster than the step size (p. 5).
  - **Practical rule** (Algs. 3.1–3.4, pp. 5–7): switch when I_k/I_scaling ≥ C_threshold, with a window E. A hybrid version falls back from I² to I¹ after E consecutive successes.
  - **Gradients g_j** (pp. 7–8): simplex gradients by regression on points already evaluated within 5α_k, computed by QR with diagonal regularization at 10⁻⁸.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: direct search with ρ(α) = α²/2 and a PSS (Alg. 1.1, p. 2); gradient method with backtracking (Alg. 1.2, p. 3). No theorem about the switch rule itself was found.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: a derivation only, (7)–(8) (p. 4). Monotonicity of the indicators is argued informally. The constant D₂(n)² and the unknown L are dropped (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: 20 CUTEr problems with n = 20; D⊕ with cyclic polling; E = 20, E_scaling = 5, C_threshold = 2; budget 500 evaluations (3000 for penalty1) (pp. 7–8).
  - A switch occurred on 15 of the 20 problems. On 4 of the 5 without a switch, direct search did beat the gradient method, and integreq is "the single failure" (p. 8).
  - With a gradient costed at 5 function evaluations (AD) or at n = 20 (finite differences), the switched method is cheaper on 8 of the 10 crossing problems (p. 8; Table 2, p. 12).
  - The pure I¹ indicator loses predictability (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - A family of indicators, called "of heuristic type" by the authors (p. 13). C_threshold is "more critical and problem dependant" (p. 16).
  - Stated scope: derivative-free trust regions (which iterations count in C_k) and any gradient method whose decrease is bounded in terms of ‖∇f‖ (p. 16).
  - A parameter called "Ethreshold" in the final remarks is named E_scaling in the algorithms (pp. 6, 16).

**Method links**:
- ⚠ **Method 2 (variant)**: the decrease inequalities of complexity proofs are used as online diagnostics, not as cost bounds (pp. 2, 4).
- ✅ **Heuristic 7 (reuse evaluations)**: simplex gradients built from the evaluation history (pp. 7–8).
- ⚠ **Method 5 (partial)**: a problem set with per-problem plots and a named failure (p. 8), but no profiles and no code (not found).
- **New-pattern candidate**: *complexity inequalities as run-time switches* (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Take the per-iteration decrease lower bounds from two methods' complexity proofs and form their ratio. Monitor it, normalized by its early-run value, to decide when to change method (pp. 4–5).
- Report effort with gradients costed both at 5 function evaluations (AD) and at n (finite differences) (p. 8).
- State the properties the experiment is meant to check, and what it is not trying to show (a better hybrid method) (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "a question that to our knowledge has never been addressed is when should one switch from a derivative-free method to a derivative-based one, when the gradient can be computed (or possibly approximated by finite differences)." (p. 2)
- "Our approach is certainly also of heuristic type as we compare lower bounds on the decreases attained by the derivative-free method" (p. 13)

### S045 · Direct search based on probabilistic feasible descent for bound and linearly constrained problems (Comput. Optim. Appl. 2019, DOI 10.1007/s10589-019-00062-4)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Gratton (Toulouse), C. W. Royer (Wisconsin–Madison) and Z. Zhang (Hong Kong PolyU) (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: the constrained sequel of probabilistic descent [19] (S019). On the deterministic side it rests on Kolda–Lewis–Torczon [22, 23, 27] and Lewis–Shepherd–Torczon [25]. **Read**: partial. Read: pp. 1–25, including Appendix A. Not read: Appendix B beyond its first step, and the Appendix C tables.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: deterministic direct search for linear constraints polls positive generators of approximate tangent cones. In the unconstrained case, random polling had given better complexity and better numerics, so random generation "also in the presence of constraints" is the natural next step (abstract, p. 1). A complexity bound for deterministic direct search under linear constraints is also noted as "left open in the literature until now" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Kept**: the unconstrained algorithm of [19] (sufficient decrease, step update), plus feasibility of every iterate (Alg. 2.1, pp. 4–5).
  - **Geometry**: work in the null space of A through an orthonormal basis W, and use tangent cones of constraints within distance α (2.5)–(2.6) (pp. 5–6).
  - **Replaced**: positive generators give way to a *κ-feasible descent set*. The cosine with −∇f is normalized by ‖P_{WT(x,α)}[−∇f]‖ (Def. 3.1, p. 7), with a probabilistic version in Def. 5.1 (p. 12).
  - **Random generators**: (i) a random subset of the cone generators (§6); (ii) a subspace split T_k = S_k ⊕ T_k^c, with r_s = ⌈log₂(1 − ln θ/ln γ)⌉ + 1 random unit directions in S_k plus a random fraction p_c of the generators of T_k^c (Prop. 7.1, p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Normalized directions (Asm. 2.1, p. 4). ρ with ρ(θα) ≤ θ̄ρ(α) and ρ(γα) ≤ γ̄ρ(α) (Asm. 2.2, p. 5).
  - f bounded below on F, Lipschitz gradient, and a bounded gradient on F; the last assumption is the one that constraints add (Asm. 3.1–3.3, p. 8).
  - The probabilistic results need γ > 1 (p. 13). A feasible x₀ is given (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **Lemma 3.2** (p. 9): an unsuccessful iteration with a κ-feasible descent set bounds the projected reduced gradient by κ⁻¹(να/2 + ρ(α)/α).
  - **Lemma 3.4** (pp. 9–10): a Moreau decomposition splits χ(x_k) into tangent and normal parts. The normal part is bounded by α/η_min times B_g, using the polyhedral Lemma 3.3 from [23].
  - **Deterministic complexity**: the proofs of [39] are a "verbatim copy" once ‖g_k‖ is replaced by χ(x_k) (Thm 4.1, pp. 10–11).
  - **Probabilistic results**: the threshold is p₀ = ln θ/ln(γ⁻¹θ) (5.6). The sum Σ[Z_l ln γ + (1 − Z_l) ln θ] is a submartingale with bounded increments, so convergence holds almost surely (p. 13; Thm 5.1, p. 14). A Chernoff-type bound gives O(ε⁻²) iterations with probability at least 1 − exp(−O(ε⁻²)) (Thm 6.1, p. 14).
  - **Dimension dependence**:
    - Bounds only, random subset of D⊕: still O(n²ε⁻²) (p. 15). With subspace directions: O(n_b n ε⁻²) (p. 17).
    - Equalities only: O((n − m)ε⁻²) instead of O((n − m)²ε⁻²) (pp. 15, 17).
    - In the degenerate case the direction count may grow exponentially in n (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: MATLAB variants dspfd-0/1/2, where dspfd-0 polls all generators in random order; ρ = 10⁻⁴α², γ = 2, θ = 1/2 (so p₀ = 1/2); budget 2000n evaluations; mean of 10 runs; profiles on normalized evaluations (pp. 17–18). Test sets: 63 + 31 bound-constrained and 106 linearly constrained CUTEst problems (pp. 18–20).
  - Against MATLAB patternsearch, dspfd-2 is the best variant (pp. 18–20).
  - Against NOMAD 3.9.1 (models and search off) and PSwarm 2.1 (search on), PSwarm is the most efficient (pp. 20–22). NOMAD stalls on linear constraints and hit memory limits on 7 problems; PSwarm's ellipsoid initialization failed on 7 (p. 22). dspfd-2 is competitive on the 48 problems with inequalities (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Almost-sure convergence and ε⁻² complexity with overwhelming probability. The dimension improves only for two subclasses (bounds only, equalities only) (p. 24).
  - Global convergence in the deterministic case is admitted to be the result of [23] (p. 8).
  - Future: nonlinear constraints via augmented Lagrangian, linearization, barrier or merit function (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- ✅ **Method 3 (evidence)**: the threshold p₀ is derived from θ and γ, and it becomes a concrete direction count r_s (7.1) (pp. 13, 16). This is Step 3 of the method verbatim.
- ✅ **Method 4 (evidence)**: the skeleton is unchanged. The one object redefined is the cosine measure, now relative to the approximate tangent cone, with χ as the criticality measure (pp. 7–11).
- ✅ **Method 2**: evaluation bounds with n, m and m_I dependence, plus a pointer that the (n − m)² factor cannot be improved [12] (pp. 11–12).
- ✅ **Method 5**: profiles against three solvers, with losses reported (p. 22). No code release was found.
- **New-pattern candidates**:
  - *Prove the deterministic theorem under the weakest per-iteration property the random version can meet*: "This relaxation will be instrumental", p. 8.
  - *Mechanism-isolating control variant* (dspfd-0): p. 17.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before randomizing, restate the deterministic proof so that it uses only a per-iteration property (a feasible descent set) instead of a structural one (positive generators). The probabilistic proof can then reuse each lemma with "holds with probability p" (pp. 8–9, 12–13).
- Normalize the cosine measure by the projected gradient, not by the full gradient. This keeps it bounded away from zero near active constraints (3.1) (p. 7).
- Handle equalities by working in null(A) with an orthonormal W. Special cases (bounds only, equalities only) then fall out of one analysis (pp. 4–6, 11–12).
- Compare against strong solvers with their accelerating components switched off, and say so (p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "showing that such a random strategy does not lead to an improvement over the deterministic setting." (p. 15)
- "We emphasize that our implementation is not as sophisticated as that of NOMAD and PSwarm, therefore we do not expect to be overwhelmingly superior to those methods." (p. 22)

### S058 · Trust-region methods for the derivative-free optimization of nonsmooth black-box functions (SIAM J. Optim. 2019, DOI 10.1137/19m125772x)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: G. Liuzzi (IASI-CNR), S. Lucidi (Sapienza) and F. Rinaldi (Padova) (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: Vicente at Lehigh and CMUC (p. 1). The paper brings the directional nonsmooth analysis (MADS [3]; Vicente–Custódio [38] = S024) to model-based trust regions. Its model Hessians follow [5] (S029). **Read**: partial. Read: pp. 1–22, with the key proofs. Skimmed: the proofs on pp. 17–19. The profile pages were read as captions only.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: derivative-free trust-region methods existed only for smooth functions or for known composite nonsmoothness [16, 18, 25, 30] (p. 2). A pure black box with unknown nonsmoothness had only directional methods, which reach Clarke stationarity by generating directions densely (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Basic DFO-TRNS** (p. 4):
    - The linear term g_k of the quadratic model is drawn randomly on the unit sphere.
    - The predicted reduction is replaced by θ‖s_k‖^{1+p}, which acts like a forcing function (pp. 3–4).
  - **Advanced DFO-TRNS** (p. 16) replaces subgradients in a bundle-style max-linear model with random directions g_i.
    - Each g_i gets a displacement β̄ⁱ computed from sample points y^j within γΔ_k ((20)–(23), p. 10).
    - Older terms are penalized by Δ_k^{1/2} ((24)–(25), p. 11), and a quadratic term is added (26).
    - The subproblem is a QP (28).
    - Reset rule: the set G_k is reset to {g_k} when ‖g̃_k‖ < ε̄Δ_k^{1/2}.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f is locally Lipschitz and bounded below (pp. 3, 5).
  - Model Hessians may be unbounded, with λ_max(B_k) ≤ MΔ_k^{−q} and −λ_min(B_k) ≤ mΔ_k^{−q}, for q ∈ (0, 1) (basic, Asm. 2.1, p. 4) or q ∈ (0, 1/2) (advanced, Asm. 3.1, p. 15).
  - Subproblems are solved exactly. The authors say inexact solves would work (p. 5).
  - The directions {g_k}_K must be dense in the unit sphere (Thm 2.3, p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **Lemma 2.1** (p. 5): Δ_k → 0, because every non-decrease iteration reduces f by a constant.
  - **Proposition 2.1** (pp. 5–7): s_k = −Δ_k D_k g_k with D_k → I. The proof bounds η_k = −s_kᵀB_k s_k − g_kᵀs_k on both sides and uses Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury.
  - **Lemma 2.2** (pp. 7–8): the Clarke derivative is ≥ 0 along limits of normalized unsuccessful steps. Theorem 2.3 then follows by contradiction with density (p. 8).
  - **Advanced method** (Thm 3.3, pp. 19–20): the Δ^{1/2} penalty makes all old linear pieces inactive for large k, so g̃_k = g_k and the basic proof applies.
  - No rate is given: a rate "does not seem likely" with dense directions (p. 25).
- **D5 Experiments**: 51 nonsmooth problems with n = 10–30; budget 10 000 evaluations; performance and data profiles at τ ∈ {10⁻³, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁶} against NOMAD 3.8.1 (pp. 20–21).
  - The comparisons are layered (p. 22): Basic with ω = 0 against NOMAD without models; a sweep over ω; Basic against Advanced; Advanced against NOMAD with its quadratic-model search.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - The first convergent trust-region method for nonsmooth DFO with no information on the nonsmoothness (p. 25). The software is available on request (p. 1).
  - Open issues: large problems (dense model Hessians), constraints, stochastic objectives, max-linear f, and a probabilistic rate argument (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- ✅ **Method 1 (evidence)**: the quadratic term is the efficiency component. Convergence comes from the random linear term plus the forcing-type ratio, so B_k is allowed to be unbounded (pp. 3–4, 25).
- ✅ **Method 4 (evidence)**: the trust-region skeleton is kept, and the acceptance test changes: the predicted reduction becomes θ‖s‖^{1+p} (p. 3).
- ⚠ **Method 3 (variant)**: randomness is used for asymptotic density of directions, not as a "with probability p" requirement (p. 8).
- ✗ **Method 2 (contradiction)**: no complexity bound, and the authors doubt one exists in this setting (p. 25).
- ⚠ **Method 5**: a collection with profiles, but code only on request.
- **New-pattern candidate**: *mechanism-isolating control variant* (Basic against Advanced; ω = 0 against NOMAD without models): p. 22.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To make a model-based method behave like a directional method in the limit, show that the step direction tends to a randomly drawn vector (s_k = −Δ_k D_k g_k with D_k → I). Then reuse the refining-direction argument (pp. 5–8).
- Replace predicted reduction by a power of the step norm when unsuccessful iterations must give an o(‖s‖) bound (p. 3).
- Penalize old cutting-plane pieces by Δ^{1/2}, so that asymptotically only the newest random piece is active. This recovers the simple proof for the richer model (pp. 11, 19–20).

**Quotes**:
- "Hence, our trust-region models will have built-in the random generation of their linear terms." (p. 2)
- "Interestingly, we have shown that the Hessian matrix of this quadratic term can be unbounded as long as it does not go to infinity faster than the inverse of a negative power of the trust-region radius." (p. 25)

### S060 · A decoupled first/second-order steps technique for nonconvex nonlinear unconstrained optimization with improved complexity bounds (Math. Program. 2020, DOI 10.1007/s10107-018-1328-7)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Gratton (Toulouse) and C. W. Royer (Wisconsin–Madison) (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: the direct sequel of S074 (cited as [18], p. 2): S074 traced its ε_g⁻³ term to a single decrease formula, and this paper decouples. The comparisons are to Cartis–Gould–Toint [5, 6], Fan–Yuan [13] and the nonlinear stepsize control of Grapiglia–Yuan–Yuan [17]. **Read**: partial. Read: pp. 1–22, plus Algorithm A.1 and Corollary A.1 with its proof. Not read: Lemma A.1 and Assumptions A.1–A.2.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: second-order globally convergent methods lose on the first-order part of their bound (p. 2):
  - trust regions give O(max{ε_C⁻²ε_E⁻¹, ε_E⁻³}) and direct search O(max{ε_C⁻³, ε_E⁻³});
  - cubic regularization does not have this loss (pp. 2–3).
  - The line-search literature suggests the cause: one step length serves two criteria of possibly very different size (p. 3). In trust regions the culprit is δ_k ≥ θ₂ min{ε_C, ε_E} (2.2) (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: DESTRESS (Alg. 2.1, p. 6):
  - two models, m^C and m^E;
  - two radii tied to their own criticality measures: δ_k^C = δ_k‖g_k^C‖ and δ_k^E = δ_k[−λ_k^E]₊;
  - the better of the two trial points is taken, with acceptance on ρ_k = min{ρ_k^C, ρ_k^E}, one ratio per model's predicted decrease;
  - a single parameter δ_k, updated in the usual way.
  - The derivative-free version needs a decoupled criticality step (Alg. A.1, p. 24).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f is C² with Lipschitz gradient and Hessian, and bounded below (p. 2).
  - First-order model: error ≤ F_C(δ^C)² in value, and gradient error ≤ C_C‖g^C‖ (Asm. 2.1). Second-order model: error ≤ F_E(δ^E)³ in value, with a relative error on [−λ]₊ (Asm. 2.3) (p. 7).
  - ‖H^C‖ ≤ B_C (Asm. 2.2), a fraction of Cauchy decrease and a fraction of eigendecrease (Asm. 2.4–2.5, p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **Lemma 3.1** (p. 10): δ_k is bounded below by a constant that does not depend on ε. This is the payoff of scaling each radius by its own criticality measure.
  - Successful iterations are at most C⁻¹(f₀ − f_low) max{ε_C⁻², ε_E⁻³} (Lemma 3.2, p. 11). Unsuccessful ones are counted by a logarithm (Lemma 3.3, p. 12).
  - Result: O(max{ε_C⁻², ε_E⁻³}) (Thm 3.1, p. 13). In the derivative-free case, O(r max{…}) (Cor. A.1, p. 28), which the authors call "somehow weaker" (p. 29).
- **D5 Experiments**: 61 CUTEst problems with negative curvature (p. 13). The algorithms compared are trbasic, destress and a coupled control, trscaled, whose radius is scaled by max{‖∇m‖, −λ_min} (4.1).
  - Setup: profiles in iterations, with problems of equal performance removed and the remaining count shown (p. 13).
  - Derivative-based results: destress is the most efficient, trscaled the least (p. 15), and γ₂ = 1.1 improves destress's robustness (pp. 17–19).
  - DFO version: profiles in iterations and in function evaluations; destress_dfo "performs extremely well" (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - The bound improves on the trust-region bound whenever ε_E⁻² < ε_C⁻² < ε_E⁻³ (p. 22). The authors believe decoupling is "most likely necessary" for bounds of this two-tolerance form (p. 22).
  - The DFO guarantees rely on tolerances that do not appear in the main algorithm (p. 29).
  - Future: decoupling inside nonlinear stepsize control and other methods (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- ✅ **Method 2 (evidence)**: recovers the first-order exponent ε_C⁻² inside a second-order bound and sets it next to the ARC, trust-region and direct-search bounds. The complexity result is used "as an indicator of an intrinsic difference in behavior" (p. 3).
- ⚠ **Method 4 (variant)**: the acceptance test is generalized (the minimum of two ratios), but by duplicating models and radii rather than by changing the problem class (pp. 5–6).
- ✅ **Method 5 (partial)**: a collection, profiles in both iterations and evaluations, and a control variant (pp. 13–22). No code was found.
- **New-pattern candidates**:
  - *Decouple coupled parameters to recover the base-case rate*: pp. 3, 5.
  - *Mechanism-isolating control variant* (trscaled): pp. 13, 15.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When adding a second criterion worsens the first-order exponent, find the single inequality that couples them (here δ_k ≥ θ₂ min{ε_C, ε_E}). Then give each criterion its own step scaled by its own measure (pp. 5–6).
- Accept on the minimum of the ratios, each computed against its own model's predicted decrease, so that an accepted step is at least as good as each of the two candidate steps (p. 5).
- Build a control variant that captures the obvious alternative explanation (here, radius scaling alone) and show that it does worse (pp. 13, 15).
- In profiles, drop problems where all methods tie and print the remaining count (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "The introduced decoupling technique consists in duplicating elements of the algorithm that intervene in the treatment of both features, treating each of them separately by means of dedicated step sizes." (p. 3)
- "We believe that the use of a decoupling is most likely necessary to obtain a bound as the one established in Theorem 3.1" (p. 22)

### S094 · The Sharpe predictor for fairness in machine learning (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv:2108.06415)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: S. Liu (Lehigh; Vicente's PhD student) (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: a short note on the stochastic multi-objective fairness line. It uses PF-SMG [23] (S017) and the fairness trade-off paper [24] (S016), and cites SAfairKM [25] (S065, this batch). **Read**: full (9 pp.).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: fairness methods mostly impose fairness as a constraint or a penalty, which does not reveal the full trade-off (abstract, p. 1). Once the Pareto front is computed, there is a "lack of guideline" for choosing a point. Three existing selection principles are listed: weights, a fairness threshold, and worst-group performance (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Borrowed from finance**: the Sharpe portfolio of Markowitz selection (p. 3).
  - **Definition** (Def. 3.1, p. 4): the Sharpe predictor maximizes (f_f − f₁(x))/f₂(x). Here f₁ is the prediction loss ("return"), f₂ the unfairness ("risk"), and f_f the loss of a risk-free predictor.
  - **Instance** (p. 5): for disparate impact, f₂ is the squared empirical covariance between the sensitive attribute and the decision boundary, a convex proxy of the CV score (4.1)–(4.3).
  - **Solver** (p. 6): the ratio is minimized by stochastic gradient with a growing batch, which gives a biased but consistent gradient estimator.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Logistic regression with two convex, smooth finite-sum objectives (p. 5). An O(1/√T) rate is quoted from [10] for convex problems with consistent biased gradients (p. 6).
  - Def. 3.1 asks f_f > max_x f₁(x). In the experiment f_f = 0.37 lies below the maximum training error of 0.394, which the text justifies by the different ranges in ML (pp. 4, 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 3.1 (p. 4) shows that the Sharpe predictor is a Pareto minimizer. The proof is a one-line contradiction ("elementary and known").
- **D5 Experiments**: Adult Income data (45 222 samples; 5000 used for training; gender as the sensitive attribute). The front is computed by PF-SMG with step 0.01 and 6500 iterations, and the Sharpe predictor lies on it (Fig. 2, pp. 5–6). No baseline selection rule is compared.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Presented as the first attempt to single out a meaningful point on the accuracy/fairness front (p. 2).
  - Broader use: comparing fairness measures, dimensionless benchmarking, information ratio, VaR/CVaR variants (pp. 6–7).
  - No limitations section was found.

**Method links**:
- ✅ **Workflow E step 3 / Heuristic 4 (evidence)**: compute the whole front first, then select one point by a principled criterion instead of fixing weights a priori (pp. 2, 4).
- ✅ **Heuristic 8 (variant)**: a practitioner notion is formalized, here imported from finance (Sharpe ratio) rather than from a verbal definition (pp. 3–4).
- ✗ **Method 5 (contradiction)**: one dataset, no baselines, no code link found.
- **New-pattern candidate**: *cross-domain transfer of a selection criterion* (finance to ML): pp. 3–4.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To select a point on a bi-objective front without weights, maximize a return-per-unit-risk ratio relative to a reference level. Prove in one line that the maximizer is Pareto optimal (p. 4).
- Replace a nonconvex fairness metric by a convex surrogate (squared covariance with the decision boundary) before optimizing (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "To our knowledge this is the first attempt to identify a prediction data point that could be put in relation to the accuracy/fairness Pareto front, rather than the two front extreme points corresponding to highest accuracy and fairness or any process involving the artificial selection of a weight or a threshold parameter." (p. 2)
- "The ranges of (f1, f2) in ML are not of the same nature of those of (r1, r2) in finance, and one difference forces us to consider ff as the accuracy of a low-risk predictor (instead of a risk-free one)." (p. 6)

### S065 · A Stochastic Alternating Balance k-Means Algorithm for Fair Clustering (LION 2022, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-24866-5_6, arXiv:2105.14172)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: S. Liu (Lehigh) (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: carries the Pareto-front, no-aggregation stance of S016 and S017 into unsupervised learning. The nondominated-list mechanism recalls DMS (S003), but DMS is not cited in the text read. **Read**: full (pp. 1–12; figure pages 12–15 as captions).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: fair clustering methods are mostly pre-processing (fairlets, coresets) or post-processing (fair re-assignment). The only in-processing trade-off method is Ziko et al.'s KL penalty [32] (pp. 2–3). Weighted sums fail here because both objectives are nonconvex, one is nonsmooth, and their magnitudes differ (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Formulation**: minimize the k-means cost and maximize balance over binary assignments (2) (p. 6).
  - **SAfairKM** (Alg. 2, p. 8): alternate n_a mini-batch k-means updates with n_b swap updates. A swap exchanges a V₂ point of the minimum-balance cluster C_l with a V₁ point of a target cluster C_h, chosen either globally (4) or as the nearest cluster (5) (p. 7).
  - **Weights by effort**: the pair (n_a, n_b) plays the role of the weights (pp. 4, 8).
  - **Front version** (Alg. 3, p. 9): a list of labels started at random. Every pair (n_a, n_b) is applied to every list member, and dominated members are removed.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: none stated. There is no convergence theory. That each swap increases the overall balance is asserted without proof (p. 7). J disjoint groups; balance as defined in [12] (pp. 5–6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none (not found).
- **D5 Experiments**: four synthetic sets (400 points in R², J = 2) and 5000-point subsamples of Adult (J = 2, gender) and Bank (J = 3, marital status) with K = 10 and 5 (pp. 10–11).
  - Baselines: VfairKM [32], FairAssign [7], VanillaKM and MinibatchKM, the last two with k-means++ initialization (pp. 9–10).
  - Metric: CPU time per nondominated solution (Table 1, p. 11).
  - The code is on GitHub (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - The SAfairKM fronts are more spread out. For Adult the front has "slightly lower quality" than VfairKM (p. 11).
  - "Clearly more computationally efficient" (p. 11), although Table 1 shows VfairKM faster on Syn unequal ds2 (0.80 against 0.10; p. 11), where VfairKM returned only the fairest solution (p. 10).
  - Future: several protected attributes as separate objectives (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- ✅ **Heuristic 4 / Method 4 (evidence)**: keep a nondominated list and do not aggregate. Success is nondominance, carried here into a combinatorial clustering setting (pp. 4, 9).
- ⚠ **Method 1 (variant)**: the practitioners' heuristic (mini-batch k-means) is kept intact and alternated with a second move, but no convergent skeleton or guarantee is added (pp. 4, 8).
- ✗ **Method 2 (contradiction)**: no complexity or convergence analysis.
- ✅ **Method 5**: public GitHub code, real datasets, four baselines (pp. 8–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Parameterize the trade-off by how many update steps each objective receives (n_a, n_b) instead of by weights. This works when the objectives differ in magnitude or smoothness (pp. 4, 8).
- Turn a single-point method into a front method by running it from every member of a nondominated list under several effort pairs and pruning dominated members after each sweep (Alg. 3, p. 9).
- When methods return fronts of different sizes, compare cost per nondominated solution (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "One can hardly preselect a good set of weights corresponding to decision-makers’ preferences to capture a well-spread Pareto front." (p. 3)
- "the number of k-means updates (denoted by na) and the number of swap updates (denoted by nb) play a role similar to the weights in the weighted-sum method, parameterizing the efforts of optimizing each objective." (p. 4)

### S078 · Optimal 3D printing of complex objects in a 5-axis printer (Optim. Eng. 2022, DOI 10.1007/s11081-021-09624-0)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: B. Ramos, D. Martins and A. I. F. Vaz (Minho), and D. Pinho (Minho and IPB) (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: the sequel of Pereira–Vaz–Vicente 2018 [26] (S036), which computed one global optimum per orientation problem (p. 4). The local solver is probabilistic direct search [11, 12] (S019, S045) (p. 24). The paper came out of the FIBR3D project (p. 24). **Read**: partial. Read: §§1–7 and Appendix A. Skimmed: §5.4.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: a 5-axis printer (table rotation and tilt) can print complex objects part by part with fewer supports (pp. 1–2). A single optimal orientation per part, as in [26], "reveal itself insufficient": it can make the sequencing problem infeasible through collisions (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: two levels.
  - **Level 1, MMLS** (Alg. 1, p. 6; Alg. 4, p. 25) finds all local or global optima of each part's orientation problem:
    - evaluation history is clustered around the run centres;
    - one quadratic underestimator is fitted per cluster by an LP;
    - a run splits when the fit residual θ exceeds α, and runs merge when their centres are close;
    - the local search is probabilistic direct search with a random direction and its negative (App. A.2–A.4, pp. 23–24).
  - **Level 2, sequencing model** (pp. 9–11): binary rotation and time-slot variables, with precedence constraints (4)–(5) and black-box NoCollision/NoSupport constraints (6)–(7).
  - **Level 2, solver**: a recursive enumeration heuristic (Algs. 2–3, pp. 11–12). It prunes with collision checks, and a decision maker picks the objective afterwards (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Smoothness is deferred to the local search (p. 5); f is expensive (p. 5).
  - Convergence "is highly based on" the local search, provided clustering and splitting are finite (pp. 5–7). No theorem is given.
  - The staircase, support-area and build-time measures are taken from [26] (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none. The split threshold θ > α is justified by an O(α) Lipschitz heuristic (p. 24).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Duct object: 2115 evaluations; 2 global and 2 local optima identified (p. 7).
  - Candelabrum: 18 parts, 15 190 facets. Per-part MMLS results are in Table 1 (p. 19). Sequences L1 and L2 are simulated in FIBR3DEmul, and computing them takes under 2 minutes on a laptop (pp. 20–22).
  - For parts 3 and 11, the numbers in §§6.1.3–6.1.4 do not match Table 1: 6665 and 6850 evaluations in the text against 7275 and 10 316 in the table, and part 11's minimum is 0 in the text against 297.078 in the table (pp. 16–17, 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - The limits: NoCollision and NoSupport are printer-specific (p. 22), the Support routine was not used in the reported results (p. 19), and validation by actual printing is outside the paper's scope (p. 4).
  - The MATLAB code is available by email (p. 22). Future work includes a collision cache (pp. 18, 22).

**Method links**:
- ✅ **Method 3 (evidence of reuse)**: probabilistic direct search with two directions is picked as the local engine because it performed best [11, 12] (p. 24).
- ⚠ **Method 1 (variant, inverted)**: here the heuristic layer (clustering, split and merge) wraps a convergent local method, and convergence is argued to be inherited if the heuristic layer is finite (pp. 5–7).
- ✅ **Taste mark 6 / Method 5 step 6**: a real engineering application with simulated prints (pp. 19–22).
- ✗ **Method 2 (contradiction)**: no evaluation bound; the unit of cost reported is evaluations per part (Table 1).
- **New-pattern candidate**: *compute all local optima when they feed a downstream combinatorial problem* (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a subproblem's solution feeds a combinatorial master problem with side constraints, return every local optimum, not one global optimum. Alternatives are what make the master problem feasible (pp. 4, 9).
- Split a multistart run when a quadratic fitted to its cluster fits poorly (residual > step size), and merge runs whose centres come within the step size (pp. 23–25).
- Enumerate sequences recursively, and check collisions at the moment a part is added. Infeasible prefixes are then pruned early (Algs. 2–3, pp. 11–13, 18).

**Quotes**:
- "However, a single optimum computation reveal itself insufficient, since the computed optimal printing direction may lead to an infeasible sequencing problem" (p. 4)
- "In practice, it is only this last stage that can properly validate the proposed strategy and show that a valid path planning is indeed obtained." (p. 4)
