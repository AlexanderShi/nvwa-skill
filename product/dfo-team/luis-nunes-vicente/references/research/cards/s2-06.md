### S089 · Modeling Hessian-vector products in nonlinear optimization: new Hessian-free methods (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2022, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drab022; arXiv:1912.10523)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: L. Song (Coimbra, supported by an FCT PhD-programme grant, p. 1). The final remarks refer to "a future PhD thesis of the first author" (p. 15). · **Position in trajectory**: the move from Coimbra to Lehigh (Vicente is listed at Lehigh and CMUC, p. 1). The paper exports DFO interpolation tools to a derivative-based setting. These are the CSV book [4], Powell's least-Frobenius-norm update [16] and ℓ1 sparse models [1] (pp. 4, 17–18). The oracle provides f, ∇f and Hessian-vector products (p. 1). **Read**: full (pp. 1–18).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Inexact (truncated) Newton methods solve the Newton system by CG using Hessian-vector products. Residual control, global convergence and negative-curvature handling are classical (p. 2). Existing ways of estimating a Hessian are called "not entirely satisfactory" (p. 2). The paper cites sparse multiple secants [9], which show no significant gain over L-BFGS or Newton trust-region methods, grouped finite differences [17] and componentwise secants [2] (p. 2). Entry: in the Hessian-free regime, use as few Hessian-vector products as possible "without losing the ability to converge" (p. 2). The products become extra interpolation conditions for a quadratic model around x, alongside f(x), ∇f(x) and the values f(y^ℓ) (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper poses two recovery problems.
  - (i) *Hessian recovery*. The interpolation conditions (1) are enriched with one product Hv = w, and the paper solves min norm(H), or the least-secant problem min norm(H − H_prev), subject to both (problems (2)–(3), p. 4). The same truncated CG as the baseline is then applied to H d = −∇f (p. 6). Remark 2.1 explains why only one product can be used and why v cannot be any y^ℓ − x: a second condition makes the system rank-deficient in H (p. 3).
  - (ii) *Newton-direction recovery*. Substituting the products z^ℓ = ∇²f(x)(y^ℓ − x) into the Taylor conditions gives conditions (13) that are linear in the direction d: (z^ℓ)ᵀd = −f(y^ℓ) + f(x) + ½(y^ℓ − x)ᵀz^ℓ. The recovery is min ‖d − d_prev‖ subject to (13) (problem (14), p. 10). This version can use p products at once (p. 3).
  - The outer iteration is unchanged: the same line search and the same comparison setting as inexact Newton (pp. 6–7). What changes is only where the curvature comes from.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is C². The oracle returns f, ∇f and ∇²f v (p. 1). The monotonicity theorems assume f quadratic and the enriched system feasible and underdetermined (Theorems 2.1, 3.1; pp. 4, 11). The determined-case accuracy theorem assumes p + n = (n² + n)/2 and M nonsingular (Theorem 2.2, p. 6). The direction-accuracy theorem assumes p ≥ n, M^z_L of full column rank, ∇²f(x) invertible and a Lipschitz Hessian (Theorem 3.2, p. 11). The authors concede that p = n products per iteration "is certainly not a desirable strategy", since it amounts to computing the whole Hessian. They get around this by reusing products, not by relaxing the theorem (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorems 2.1 and 3.1 follow Powell's argument [16] (p. 4). If the truth C satisfies the constraints, then C − H* is a feasible direction of the affine set. So m(θ) = ½‖(H* − H_prev) + θ(C − H*)‖² is minimized at θ = 0, which gives orthogonality and then ‖H* − C‖² = ‖H_prev − C‖² − ‖H* − H_prev‖² (pp. 4–5; the same argument for d on p. 11).
  - Theorem 2.2 shows that the determined system yields a fully quadratic model, ‖H − ∇²f(x)‖ = O(Δ_y), with a constant that depends on the inverse of a scaled M. It uses the Taylor argument of [3, Thm 4.2] (p. 6).
  - Theorem 3.2 and Corollary 3.1 bound the direction error by Λ_z O(Δ_y³/Δ_z) and by ‖R_y‖ O(Δ_y²) (pp. 11–12).
  - The recovery cost through the KKT system (10) is O((p+n)²n²) + O((p+n)³) with a direct solve, or O(cg·(p+n)n²) with CG (pp. 9–10).
  - No convergence or complexity theorem is stated for the resulting optimization algorithms (checked pp. 1–15).
- **D5 Experiments**: Three CUTEst sets: 48 very small problems (n ≤ 30), 12 sparse small problems and 26 small problems (n ≤ 200) (Appendices B–D, p. 16). The baseline is inexact Newton [15, §7.1], with the same truncated CG and cubic line search (Armijo c1 = 10⁻⁴), stopping at ‖∇f‖ < 10⁻⁵ (pp. 6–7). Performance profiles are drawn for Hessian-vector products, iterations and function evaluations (Figs. 1–8, pp. 7–14). The Hessian recovery saves about 50% of the products, but costs of the order of n² function evaluations per iteration (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic ideas plus motivating theorems (§§2.2 and 3.2 are both headed Theoretical motivation), which the authors present as groundwork (pp. 4, 10, 15). The limits are stated concretely:
  - approach (i) can use at most one product, and approach (ii) must solve a dense linear system (p. 15);
  - the test problems are small, the linear algebra is dense, and the number of function evaluations can be relatively high (p. 3).

  Future work: medium- and large-scale versions, with an iterative solve of (13) and row-wise parallel storage of M^z_L (p. 15). A third approach, which recovers the direction and the inverse Hessian jointly, "performed the worse". Its details were left for the first author's thesis (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence (pp. 12–13): reuse information already paid for. Old products are corrected by gradient differences, z_prev + ∇f(x_prev) − ∇f(x) (eq. (18)). Each iteration then needs only one new product and one new function evaluation.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (pp. 1, 7, 9–10): the counted unit is the expensive oracle call (the Hessian-vector product), and the price paid elsewhere (function evaluations, linear algebra) is named in the abstract. But there is no worst-case bound and no yardstick theorem. The evidence is profiles only.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 6–7, 13): the recovered direction is a heuristic placed inside a standard sufficient-decrease line search, with a cosine safeguard. The skeleton is borrowed, but global convergence is not proved.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 7–16): public CUTEst sets and performance profiles against the standard method. No code release is mentioned (searched the full text), and n ≤ 200.
- new-pattern candidate: reverse field import (pool; S029, S041). DFO interpolation is exported to derivative-based Hessian-free optimization (pp. 4, 15).
- new-pattern candidate: design around the user's existing oracle (pool; S040, S011). The method uses only f, ∇f and ∇²f v (p. 1).
- new-pattern candidate: change the unknown to escape a degeneracy. Remark 2.1 caps Hessian recovery at one product, and posing the recovery in the space of Newton directions admits p products (pp. 3, 10).
- new-pattern candidate: state where you lose (pool): the function-evaluation and linear-algebra costs appear in the abstract and on pp. 3 and 7. Also disclose omitted experiments (pool): the third approach that performed worst is mentioned but not shown (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Least-change monotonicity lemma. If the unknown true object satisfies your affine data conditions, then the minimum-norm-change solution is its projection, and the error obeys Pythagoras: ‖new − truth‖² = ‖old − truth‖² − ‖new − old‖². Prove it by minimizing m(θ) along the feasible direction (truth − new) (pp. 4–5, 11).
- Before adding extra conditions to an interpolation system, check for degeneracy: multiply each new condition by the other conditions' vectors and look for repeated rows (Remark 2.1, p. 3).
- Recycle expensive oracle outputs across iterations with a first-order correction (eq. (18)). Refresh only the point farthest from the iterate each iteration, and restart from scratch when cond(M^z_L) ≥ 10⁸ (pp. 12–13).
- Turn any model direction into a descent direction with a fixed angle: d = d_N − β∇f, with β chosen so that cos(d, −∇f) = η = 0.95 (p. 13).
- Tie the sampling radius to the last step: r_k = min{10⁻², max{10⁻⁴, ‖x_k − x_{k−1}‖}} (p. 7).
- Profile both the resource you save (Hessian-vector products) and the resource you spend more of (function evaluations) (Figs. 1–2, pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "These techniques can lead to a significant reduction in the overall number of Hessian-vector products when compared to the inexact or truncated Newton method, although simple implementations may pay a cost in linear algebra or number of function evaluations." (p. 1)
- "We aimed at presenting new, refreshing ideas, laying down the theoretical groundwork for future more elaborated algorithmic developments." (p. 15)

### S080 · An integrated assignment, routing, and speed model for roadway mobility and transportation with environmental, efficiency, and service goals (Transportation Research Part C: Emerging Technologies 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.trc.2023.104144; arXiv:2210.03717)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: T. Giovannelli (Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period. This is an application paper in the urban-transport area named in the Lagrange Prize citation (SKILL.md, Research Taste #6). It is built on multilevel and multiobjective modelling, not on a DFO algorithm. **Read**: full (pp. 1–24; references, pp. 25–29, not read).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The setting is a smart city with autonomous vehicles, where assignment, routing and speed must be decided together. There are three conflicting goals: emissions, efficiency and service quality (pp. 1–3). Speed control counts as a decision only because the vehicles are autonomous (footnote, p. 3). The paper identifies three gaps (pp. 2–5):
  - one component's decisions affect the others through congestion, yet each transportation service is modelled on its own;
  - VRP formulations assume complete graphs, and their assumptions fail on road networks, where only some nodes are customers and nodes or edges may have to be visited repeatedly;
  - multilevel VRP models in the literature stop at two levels.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper makes three contributions (pp. 3–4).
  - (i) A trilevel multi-objective *architecture*: assignment at the upper level, routing in the middle and speed at the lower level, each with a vector objective (problem (3.2), p. 7). It is then reduced to a bilevel problem, with assignment-routing above speed (3.3)–(3.4) (p. 7).
  - (ii) An integration constraint. The speed–density volume-delay relation (4.1) is turned into an upper bound on the speed of each edge (4.3), driven by the number k of vehicles on that edge from other components. It comes with a capacity constraint (4.4) (pp. 8–9).
  - (iii) Algorithm 1 (p. 19). An outer loop enumerates the number of vehicles. An inner loop alternates three steps: (2.1) solve the assignment-routing problem on an auxiliary complete graph over the customer nodes, whose edge lengths are shortest paths, using existing VRP methods (OR-Tools); (2.2) map the routes back through the shortest paths (5.33); (2.3) solve the speed NLP on the real road network (IPOPT). The loop stops when nothing changes or after j_max iterations (pp. 14, 17–19, 22).
  - The congestion constraint (5.17) is left out of the auxiliary problem because VRP solvers cannot handle it. It is enforced afterwards by an infinite penalty (5.34) on offending edges and a re-solve (p. 17).
  - Objectives are combined by a weighted sum, with each term normalized by its value at the initial solution. Dominated points are then filtered out (pp. 18, 21–22).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The routes r^{−i} of the other components enter as parameters, and w stands for uncertainty such as weather or drivers who do not share GPS data (p. 7). The bound (4.3) is admitted to be pessimistic, because vehicles traverse an edge at different times (p. 9). The speed on an edge traversed more than once equals the speed of the first traversal; this is listed as a limitation (p. 24). The experimental data choices are:
  - demands set to 0, which removes the capacity constraints;
  - setup and lateness costs set "arbitrarily" to $100;
  - an emission curve taken from Demir et al.;
  - γ = 1 and η = 2 from Kucharski and Drabicki (pp. 20–21).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No theorems. The only formal argument is a feasibility transfer: constraints (5.23)–(5.28) of the auxiliary problem guarantee that constraints (5.11)–(5.16) of the original problem hold at the mapped solution (p. 17). Algorithm 1 has no convergence or optimality analysis. It stops at a fixed point or after j_max iterations (p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two instances cut from a New York City traffic dataset: 269 nodes, 656 edges and 10 customers; and 1130 nodes, 2703 edges and 30 customers (p. 20). Congestion is set to 0% or 50% of capacity, and a stochastic case draws Poisson background traffic with λ = β·k_max, β ∈ {0.10, 0.15} (pp. 21, 23). The outputs are approximate Pareto fronts, trade-off percentages (e.g. going from 5 vehicles to 1 saves 53.27% of CO₂), and CPU times of 408.58 s and 2995.91 s per weight vector (pp. 22–23). There is no baseline method.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a modelling framework, an integration constraint and a decomposition heuristic, called the "groundwork" for a green, integrated system (pp. 23–24). The limits are specific (p. 24):
  - formulations for pickup-and-delivery (PDP) and people-and-freight (PFITP) components are still to be developed;
  - the same-speed-on-repeated-edges assumption should be removed;
  - speed optimization is slow, with only local guarantees, and needs heuristics for real time (pp. 22, 24).

  Out of scope: how cities acquire the technology, and the design of the city-brain platform (p. 2). Also left for the future: a smartphone app to collect user requests (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Research Taste #6 ✅ evidence (pp. 1–3, 20): the paper is anchored in the urban-transport application named in the Lagrange citation, and it uses real traffic data.
- Heuristic 4 ✗ contradiction (pp. 18, 21–22): objectives are aggregated with normalized weights before the front is known. The only nondominance device kept is a filter applied afterwards to discard dominated points.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 4, 14, 17): the existing VRP solver machinery is kept, and a single object is changed: the graph is replaced by an auxiliary complete graph, with a map back and a penalty repair. Here the change is made to the problem data, not to an acceptance test.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 20–23): real-data instances and released solvers (OR-Tools, IPOPT) are used, but there is no baseline, no profiles and no code release. The URLs found are only for OR-Tools and the traffic dataset (references pp. 25–29, located by search).
- new-pattern candidate: reformulate to reuse existing solvers (pool, previously named for DFO solvers only). The auxiliary complete graph exists "to use existing VRP methods for complete graphs" (p. 4). The constraint the solver cannot handle is moved into a penalty (p. 17).
- new-pattern candidate: check objective conflict before going multiobjective (pool, S036). Here the check is made afterwards: the service-quality terms were "observed not to be conflicting" and were dropped from the plots (p. 22).
- new-pattern candidate: architecture first, instance second. State the most general multilevel multiobjective template, then derive the concrete problem as a particular case (pp. 6–7, 12, 17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a standard solver's structural assumption fails on your data (here a complete graph), solve on an auxiliary instance that satisfies it. Map the solution back, prove that the mapped solution satisfies the constraints that carry over (p. 17), and enforce the rest with a penalty-and-re-solve loop (pp. 14, 17).
- Normalize each term of a weighted-sum objective by its value at the initial solution, so that the weights are unit-free (p. 21).
- Model how components interact by turning a published empirical relation (the volume–delay curve) into a parametric bound on the decision variables of one component (pp. 8–9).
- Sweep the fleet size in an outer loop and report the trade-off as percentages between named Pareto points, for example: going from 5 vehicles to 1 saves 53.27% of CO₂ and raises the maximum driving distance from 5.37 km to 8.87 km (p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "The advantage of considering first such a complex trilevel formulation is to comprehensively understand the overall problem complexity and guide the development of subproblems" (p. 7)
- "In the formulation of problem (5.22), we do not include a constraint enforcing (5.17) because it would not allow solving problem (5.22) by using existing VRP methods, which are not designed to handle such a constraint." (p. 17)

### S093 · Convergence rates of the stochastic alternating algorithm for bi-objective optimization (Journal of Optimization Theory and Applications 2023, DOI 10.1007/s10957-023-02253-w; arXiv:2203.10605)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Liu (Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period, in Liu's fairness and multiobjective line. It is the rates companion of the SAfairKM fair-clustering method (ref. [28], p. 3) and a sibling of the stochastic multi-gradient paper S017 (ref. [29]). It reuses four DMS test problems (ref. [15], p. 15). **Read**: full (pp. 1–17; middle steps of the proof of Theorem 3.2, pp. 12–13, skimmed).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is to minimize (f^a, f^b) over a compact convex set, with only unbiased stochastic (sub)gradients available (pp. 1–2). The entry is an application. In fair k-means clustering the authors "could not find a reasonable way" to optimize a convex combination of the two objectives, but knew how to optimize each one separately (p. 3). The question is what alternating blocks of steps on each objective converge to, and how fast. The paper states when its method should be preferred: legacy or binary black-box optimizers, descent steps that depend on the function, or objectives whose scales are hard to balance. Otherwise weighted-sum or ε-constraint methods "would be the reasonable choices" (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm SA2GD (Algorithm 1, p. 4) takes n_a stochastic (sub)gradient steps on f^a, then n_b steps on f^b, all with the same step size α_t, and projects once at the end of the iteration. The key insight is that the effort split acts as a scalarization weight. The iterates converge to the minimizer of S(·, λ) = λf^a + (1 − λ)f^b with λ = n_a/(n_a + n_b). Sweeping n_a with n_a + n_b fixed traces the Pareto front (pp. 6, 15). The paper classes the method as a priori, like weighted-sum. Unlike the multi-gradient method, it is parameterized, so the front can be traced without a nondominated list (pp. 3–5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The feasible set X is compact with diameter Θ (Assumption 3.1, p. 5).
  - Smooth case: L-Lipschitz gradients, strong convexity (constant c = min(c_a, c_b)), unbiased gradients, and a second moment bounded by G + Ḡ‖∇f‖² (Assumptions 3.2–3.4, pp. 5–6).
  - Nonsmooth case: Lipschitz functions, unbiased subgradients with bounded second moment, and strong convexity (Assumptions 3.5–3.7, pp. 10–11).
  - Plain convexity is used for the O(1/√T) corollaries (Assumptions 3.8–3.9, pp. 14–15).
  - Theorem 3.1 still holds if only one of the two objectives is strongly convex (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 3.1 (p. 6): with α_t = 2/(c(t+1)(n_a+n_b)), min_t E[S(x_t, λ*)] − S(x*, λ*) ≤ 4(Ĝ² + LΘĜ)/(c(T+1)). The proof has three parts.
  - Part I (pp. 6–7) uses Proposition A.1 (p. 17), an intermediate value theorem (IVT) statement for a finite sum. It turns Σ_r φ(y_r) into n·φ(w), with w in the convex hull of the inner iterates, applied to φ(y) = −2α_t(x_t − x*)ᵀ∇f^i(y).
  - Part II (pp. 8–9) bounds ‖x_t − w^i‖ ≤ α_t(n_a + n_b)·√(G + ḠM∇), so the bundling error is O(α_t²). The decaying step size "compensates for the error" (p. 10).
  - Part III (p. 9) is the standard weighted telescoping: multiply by t and sum.
  - Corollary 3.1 gives an O(1/T) rate for the iterates, following Nemirovski et al. (p. 10). In the nonsmooth Theorem 3.2 the IVT is applied *after* strong convexity, to ψ(x) = f(x) − f(x*) + (ĉ/2)‖x − x*‖², because Lipschitz gradients are unavailable (pp. 11–14). Corollaries 3.2 and 3.3 give O(1/√T) in the convex case, and 3.3 is stated without proof (pp. 14–15). All bounds also hold for weighted averages of the iterates, by Jensen (p. 15).
- **D5 Experiments**: This is an illustration only. Four deterministic bound-constrained problems from DMS (MOP1, IM1, MOP3, FAR1) were run with full-batch gradients and a fixed step of 10⁻³. There were 201 runs with n_a + n_b = 200, compared with 201 weighted-sum runs over a λ grid of 1/200, each run stopped after 300 iterations (pp. 15–16). The authors say the fronts look similar: "Regardless of the shape of Pareto front (convex, concave, or disconnected)" (p. 16). The comparison is visual only (Fig. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical: rates that match single-objective SGD and subgradient methods, with Beck's theorems cited as the counterparts (pp. 3, 11, 14, 16). The authors claim three extensions without proof (p. 17): the rates hold for any ordering of the steps, including random interleaving within an iteration, and for more than two objectives. The stated open problem is the nonconvex case that the fair-clustering application actually needs (p. 17). The experiment is deterministic and includes concave and disconnected fronts, both outside the convex stochastic theory (pp. 15–16). The paper does not comment on this.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (pp. 3, 11, 14, 16–17): the explicit yardstick is the single-objective stochastic gradient and subgradient rate. The authors write that "we recover the same convergence rates" (p. 3). This is an earlier instance of the claim, paraphrased in SKILL.md from the 2026 block-coordinate paper, that the classical single-objective rates are recovered.
- Research Taste #3 ✅ evidence (pp. 3–4): the practitioner's alternating scheme is analyzed exactly as it is used in SAfairKM. No safeguard is added.
- Heuristic 4 ✗ contradiction (pp. 3–4, 15–16): the method is an a priori, scalarizing method. The paper recommends weighted-sum or ε-constraint methods whenever the combination can be optimized. It recovers the front by sweeping the effort weight, not by keeping a nondominated list.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 4, 17): the SGD skeleton and its step-size rule are kept unchanged across the move to two objectives. The new object is found in the analysis (an implicit weighted sum), not in the acceptance test.
- new-pattern candidate: implicit scalarization in the analysis only (pool, S015). The IVT bundling reveals the weight n_a/(n_a + n_b), which is never formed in the algorithm (pp. 3, 17).
- new-pattern candidate: theory paper / application paper split (pool). SAfairKM [28] is the application and this is the rates paper (pp. 3, 15).
- new-pattern candidate: disclosed theory–implementation gap (pool). The illustration uses deterministic, partly nonconvex problems (p. 15). Here the gap is only partly disclosed: the deterministic choice is flagged ("For simplicity"), but the convexity gap is not.

**Transferable techniques**:
- IVT bundling for multi-step inner loops. First reduce the sum to scalar terms by taking the inner product with (x_t − x*). Then replace Σ_r φ(y_r) by m·φ(w), with w in the convex hull of the inner iterates (Proposition A.1, p. 17). Bound ‖x_t − w‖ by α_t times the number of steps times the gradient bound. The resulting O(α_t²) error is absorbed by a diminishing step size (pp. 6–10).
- Without Lipschitz gradients, use strong convexity first and apply the IVT to the quadratic surrogate ψ(x) = f(x) − f(x*) + (ĉ/2)‖x − x*‖² (pp. 12, 14).
- To trace a front with a method that has no explicit weights, sweep the split of a fixed step budget (n_a = 0, …, 200 with n_a + n_b = 200). Compare against a weighted-sum sweep at the same resolution and with the same budget (pp. 15–16).
- Include a paragraph on when to use your method that names the settings where the standard alternative is better (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "In this application we could not find a reasonable way to optimize a convex linear combination of the two functions. Rather, we were faced with a situation where we knew how to optimize them separately." (p. 3)
- "We ingeniously applied the Intermediate Value Theorem to bundle all steps taken on each of the functions, a process that led to an implicit scalarization" (p. 17)

### S120 · The Effects of Travel Distance on MLB Performance (manuscript, undated; no DOI or arXiv id)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. Khromchenko (undergraduate sophomore, corresponding author) and S. Parlett (undergraduate senior), both in Lehigh ISE. Vicente is listed as "Timothy J. Wilmott Endowed Chair Professor" (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: late Lehigh period (it cites a 2026 paper, p. 10). This is an undergraduate-led sports-analytics study in the line of S086 and S092. It contains no model, algorithm or theorem (checked pp. 1–10). **Read**: full (pp. 1–10).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: MLB teams travel 40,000–50,000 miles a year (pp. 1–2). The research question is "To what extent does travel-related fatigue impact win probability" (p. 2). The gap is that earlier findings are mixed or insignificant (pp. 2–3). Prior work points away from raw mileage and toward jet lag in the direction of travel (Song et al. 2017, PNAS; p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The data are Retrosheet game logs for 2014–2023, joined with ArcGIS stadium coordinates. Distances are great-circle (Haversine), and the tests are Z-tests and logistic regression, run in Pandas and NumPy (pp. 3–4). Two devices control for team talent:
  - the samples are split into "Top 10 vs. Top 10" and "Top 10 vs. Bottom 10" matchups;
  - the rank difference (visitor minus home) is a covariate (p. 4).

  Four analyses follow. (i) Home win probability is regressed on visitor distance (p. 5). (ii) Games are grouped by consecutive travel moves: static, City 1, City 2, City 3+ (p. 6). (iii) Games below 300, 600 and 900 miles are excluded in turn (p. 7). (iv) A 200-mile cutoff is used as a proxy for bus versus jet travel (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The travel mode is inferred from distance, not observed: teams "typically" take a bus under 200 miles (p. 8). The paper does not say how teams are ranked into the Top 10 and Bottom 10 (not stated on pp. 3–4). The authors acknowledge that the distance measure ignores direction and time zones (pp. 8–9). The two Z-tests were planned in advance, so no multiple-comparison correction was applied (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Statistics only: logistic regression with 95% bands (pp. 4–5), two-proportion Z-tests (pp. 6–8) and correlation coefficients at distance thresholds (p. 7). No theorems.
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Distance effects are not significant (p = 0.8115 and p = 0.69860; p. 5).
  - The consecutive-travel groups form a U shape: visitor win percentage is 53.7% static, 46.9% at City 1, 47.2% at City 2 and 50.7% at City 3+, with p < 0.0001 and p = 0.0012 for the two planned tests (p. 6).
  - Threshold correlations run from r = −0.0229 to −0.0576 (p. 7).
  - Jet trips win 50.3% against 47.9% for bus trips, p = 0.0240 (pp. 7–8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is empirical. Mileage has no measurable effect, there is an initial "travel shock" followed by adaptation, and there is a "jet advantage" (pp. 8–9). The stated limitation is concrete: the great-circle distance cannot capture the east/west asymmetry that Song et al. report (pp. 8–9). Future work covers college athletes (a "resource gap"), academic stress and richer metrics such as ERA and batting average (p. 9).

  Reader's observations, not stated by the authors:
  - Because the travel mode is inferred from the 200-mile cutoff, the "jet advantage" is also a short-versus-long distance contrast (p. 8).
  - The conclusion that teams "have successfully neutralized the impact of travel" (p. 9) rests largely on non-significant tests (p. 5).
  - The abstract gives the threshold correlations as "r=-0.046 to -0.058" (p. 1), while p. 7 reports −0.0229, −0.0464, −0.0576 and −0.0454.
  - The text does not say whose win percentage the bars of Fig. 3 show (p. 7).
