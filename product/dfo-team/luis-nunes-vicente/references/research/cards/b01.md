### S040 · Trust-Region Interior Point Algorithms for a Class of Nonlinear Programming Problems (PhD Thesis, Rice University 1996, no DOI/arXiv)

Dennis (p. 1, p. 5); most of the work came from collaboration with M. Heinkenschloss, who introduced him to optimal control (p. 5). Results also appear in Dennis–Heinkenschloss–Vicente TR94-45 [36] (p. 18, p. 171), Dennis–Vicente TR94-42 [41] and TR94-36 [42] (p. 172), Heinkenschloss–Vicente TR95-18 [77] (p. 135, p. 175), and a solver guide [76] (p. 175) · **Position in trajectory**: the derivative-based period (1990–1996 row of the SKILL.md trajectory). - **D1 Problem & entry point**: The entry is an application-defined problem class with exploitable structure, not a gap in general NLP theory. The thesis opens by saying that optimization now works class by class, tailoring existing ideas to a class that comes from an application (p. 15). The class is discretized PDE-constrained optimal control (also design, parameter identification, inversion) with bounds only on controls. State bounds are excluded explicitly as a different, harder problem (p. 15). Three constraints of the application shape the entry (pp. 16, 88): the Jacobian is too large, or not available explicitly, for QR-type factorizations; efficient linearized-state and adjoint solvers already exist in the application; and the problems are often ill-conditioned (p. 17). The gap is stated as the combination of this structure with global convergence and bound handling. Purely local analyses without bounds existed [83], [86], [87], [89] (p. 88). Inexactness for trust-region SQP had, to the author's knowledge, not been studied (p. 18, p. 137). Chapter 3 has its own smaller entry: Dennis–El-Alem–Maciel [35] proved first-order convergence for the quasi-normal TR reduced SQP family. The contribution is the second-order result, extending Moré–Sorensen from unconstrained optimization (pp. 39, 48).
  - Transferable technique: define the problem class by the operations the application already provides (here: solves with C_y and C_yᵀ, matrix-vector products with C_u), and list the operations it cannot provide (factorizations). The algorithm-design constraints then follow from the class (pp. 74, 88).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key idea is composition. Take three techniques that work elsewhere and fit each to the state/control split (pp. 16–17): (1) reduced SQP, where the null-space basis W(x) = [−C_y⁻¹C_u; I] comes from the linearized state equation (pp. 73–74); (2) trust regions, for global convergence and as a regularizer of ill-conditioned second-order terms (p. 17, p. 89); (3) the Coleman–Li primal-dual affine scaling for the bounds on u, which needs no information beyond the unbounded case (p. 17, p. 89). What changes relative to the closest classical method (Dennis–El-Alem–Maciel TR reduced SQP, Ch. 3):
  - the quasi-normal component moves only in y (form (5.9)). It is a trust-region globalization of a Newton step on C(y,u)=0 for fixed u (pp. 94–95);
  - the tangential subproblem is scaled by D_k and augmented with the Newton term E_k D_k⁻² (5.14)–(5.15). It comes from symmetrizing Newton's method on the nonsmooth system C=0, D²Wᵀ∇f=0 (pp. 90–96);
  - strict interiority by damping σ_k ∈ [σ,1). The TR subproblem may be solved without the box and then scaled back (pp. 95–96);
  - two trust-region shapes. The decoupled shape bounds s^q and D⁻¹s_u separately and is cheaper. The coupled shape bounds the whole tangential component and regularizes better when C_y is ill-conditioned (condition-number argument, p. 97).

  What stays the same: the augmented Lagrangian merit function, the ared/pred ratio test, El-Alem's penalty update (pp. 103–104, identical to Algorithm 3.4.1, pp. 54–55), and the requirement that step components satisfy only fractions of Cauchy or optimal decrease, not exact solutions (pp. 95, 99–102). The why: every quantity is computable with the application's solvers, and the reduced SQP step coincides with the Newton step of the black-box reduced problem. So the all-at-once method uses black-box information without solving C(y,u)=0 at each iteration (pp. 76–77).
  - Transferable technique: when a problem class sits at the intersection of two solved special cases (equality constraints; simple bounds), compose the two best-understood algorithms and change only the pieces that interact. Here these are the scaling of the tangential step and the interiority damping (pp. 89–96).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Smooth setting. f and C are C² on an open set Ω containing iterates and trial points; C_y(x) is nonsingular and C_y⁻¹ uniformly bounded; derivatives, W_k, H_k, λ_k and u_k are bounded (Assumptions 5.1–5.6, p. 105; they imply Ch. 3's Assumptions 3.1–3.5, p. 105). Algorithmic conditions are split by the strength of the result (pp. 106–107): Condition 5.1 (quasi-normal decrease and ‖s^q‖ ≤ κ‖C_k‖; tangential fraction of Cauchy decrease) for first order. Condition 5.2 (fraction of optimal decrease) and 5.3 (exact Hessian, Lipschitz second derivatives, adjoint multipliers) for second order. Condition 5.4 (take the damped Newton step when it fits in the trust region) for the q-quadratic rate. Second-order limit points need nondegeneracy/strict complementarity (Definition 4.4.2, p. 84; Theorem 5.5.2, p. 118). Admitted to be for convenience or scope: inexactness only in derivatives of C, not f, to keep the presentation simpler (p. 135); no state bounds (p. 15). The square roots in D(x) are needed only for the local quadratic rate. Most global results hold with D^p, p ≥ 1 (p. 84; Remark 5.5.1, p. 121). The claim is that the assumptions reduce to the weakest ones used for unconstrained, equality-constrained and box-constrained optimization (p. 17, p. 165).
  - Transferable technique: tier the algorithmic conditions (Condition 1 → first order; + Condition 2, 3 → second order; + Condition 4 → rate) so that a reader can see which requirement buys which theorem (pp. 106–107).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The global proofs reuse one skeleton, by contradiction, inherited from [35] (p. 62, p. 112). Suppose ‖D_k g_k‖ + ‖C_k‖ (+ γ_k for second order) > tol for all k. Split on ‖C_k‖ ≤ αδ_k versus > αδ_k. In the first case the tangential decrease dominates and pred ≥ κδ_k (Lemmas 5.4.2–5.4.3, pp. 112–113). In the second case the quasi-normal decrease gives pred ≥ κρ‖C_k‖min(‖C_k‖, δ_k) (p. 114). Show the penalty ρ_k stays bounded and δ_k stays away from zero (Lemma 5.4.4 cites [35] Lemmas 7.9–7.13, 8.2, p. 114). Then summing ared ≥ η pred over successful iterations contradicts boundedness of the merit function (pp. 114, 67). Supporting devices:
  - Powell's lemma turns fraction of Cauchy decrease into pred ≥ κ‖g‖min(‖g‖, δ) (Lemma 2.3.1, p. 28). It is re-proved for the scaled, box-damped Cauchy step, where the step length is capped by the distance to the bounds (Lemma 5.3.3, pp. 109–110).
  - For second order: fraction of optimal decrease gives pred ≥ κγ_kδ_k² through the Gay–Sorensen characterization (Proposition 2.3.3, p. 31; Lemma 2.3.2, p. 32; (5.27), p. 101).
  - Ared–pred consistency |ared − pred| ≤ κ(‖s‖² + ρ(‖s‖³ + ‖C‖‖s‖²)) (Lemma 3.5.4, p. 59; Lemma 5.3.6, p. 111).
  - Ch. 3 needs two extra conditions on s^q and the multipliers: ∇ℓᵀs^q = O(δ‖C‖) and Δλ = O(δ) (3.13) (p. 48). In the TRIP setting they come for free from the structure. The y-only quasi-normal step and the adjoint multiplier give ∇_xℓᵀs^q = 0 (Remark 5.2.1, p. 107).
  - Local rate: near a nondegenerate second-order point, ρ_k is bounded, δ_k is bounded away from zero, and all iterations succeed (Theorem 5.6.1, pp. 121–124). The damped Newton step is eventually taken with |σ_k − 1| = O(‖x_k − x*‖), which gives q-quadratic convergence (Corollary 5.6.1, pp. 125–126).
  - Inexactness (Ch. 6): write each inexact solve or product as an exact operation with a perturbed operator, E_A = e_A rᵀ/‖r‖² and A_k, B_k, P_k, Q_k (pp. 139, 150–151, 153). Assume only boundedness of A_k and B_k, a bound on the distance of Q_k s_u to the null space of J_k, and vanishing reduced-gradient error on feasible subsequences (Assumptions 6.1–6.3, p. 140). The exact proof then goes through almost unchanged: the author says it is virtually the same (p. 141). Directional-derivative errors e_k = O(min{‖C_k‖, ‖s_k‖²}) are also allowed (p. 144).
  - No worst-case complexity or iteration counts appear. Results are lim inf stationarity, second-order limit points and a local rate.
  - Transferable technique: when adding inexact inner solves to a proven method, write every inexact computation as an exact computation with a perturbed operator. Then state bounds on the perturbation in terms of quantities the proof already controls (δ_k, ‖C_k‖), and re-check only the lemmas where the perturbation enters (pp. 139–143).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Two discretized optimal control problems. The first is boundary control of a nonlinear heat equation, n = 2100, m = 2000 (pp. 85–86, 131). The second is distributed control of a semilinear elliptic (Bratu-type) equation, m = n − m = 289, plus mesh-refinement instances up to 33 282 variables (pp. 86–87, 160–161, 164). Implementation in Fortran 77 on a Sun Sparcstation 10 (pp. 130, 157). L-BFGS with 5 pairs for reduced or full Hessians (p. 132). Fairly standard radius update (p. 131). Stopping ‖D_kW_kᵀ∇f_k‖ + ‖C_k‖ < 10⁻⁸ (p. 132). There are no external baselines. The comparisons are internal ablations:
  - decoupled vs coupled × reduced vs full Hessian. Iterations 14/20/17/18 and 16/18/17/19 for two regularization values, no rejected steps (Tables 5.2–5.3, pp. 133–134);
  - Coleman–Li vs Dikin–Karmarkar scaling. Dikin–Karmarkar stopped in 11 iterations but at a wrong solution, with variables at the wrong bound, shown with control plots (pp. 133–134);
  - exact vs inexact inner solves (GMRES(10) for the heat problem; left-preconditioned GMRES(20) with multilevel CG for the elliptic one) with tolerances min{ε, 10⁻²min{‖C_k‖, δ_k}} and min{ε, 10⁻²‖C_k‖} (pp. 157–158, 161). Inexactness left the decoupled variants unchanged but hurt the coupled ones (16/18/29/48 on the heat problem; 18/20/27/36(39) on the elliptic one; Table 6.1, p. 158). The mechanism is shown by plotting ‖J_k s^q‖ against ‖J_k(s^q+s^t)‖: errors accumulate when W_k is applied inside every CG iteration (pp. 158–160, 162–163);
  - scaling in problem size. Iterations 18/22/26(31)/49 and the number of state/adjoint solves as the mesh is refined, argued to grow at most polynomially as expected of interior-point methods (Table 6.2, p. 164; argument pp. 162–163).

  The cost unit is iterations plus the number of linearized-state and adjoint solves (Table 5.1, p. 129; Table 6.2, p. 164). The failure case (Dikin–Karmarkar) and the rejected steps (36 accepted of 39, p. 161) are reported.
  - Transferable technique: when a design variant loses, add a diagnostic plot of the quantity your explanation predicts (here, distance of the step from the null space of J_k) rather than only an iteration table (Figs. 6.2 and 6.4, pp. 159–163).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The thesis presents itself as a generalization that contains the known special cases. Figs. 1.1–1.2 map the first- and second-order results onto Powell / Moré–Sorensen (unconstrained), Dennis–El-Alem–Maciel / Dennis–Vicente (equality), Coleman–Li (bounds) (pp. 19–20). The claim is repeated at the start of Ch. 5 (p. 90) and in the conclusions (p. 165). Ch. 3 surveys TR globalizations of SQP and reduced SQP by name (Celis–Dennis–Tapia, Byrd–Omojokon, Conn–Gould–Toint, El-Alem, Lalee–Nocedal–Plantenga and others, pp. 46–47). It then organizes them around four design questions: subproblem, step decomposition, multipliers, merit function (pp. 46–47). Criticism of others is mostly reformulation, with occasional direct notes. QR-based decompositions are problematic for this class because of sparsity, fill-in, work the user does only for the optimizer, and a normal component that touches the bounded u (p. 78). Lalee–Nocedal–Plantenga's nonmonotone penalty scheme came without convergence analysis (p. 54). The inexact-SQP literature studied mostly local rates or line searches, and the two-stage TR step needs different techniques (p. 137). The thesis sides with all-at-once over black-box formulations while showing that the two use the same derivative information (pp. 74–77). Historical credits are given in footnote style: Newton, Raphson and Simpson (p. 24), Cauchy (p. 27), Lagrange and Euler (p. 40), Karush, Kuhn and Tucker (p. 80).
  - Transferable technique: draw a contribution map, with problem classes as nodes and the authors whose results you generalize on each node. Put it in the introduction so the reader sees the positioning before any theorem (Figs. 1.1–1.2, pp. 19–20).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Contributions are enumerated early and concretely: a numbered list of the three ingredients (pp. 16–17) and a numbered list of what is proved (p. 17), followed by the special-case reduction claim (pp. 17–18). The reading contract is explicit. Each chapter opens with an introduction and summary that cite related work and justify the algorithmic choices (p. 21), and Chapters 3–6 follow it (pp. 39–40, 72–73, 88–90, 135–137). Remarks separate what is needed from what is not (Remark 5.2.1, p. 107; Remark 5.5.1, p. 121; Remark 5.7.1, pp. 129–130). Hedging is low but scope is marked plainly: the infinite-dimensional scaling theory is beyond scope (p. 87), and inexactness in f is omitted to keep the presentation simple (p. 135). Theorems outweigh intuition, but each design choice gets one paragraph of geometric or numerical reasoning, e.g. the condition-number argument for coupled trust regions (p. 97) and the gradient-direction dependence of the scaling (p. 89). Two chapters (2 and 3) are presented as review that expert readers may skip (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: after the algorithm statement, give a tiered Conditions block with one sentence mapping each condition to the theorem it enables (pp. 106–107). This is the thesis's way of keeping a long analysis navigable.
- **D8 Contributions & limitations**: The contributions are theoretical, algorithmic and software. On theory: first- and second-order global convergence and q-quadratic local rate for TRIP reduced SQP (pp. 112–126); a second-order result for the general quasi-normal TR reduced SQP family (pp. 60–67); inexactness conditions that keep first-order global convergence (p. 136, p. 166). On algorithms: the TRIP family with decoupled and coupled variants and CG-type step computations (pp. 94–104, 126–130). On software: a solver in Fortran 77 and Matlab, being beta-tested for electronic distribution, already used on fluid-flow control problems (p. 18, p. 166; [76], p. 175). The limitations are specific and ranked. The most important open question is bounds on states: the multiplier then depends on unknown bound multipliers, and the author is not sure affine scaling is the right interior-point approach (pp. 166–167). Other open items: an infinite-dimensional formulation; better step solvers ([129], [133]); testing the coupled approach on ill-conditioned problems; the practical side of inexactness control, such as oversolving and faster local rates, linked to inexact Newton theory; and loss of symmetry in the coupled approach under inexactness (p. 167). The inexact analysis covers first-order convergence only (p. 136, p. 166). The numerics cover two problems (pp. 157–164).
  - Transferable technique: rank the open questions and state why the top one is hard in one equation, here the dependence of λ on the unknown state-bound multipliers (pp. 166–167). Do not list generic extensions.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (Heuristic Inside a Convergent Skeleton): variant ⚠. The same architecture appears one level down: a fast local step (the damped Newton step, Condition 5.4, pp. 106–107) is used whenever it lies in the trust region and meets the Cauchy decrease condition. Convergence comes from a weak acceptance mechanism (ared/pred ratio + fraction of Cauchy decrease, pp. 103–104, 125–126). But the fast step is Newton, not a heuristic, and the glue is a trust-region ratio test, not direct-search sufficient decrease.
- Method 2 (Count Evaluations Against the Gradient Benchmark): variant ⚠. Cost is counted in the expensive oracle (linearized state and adjoint solves per step, Table 5.1, p. 129; Table 6.2, p. 164), but there are no worst-case bounds. Theory stops at lim inf stationarity and the local q-quadratic rate (pp. 114, 125–126). This fits the era effect noted in Method 2's limitations.
- Method 3 (Relax Deterministic Requirements to Probabilistic Ones): variant ⚠ (deterministic ancestor). Exact linear solves are replaced by inexact ones whose accuracy is tied to quantities known at the start of the iteration, the trust radius and the infeasibility: ‖r_k‖ = O(min{δ_k, ‖C_k‖}) and ‖r̂_k‖ = O(‖C_k‖) (p. 136, p. 166). The saving comes from not solving accurately far from feasibility or with a large radius. There is no probability.
- Method 4 (Generalize the Acceptance Test, Not the Algorithm): variant ⚠. Skeleton reuse is strongly evidenced. The merit function, ared/pred and penalty update of Ch. 3 are kept unchanged for the bound-constrained class (p. 103). The first-order proof follows [35] (p. 112), and the inexact proof is virtually the same as the exact one (p. 141). But what changes for the new class is the stationarity measure (D_kW_kᵀ∇f) and the trust-region shape and scaling, not the acceptance test.
- Method 5 (Ship the Solver, Profile It on a Collection): evidence ✅ for the software half and variant ⚠ for the benchmark half. The solver is built with switchable design choices (decoupled/coupled, reduced/full Hessian, scaling type, exact/inexact solves) and those switches are the ablation (pp. 132–134, 157–164). The solver is prepared for distribution (p. 18, p. 166). But testing uses two applied problems plus mesh refinement, with no collection, no external baseline and no profiles (pp. 130–134, 157–164).
- new-pattern candidate: special-case reduction as positioning and correctness check. State the theorem so that it reduces to named results for simpler classes, and check that the assumptions reduce to the weakest known ones (pp. 17–20, 90, 165).
- new-pattern candidate: design around the user's existing oracle. The algorithm needs only operations the application already supplies (state/adjoint solves, matrix-vector products) and nothing that serves only the optimizer (pp. 17, 77–78, 88). This is a derivative-based precursor of the black-box interface discipline of DFO (interpretation; the later work is not in this text).
- new-pattern candidate: inner accuracy tied to trust radius and infeasibility (p. 136, p. 166). This may be the deterministic root of Method 3 and of radius-dependent model accuracy in later DFO work (interpretation, not stated in the thesis).
- new-pattern candidate: backward-error representation of inexactness, rewriting inexact computations as exact ones with perturbed operators A_k, B_k so the exact proof carries over (pp. 139, 150–153).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Set inner-solver tolerances from quantities available at the start of the iteration: tol_state = min{ε, 10⁻²·min{‖C_k‖, δ_k}}, tol_adjoint = min{ε, 10⁻²·‖C_k‖}. Prove the O(min{δ_k, ‖C_k‖}) and O(‖C_k‖) conditions once, then implement them literally (pp. 136, 157).
- Represent each inexact linear-algebra operation as an exact one with a rank-one perturbed operator (E = e rᵀ/‖r‖²). Assume only that the perturbation is bounded and vanishes along feasible subsequences, and reuse the exact proof (pp. 139–141, 150–154).
- Decompose the step into a feasibility component and an optimality component, and impose only a fraction of Cauchy decrease on each. Any solver that minimizes first along the steepest-descent direction (dogleg, truncated CG, GMRES on the linearized constraints) then fits the theory without new proofs (pp. 95, 99, 129, 144–149).
- The TR contradiction skeleton: assume the stationarity measure > tol; split on ‖C_k‖ ≤ αδ_k; lower-bound pred by κδ_k; bound the penalty parameter; bound δ_k away from zero; sum the actual decreases to contradict a bounded merit function (pp. 62–67, 112–115).
- Draw a contribution-map figure of problem classes and prior results that your theorem generalizes, and put it in the introduction (Figs. 1.1–1.2, pp. 19–20).
- For interior-point or scaling choices, run the natural alternative and show the solution, not only the iteration count. A faster method that ends at a wrong bound is a result worth reporting (pp. 133–134).
- Report a mesh-refinement table with iterations and counts of the expensive solves to support a size-independence or polynomial-growth claim (Table 6.2, p. 164).
