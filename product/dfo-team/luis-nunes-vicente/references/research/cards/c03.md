# Paper cards · batch c03 (core)

Batch intent: Look at Vicente's 2007–2009 foundation work in derivative-free optimization on its two sides. The direct-search side is SIAM J. Optim. 2007 with Custódio (SID-PSM: reuse past evaluations through simplex derivatives inside pattern search). The model-based side is the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente (CSV) trilogy: Λ-poisedness for interpolation sets (Math. Program. 2008), its extension to regression and underdetermined interpolation (IMA J. Numer. Anal. 2008), and the general derivative-free trust-region convergence theory built on fully linear and fully quadratic models (SIAM J. Optim. 2009). Four questions. (a) In this era, what is the *glue* of Method 1: an integer-lattice mesh, sufficient decrease, or certified model quality? (b) How does Vicente abstract a component (a geometry constant, a model class) so that the theory does not depend on how practitioners implement it? (c) Where do Method 2 (counting against a benchmark) and Method 5 (solver, collection, profiles) appear, and where are they absent? (d) How does the model-geometry theory flow into the direct-search work?
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all; core batch). Extra weight on D2 (what was kept and what was changed), D3 (which assumptions are admitted to be for convenience), D4 (named proof devices) and D6 (how Powell's practical methods are treated).
Material roles: S008 core (SIAM J. Optim. 2007) · S010 core (Math. Program. 2008) · S020 core (IMA J. Numer. Anal. 2008) · S006 core (SIAM J. Optim. 2009).

### S008 · Using sampling and simplex derivatives in pattern search methods (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2007, DOI 10.1137/050646706)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. L. Custódio · **Position in trajectory**: Custódio was at FCT-UNL and Vicente at Coimbra (p. 1). The paper brings the sample-set geometry (Λ-poisedness) of Vicente's concurrent CSV work into direct search. S020 and S010 are cited as [6] and [7] for the simplex-gradient error bounds and for Λ-poisedness (p. 6, p. 19). The paper was revised while Custódio visited William & Mary; Lewis, Torczon and Trosset are thanked (p. 20). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–20), including all tables.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an *observed phenomenon*. Plotted against evaluations, f in pattern-search runs has an L-shape: fast early decrease, then slow progress near stationarity, where unsuccessful iterations become frequent (p. 1). The second entry is a gap in the literature: "There has not been much effort" on efficient serial implementations of pattern search. Prior work had used parallelism, curvature-based rotation of generating sets, partial separability, user-provided points and incomplete gradients (p. 1). The problem is unconstrained min f with no structure assumed, so black-box codes and physical experiments are covered (p. 1–2). The goal is to improve the current iteration with evaluations already made in earlier iterations (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The base is Audet–Dennis generalized pattern search. It is written with three procedures left open, [search], [order] and [mesh] (Fig. 2.1, p. 3–4). The new algorithm (Fig. 4.1, p. 8) adds a [store] procedure and one step at the start of each iteration:
  - keep a list X_k of evaluated points (at most p_max): either *store-succ* (successful iterates only) or *store-all* (every evaluated point) (p. 8–9);
  - inside B(x_k; Δ_k), with Δ_k = σ_k α_{k−1} max‖b‖, find a Λ-poised subset Y_k that contains x_k (p. 8);
  - from Y_k, compute a simplex gradient or a simplex gradient plus a diagonal simplex Hessian (2n+1 points). The simplex gradient is determined, underdetermined or overdetermined, via the reduced SVD of the scaled S^T (p. 5–7). This gives a descent indicator d_k = −∇_s f or −H⁻¹g (p. 9);
  - [order]: poll the directions in decreasing cos(d_k, b) (Fig. 4.2, p. 9);
  - [mesh] variant *mesh-sd*: after a success, expand only if the ratio ρ_k of actual to simplex-model decrease exceeds ξ; otherwise keep α (Fig. 7.1, p. 13–14);
  - a search step that minimizes a model is only sketched: "The use of surrogate models in the search step is the topic of a separate research" (p. 13). The tests use "a very crude search step" along d_k (p. 14).

  Kept: the mesh/lattice, the poll, positive spanning sets, and acceptance by *simple* decrease f(x_{k+1}) < f(x_k) (p. 3–4). Changed: only the procedures that were left open. Why: the add-ons cost no evaluations and do not touch the convergence requirements (Quote 2, p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Convergence is inherited. A compact level set gives lim inf α_k = 0 (Torczon; Thm 2.1, p. 4–5). Strict differentiability at the limit, or C¹ f, gives stationarity (Thm 2.2, p. 5). The mesh rules need a rational τ and integer exponents (Fig. 2.2, p. 4), and a finite number of positive bases (p. 2, p. 13). Simplex-gradient accuracy needs a Lipschitz ∇f (Thm 3.1, p. 6). For this paper, Λ-poisedness is simplified to ‖Σ⁻¹‖ ≤ Λ (p. 6). Admitted gap: the pruning condition (6.2) on α_k may fail asymptotically, and it depends on an unknown Lipschitz constant (p. 12–13).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There is no new convergence theorem. The argument is that the add-ons sit in slots that the proof does not use. [search] and [order] carry no requirements (p. 3–4). mesh-sd keeps integer powers of τ and so "has no influence on the global convergence properties" (p. 13). There are two new results.
  - **Thm 5.1, geometry for free** (p. 10–11): after an unsuccessful iteration under store-all, x_k plus the |B_{k−1}| poll points (or |B_{k−1}|−1 of them) is Λ-poised, with Λ independent of k. Device: S_k = α_{k−1}B, and dividing by Δ_k = σ_k α_{k−1} max‖b‖ cancels α. With finitely many positive bases, take Λ as the maximum over them (p. 10–11). The |B|−1 case uses the fact that a positive spanning set minus one vector still spans linearly (Davis, p. 10–11).
  - **Thm 6.1, pruning** (p. 11–12): if α_k is small relative to ‖∇f(x_k)‖∞/(√q γ Λ σ_max max‖b‖), then −∇_s f is an ε_k-approximation to the large components of −∇f. By Abramson–Audet–Dennis Thm 4, it then prunes the poll set to a singleton (p. 12). Device: the error bound of Thm 3.1, combined with sign agreement whenever the error is at most half the largest gradient component (p. 12).
  - The authors relate this to the cosine-measure bound ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≤ γκ(B_k)⁻¹ max‖b‖ α_k at unsuccessful iterations. They add that α_k may go to zero faster than ‖∇f‖ (p. 12–13).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Setup: Matlab; 27 unconstrained CUTEr problems gathered from DFO papers, n = 6–20 (Table 8.1, p. 14–15). Stop when α < 10⁻⁵ or after 100000 iterations. Λ = 100, α₀ = 1, contraction 0.5, expansion 2, ξ = 0.75 (p. 14).
  - Bases: the two positive bases were picked by tuning the *baseline*. [I −I] was best for the basic version among several bases and orderings, and [−e I] was the best minimal basis (p. 14).
  - Baselines: "basic" (no search, α unchanged after success, fixed poll order), plus two published heuristics, dynamic polling (Audet–Dennis) and mesh-HKT (Hough–Kolda–Torczon) (p. 13–14).
  - Scale: 120 versions, 112 of them with simplex derivatives. The complete results are in a separate report [9] (p. 14).
  - Metrics (Table 8.3, p. 16): average % change in evaluations vs basic; the 75th percentile of that change; % of iterations with a poised set; cumulative % of problems reaching optimal gaps 10⁻⁷ / 10⁻⁴ / 10⁻¹. There are **no performance profiles**.
  - Results: 91% of the simplex-derivative versions reduce evaluations on average, and 61 of 112 reduce them on 75% of the problems (p. 14–15). order (store-all) gives −51%, against −11% for dynamic polling. The best version, order + mesh-HKT, gives −54% (p. 17).
  - Extras: n = 40 and 80, with unchanged conclusions. The gains depend on the number of poll vectors and the cosine measure, not on n (p. 17–18). A ξ sensitivity check (p. 18). Seven random quadratics with n = 10: the simplex gradient made an acute angle with the true gradient in 77% of computations (p. 18).
  - Pruning test: it deliberately violates the theory's conditions and says so. Pruning to one direction lost quality; pruning to all acute-angle directions saved 10% (store-succ) and 42% (store-all) (p. 18).
  - Negative results shown: mesh-HKT alone +4.02% and mesh-sd (store-all) +0.45% (Table 8.3, p. 16). Table 8.4 shows brownal n = 20 ending at f = 1.00e+00 under order + mesh-HKT, against 6.16e-06 under order alone. The text does not discuss this row (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on Audet–Dennis GPS [3] and the Kolda–Lewis–Torczon review [17] (p. 3, p. 5). Criticism takes the form of pointing out a gap. Lewis–Torczon ordered simplex vertices only to find the best and worst ones: "The authors explicitly state in [18] that their intention was not reordering" the rest. And "Most papers do not address the issue of poll ordering at all" (p. 3). Prior uses of simplex gradients are listed neutrally: Bortz–Kelley (implicit filtering), Tseng (stopping test), Kelley (Nelder–Mead stagnation) (p. 5). Pruning is placed on top of Abramson–Audet–Dennis (p. 11–12). Self-citations: Alberto et al. (user-provided points) and the CSV geometry reports [6, 7] (p. 1, p. 6).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The motivation opens with an observed curve shape (Quote 1, p. 1). The contribution is stated as two checkable properties, (i) and (ii) (p. 2). The algorithm diff is made visible: "All modifications to the algorithm reported in Figure 2.1 are marked in italics in Figure 4.1" (p. 7), and the open procedures are written in [brackets] (p. 3). Hedges are local and concrete: "A detailed numerical study of these asymptotic behaviors is beyond the scope of this paper" (p. 13); "More research is needed" on pruning (p. 18). The recommendations in the conclusion depend on the basis type (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and empirical, with two supporting theoretical results. The limits are concrete: the pruning condition is not guaranteed (p. 12); the pruning test violates the assumptions (p. 18); the search step is not developed (p. 13). Future work: parallel pattern search; simplex gradients for nonsmooth functions [8]; a model-based search step (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The shape is the same: add efficient ingredients (simplex-derivative ordering, mesh-sd) in slots the convergence proof does not use (p. 2–4, p. 13). But the glue is the rational-lattice mesh with *simple* decrease (Fig. 2.2, p. 4; p. 3), not sufficient decrease. Sufficient decrease appears only in deciding mesh *expansion* (Fig. 7.1, p. 14). **Correction for SKILL.md *Signature Work Anatomy***: in this paper the model-based search step is deferred to "separate research" (p. 13) and is not implemented, and the paper does not name the SID-PSM software.
- Research Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence. Past evaluations are reused through simplex gradients to order the poll, at no extra cost (p. 7–9). This is the largest gain in the paper (−51%, p. 17).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The collection is stated (27 CUTEr problems) and the baselines include the strongest published heuristics. But results are given as tables, not profiles, the full results are moved to a report (p. 14–17), and the paper says nothing about releasing code.
- Method 2: no link (no complexity count).
- New-pattern candidate: *zero-extra-evaluation add-ons in the proof's free slots* (p. 2, p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate: *import model-geometry theory into direct search*: Λ-poisedness and the CSV error bounds control the simplex gradient (p. 6, p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Rewrite the base algorithm with its theory-free slots as named procedures ([search], [order], [mesh]). Then put each contribution into a slot and show that the slot's requirements are still met (p. 3–4, p. 13).
- [D4] Choose the sampling radius proportional to the previous step size (Δ_k = σ_k α_{k−1} max‖b‖). The scaled geometry of the last poll then does not depend on α, and the poisedness constant is a maximum over finitely many bases (Thm 5.1, p. 10–11).
- [D5] Ablation grid with honest aggregates: report the average % change in evaluations vs the baseline, its 75th percentile, the % of iterations where the add-on was active, and cumulative optimal-gap thresholds, all in one table (Table 8.3, p. 16). Put the full grid in a companion report (p. 14).
- [D5] Diagnostic experiment for the mechanism: on random quadratics, measure how often the surrogate direction is a true descent direction (acute angle in 77% of cases, p. 18).
- [D7] Mark the changes to the algorithm box in italics against the base algorithm (Fig. 4.1, p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "The curve representing the objective function value as a function of the number of function evaluations frequently exhibits an L-shape for pattern search runs." (p. 1)
- "More importantly, these strategies (i) require no extra function evaluation and (ii) do not interfere with existing requirements for global convergence." (p. 2)

### S010 · Geometry of interpolation sets in derivative free optimization (Mathematical Programming 2008, DOI 10.1007/s10107-006-0073-5)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, K. Scheinberg (IBM T.J. Watson) · **Position in trajectory**: Vicente wrote most of the paper while visiting IBM T.J. Watson (p. 1). It is the first CSV geometry paper, and the regression/underdetermined sequel (S020) is announced as "an upcoming paper" (p. 2). S008 uses its Λ-poisedness (S008 p. 6). S006 calls its LU/QR procedures as model-improvement algorithms (S006 p. 11–12). **Read**: full text, including the appendix on the cubic case (p. 24–25).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a *theory gap under existing practice*. For DFO sampling methods to reuse derivative-based convergence theory, their models need Taylor-like error bounds, and the geometry constant Λ_Y must stay uniformly bounded (p. 2). The existing ways of handling this are "not very satisfactory" (p. 18):
  - Powell's Lagrange-polynomial updates come with no proof of a uniform bound (p. 3, p. 5, p. 19);
  - the Newton-polynomial method of Conn–Scheinberg–Toint (the authors' own earlier work) needs an expensive step that replaces every point (p. 6, p. 19);
  - Ciarlet–Raviart's "stretchedness" factor can mislead: small perturbations of six points on a circle are nearly nonpoised yet have small stretchedness (p. 6).

  The stated purpose is "to provide the necessary theoretical foundation" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: There are three moves (p. 3).
  1. A geometric re-definition. Y is Λ-poised in B(1) if every φ(x), x ∈ B(1), can be written as Σλ_i φ(y^i) with ‖λ‖ ≤ Λ. The λ_i are exactly the Lagrange polynomials, whatever the basis (Lemma 3.1, Def. 3.2, p. 6–7).
  2. Link Λ to the condition number of the Vandermonde-type matrix M in the natural (factorial-scaled) monomial basis, after scaling Y into the unit ball. The condition number is meaningless without a fixed basis and scale (p. 4, p. 7–8, p. 11).
  3. Certify-and-repair algorithms: LU (Alg. 5.1) and QR/Gram–Schmidt (Alg. 5.2) factorizations of M. A point is accepted if its pivot-polynomial value is at least ξ ∈ (0, 1/4). Otherwise it is replaced by a maximizer of the pivot polynomial on the ball (p. 20–23). Unlike earlier approaches, only the "bad" points are replaced, never the whole set, but the polynomials must be recomputed (p. 19).

  Kept: polynomial interpolation models, with Lagrange-polynomial bounds as the quality measure (p. 3). Changed: the measure is made computable through factorization pivots.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Without loss of generality, the smallest enclosing ball is centred at the origin, first with radius 1 and then Δ (p. 6, p. 11). y⁰ = 0 is the current iterate, which is "very natural in a DFO setting" (p. 11). Linear case: f is C¹ with Lipschitz ∇f (γ_L) on an open Ω ⊃ B(Δ) (p. 12). Quadratic case: f is C² with Lipschitz ∇²f (γ_Q) (p. 14). The natural basis is a choice, and θ depends on it (Remark 3.1, p. 10–11). The algorithms cover degree ≤ 2; the constant θ for higher degree "is beyond the scope of this paper" (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Two-sided equivalence (Thm 3.3, p. 8–9)**: ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ Λ ⇒ √p₁Λ-poised, and Λ-poised ⇒ ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ θΛ. The converse has three steps. (i) M is nonsingular, because a polynomial that vanishes on a ball is zero. (ii) ψ(v) = max_{x∈B(1)} |vᵀφ(x)| is a norm, so norm equivalence gives some σ > 0 with ψ(v) ≥ σ‖v‖. (iii) An SVD orthogonality argument compares ‖M^{-T}v̄‖ with ‖M^{-T}φ(y)‖ (p. 8–9).
  - **Lemma 3.4 (p. 9–10)**: a quadratic with ‖v‖∞ = 1 has max|·| ≥ 1/4 on the unit ball. The proof sets every variable not in the largest term to zero and checks at most five points. This gives θ ≤ 4√p₁ (3.9) (p. 10). The same 1/4 guarantees that a pivot threshold ξ < 1/4 can always be met, and it reduces the pivot maximization to "a trivial enumeration" (p. 21).
  - **Scaling (Lemma 3.5, p. 11)**: Lagrange polynomials are scale-invariant but ‖M⁻¹‖ is not, so scale Y by 1/Δ before measuring.
  - **Error bounds (Thms 4.1, 4.2, p. 13–16)**: subtract the Taylor expansion at x from each interpolation condition. Subtracting the y⁰ = 0 equation then cancels the unknown function error e^f(x), and the resulting matrix "does not depend on the point x" (p. 13, p. 15). Scaling by diag(Δ, Δ²) gives a Δ-free matrix. The constants are explicit. Gradient error ≤ (5√n γ_L ‖L̂⁻¹‖/2)Δ (p. 13). Quadratic constants: α^H = 3√2/2, α^g = 3(1+√2)/2, α^f = (6+9√2)/4, β^f = 1/6 (p. 16). The cubic case is sketched in the appendix (p. 24–25).
  - **Sharpness claim**: written in terms of M̂, their constant grows like √p₁, where the Ciarlet–Raviart bound grows like p₁ (p. 17). They also bound the derivatives of the Lagrange polynomials: ‖Dλ(x)‖∞ ≤ 2θΛ (p. 18).
  - **Cost**: O(n⁶) flops for the LU version (p. 21), and ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ c(p₁)ε_growth/ξ (p. 19, p. 21, p. 23).
- **D5 Experiments**: There is one small Matlab test. Take 100 random points in the unit cube, and select interpolation sets by Powell's Lagrange maximization, by LU and by QR. ‖M⁻¹‖ was mostly 10–100 and never above 1000 (p. 24). The authors warn that this "may be the consequence of the random nature of the chosen interpolation points", and that a practical test needs a real DFO package (p. 24). There are no optimization runs.
- **D6 Positioning**: Powell [16, 17] and the authors' own earlier CST work [7] get the same kind of critique: the method is reasonable in practice, but there is "no proof" of uniform poisedness (p. 18–19). Powell's rule is conceded to "seems to work well in practice" (p. 19). Ciarlet–Raviart [4] is the classical bound that they re-derive in optimization terms (p. 3, p. 6, p. 17). de Boor's Gaussian elimination is set apart by its motivation: fixed data there, sample sets that change during optimization here (p. 19). A bridge to direct search: keeping one good set and shifting and scaling it "is closely related to the use of a finite number of positive bases in pattern search methods" (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper says openly that the value of the error-bound section is not novelty (Quote 1, p. 17). It calls its own proof "in our opinion, very simple and intuitive" (p. 11). The order is definitions → equivalence → bounds → algorithms → practical considerations (p. 3). A concrete counterexample (six points on a circle) motivates the new definition (p. 6). Hedges are honest about the competition: they concede that the new algorithms may not beat Powell's (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory plus maintenance algorithms. Limits: the polynomials are recomputed at every update (p. 19); O(n⁶) cost (p. 21); the practical value is untested inside a DFO code (p. 23–24). Future work: prove uniform well-poisedness for Powell-type algorithms, "a subject of a separate study" (p. 24); the regression and underdetermined cases (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The practice is kept: one-point updates of the interpolation set, as in Powell's codes. A certificate (the pivot threshold) is added to make the uniform bound provable, so the methods are "both practical and theoretically sound" (p. 19). The *heuristic* here is a rule for managing points, not a trial-point generator. This matches Research Taste 1 (*Rigorous and efficient at the same time*).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. Constants and dimension factors are explicit (√n, √p₁, O(n⁶)) (p. 13, p. 16, p. 21), but they bound approximation error and linear-algebra cost, not the number of evaluations.
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (era/genre). There is no solver and no problem collection, only one test on 100 random points (p. 24).
- New-pattern candidate: *two-sided equivalence between the classical measure and a computable one* (Thm 3.3, p. 8). This recurs in S020.
- New-pattern candidate: *certify-and-repair component abstraction*: a threshold test either certifies quality or replaces the offending point (p. 19–23). S006 reuses it as the model-improvement algorithm, and S020 extends it.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] When a quality measure depends on the basis, fix a well-scaled basis, scale the data to the unit ball, and prove that the classical measure and the computable one agree up to constants in both directions (Thm 3.3, p. 8–9).
- [D4] Norm-equivalence trick for lower bounds: ψ(v) = max_{x∈B(1)} |vᵀφ(x)| is a norm, so it is ≥ σ‖v‖. Then get σ explicitly by restricting to ≤ 2 variables and checking a few points (Lemma 3.4, p. 9–10).
- [D4] Deriving an error bound: Taylor-expand at the evaluation point x and subtract the equation of the centre point, so that the unknown function error cancels. The system matrix then does not depend on x (p. 12–15).
- [D2] Set the acceptance threshold of a repair algorithm below a proven attainable value (1/4). A repair point then always exists, and only the offending points are replaced (Alg. 5.1–5.2, p. 20–23).

**Quotes**:
- "We would like to emphasize that the goal of this section is not so much the novelty of the bounds, in general, but their usefulness for the reader with primarily optimization motivations, not to mention the simplicity of the derivation of these bounds." (p. 17)
- "In practice, it is more efficient to maintain well-poisedness throughout the algorithm, not just when it is necessary to pass the criterion needed for the convergence proof." (p. 18)

### S020 · Geometry of sample sets in derivative-free optimization: Polynomial regression and underdetermined interpolation (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2008, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drn046)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, K. Scheinberg · **Position in trajectory**: The sequel to S010 (cited as [8], p. 1). It cites the book *Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization* as "to appear" [9] and S006 as "under final review" [10] (p. 28). It cites S008 [12] as a user of regression simplex gradients (p. 1). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–28).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Two gaps where practice is ahead of theory (p. 1–2).
  - Regression is used (implicit filtering; regression simplex gradients) and fits cheap but noisy evaluations, yet "there has been little other work employing regression". The authors blame the lack of supporting theory (p. 1).
  - Underdetermined (minimum-norm or minimum-Frobenius) interpolation is widely used (the DFO code; Powell [17]), but "the theory in [8] does not immediately extend" (p. 2).

  A third motive: approximation-theory generalizations of Lagrange polynomials are "ill-suited for an optimization audience" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The S010 machinery is kept, and only one thing changes: which solution of the λ-system defines the Lagrange polynomials.
  - Regression (p > q): the Lagrange regression polynomials are the minimum-ℓ₂-norm solution of Mᵀλ(x) = φ(x) (Lemma 2.4, p. 9). m(x) = Σ f(y^i)L_i(x) still holds (Lemma 2.3, p. 8). Regression Λ-poisedness requires some λ with ‖λ‖ ≤ Λ (Def. 2.6, p. 9).
  - Underdetermined (p < q): the minimum-norm Lagrange polynomials are the least-squares solution of Σλ_iφ(y^i) = φ(x) (Lemma 5.4, p. 22). The paper calls this "a kind of duality" (p. 22).
  - For the underdetermined case, two candidate definitions are compared: *sub-basis* (choose the p₁ columns with the best geometry; Def. 5.1, p. 19) and *minimum-norm in the natural basis* (Def. 5.5, p. 22). Example 5.2 decides between them (p. 19–20). As ε → 0 two sample sets converge to each other, but their best-sub-basis models stay far apart ([0, b1, b2, 0] vs [0, b1, 0, b2]). The minimum-norm solutions of both converge to [0, b1, b2/2, b2/2]. The natural basis is justified by four reasons (Remark 5.1, p. 23).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The same scaling and y⁰ = 0 as in S010 (p. 10, p. 14), with C¹ or C² f and a Lipschitz ∇f or ∇²f (p. 15). The minimum-norm solution "depends on the choice of φ" (p. 20). The definition is admitted to depend on the basis, and it is fixed to φ̄ (Remark 5.1, p. 23). Remark 2.1: the Lagrange extension fails for ℓ₁ and ℓ∞ regression, because those fits are not linear in f_Y (p. 8). Noise: with i.i.d. zero-mean noise, the regression models of the noisy and the true f converge as the number of points grows (p. 17, citing [9]).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Lemma 1.1 (p. 4)**, max-determinant subset: among m vectors in ℝⁿ, choose the n with the largest |det|. By Cramer's rule every other vector has expansion coefficients ≤ 1. So any combination with |λ_i| ≤ Λ can be rewritten on the subset with coefficients ≤ (m − n + 1)Λ. The proof is credited to D. Coppersmith (p. 27).
  - **Thm 2.7 (p. 10)**: regression Λ-poised ⇒ some q₁-subset is (p − q + 1)√q₁Λ-poised for interpolation; the converse is immediate.
  - **Thm 2.8 (p. 11–13)**: equivalence with ‖Σ⁻¹‖ of the reduced SVD. The proof is "very similar" to S010 Thm 3.3, with "a few extra steps" (p. 11), and θ ≤ 4√q₁ (p. 13).
  - **Thm 5.6 (p. 22–23)**: sub-basis and minimum-norm poisedness agree up to the factors √(p₁q₁)θ and (q − p + 1)√(p₁q₁)θ, again through Lemma 1.1.
  - **Error bounds**: the regression bounds (Thms 3.1–3.2) are stated without proof as "straightforward adaptations" (p. 14–16). The underdetermined bound (Thm 5.8) controls only the projection Vᵀ of the error, "which seems reasonable since we do not have enough information to do better" (p. 26). It recovers the full-interpolation bound when p = q (p. 26).
- **D5 Experiments**: Toy 2-D tests (p. 16–17, p. 26–27).
  - Setup: three functions f1–f3, of which only f1 is reported, "since the results for the others seem to follow the same pattern" (p. 16). p random points in the unit square, plus the origin.
  - Comparisons: best-subset interpolation (best condition number, found exhaustively) vs least-squares regression (Fig. 4.1, p. 17); best-basis vs minimum-norm (Figs. 5.1–5.2, p. 27).
  - Protocol: errors measured on a 0.1 lattice, as E1 (max) and E2 (ℓ₂ sum), over 200 repetitions. A win counts only if one model beats the other by > 0.001 in both errors (p. 17, p. 26).
  - Findings: neither regression nor best subset is consistently better, but best subset is worse more often (138 vs 28 runs at 21 points) (p. 17). Minimum-norm beats best-basis, and it was "consistently better" on the square of radius 0.01 (p. 26–27).
  - An oddity the paper does not explain: in Fig. 5.1 the 5-point row lists BB error 81950 and MN error 653000, although BB was worse in 133 runs and MN in 28 (p. 27).
  - The claimed benefit of regression for spiky functions "has not been verified experimentally" (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper is a direct continuation of S010 [8] (p. 1). Approximation theory (Ciarlet–Raviart [4], Davis [13]) is respected but restated: definitions "in simple terms" with "straightforward proofs" (p. 7). Remark 5.2 relates Powell's minimum-Frobenius Lagrange polynomials to their Definition 5.2 with a modified norm on the second-order terms (p. 23–24). Deng–Ferris' UOBYQA with repeated sampling for noise is the contrast case for regression on random points (p. 27).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper puts examples before definitions (Examples 5.1–5.2, p. 18–20). It admits a "definition defect" (Λ-poisedness is written with a basis it does not depend on) and keeps it because it is useful (p. 10). The numerical sections are labelled "very limited" (p. 26) and "simple" (p. 3). Proofs that are adaptations are omitted (p. 14, p. 26). Judgements are hedged with "in our opinion" (p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory; the algorithms are deferred to the book (p. 27). Limits: a separate computational study is needed to show that regression helps (Quote 2, p. 3); θ grows "most likely, exponentially" with the degree (p. 13). Future work: algorithms that maintain the regression set directly, not through an interpolation subset (p. 14); for noisy f, regression on random sample points instead of repeated sampling at the same point (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant, at the level of model theory. The skeleton is kept (λ-system, Λ-poisedness, condition-number equivalence, Taylor bounds), and one object changes: which generalized solution defines λ(x). This covers two new cases (p. 8–9, p. 22). It is not an acceptance test, but it is the same move: change the smallest object, then re-derive.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] To pass from a large set to a well-conditioned subset, pick the subset with the largest |det|. Cramer's rule then bounds every expansion coefficient by 1 (Lemma 1.1, p. 4).
- [D2] To choose between two candidate definitions, build a pair of inputs that converge to each other and show that one definition's output jumps while the other's converges (Example 5.2, p. 19–20).
- [D4] Extend a definition by picking the matching generalized inverse: minimum-norm λ for overdetermined data, least-squares λ for underdetermined data (p. 9, p. 22).
- [D5] A protocol for toy comparisons: 200 random sample sets; a win counts only above a fixed margin in two error measures; repeat on a shrunken region to see the local regime (p. 17, p. 26–27).

### S006 · Global convergence of general derivative-free trust-region algorithms to first- and second-order critical points (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2009, DOI 10.1137/060673424)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, K. Scheinberg · **Position in trajectory**: The algorithmic payoff of the geometry papers S010 and S020, cited as [4] and [5] (p. 2, p. 11–13), It cites the CSV book [6] (2009) in its references (p. 29). The acknowledgement says that Michael Powell's comments "led to an improved version of Step 1, to Lemma 3.2, and to the insertion of Lemma 5.5" (p. 28). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–29).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry *splits the literature into two camps* (p. 2). On one side are practical trust-region DFO methods (Powell; the Marazzi–Nocedal wedge method) with no convergence theory. On the other are provable methods obtained "at the expense of practicality" (the Conn–Gould–Toint (CGT) book; Conn–Scheinberg–Toint 1997). The paper aims to bridge the two (Quote 1, p. 2). The problem is unconstrained min f, with f C¹ or C² and bounded below (p. 4). The new issues listed are acceptance on simple decrease, keeping the trust-region radius in the criticality step, and second-order global convergence (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Model building is abstracted into classes** (Defs. 3.1 and 3.3, p. 7–9). A class is fully linear (fully quadratic) if (1) Taylor-like bounds hold with uniform constants on every B(x; Δ), Δ ≤ Δ_max, and (2) a model-improvement algorithm exists that, in a finite and uniformly bounded number of steps, either certifies such a model or produces one (p. 7–8). Interpolation on Λ-poised sets and regression models are shown to be members (p. 11–13).
  - **Algorithm 4.1** (p. 13–14):
    - Criticality step (Alg. 4.2, p. 14): if ‖g^icb‖ ≤ c, and the model is not certifiably fully linear or Δ > μ‖g‖, improve the model on the shrinking balls α^{i−1}Δ until Δ̃ ≤ μ‖g̃‖. Then set Δ_k = min{max{Δ̃, β‖g̃‖}, Δ^icb}.
    - Step: a fraction of Cauchy decrease.
    - Acceptance: accept if ρ ≥ η₁, or if ρ ≥ η₀ and the model is fully linear. With η₀ = 0, simple decrease is enough (p. 14).
    - Model-improvement step when ρ < η₁. The radius shrinks only if the model is fully linear; otherwise it stays the same (p. 14).
  - One trust region is kept. Powell's two trust regions make the method "more complicated" (p. 2).
  - **Second order** (Alg. 6.1, p. 21–22): σ^m = max{‖g‖, −λ_min(H)} replaces ‖g‖. The step must give a fraction of both the Cauchy and the eigenstep decrease (Assumption 2.2, p. 6). The radius must grow only on successful iterations with Δ_k < βσ^m_k (p. 22), whereas classical methods increase it on every success (p. 3, p. 21).

  Kept: the classical trust-region machinery (Cauchy step, ratio test, radius update). Changed: the model requirements, the criticality step, the acceptance rule, and model-improving iterations that leave x unchanged.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Assumption 3.1: f is C¹ with Lipschitz ∇f on the enlarged level set L_enl(x₀) = ∪_{x∈L(x₀)} B(x; Δ_max), because sampling may leave L(x₀) (p. 7).
  - Assumption 3.2: f is C² with Lipschitz Hessian (p. 9).
  - Assumption 5.1: f is bounded below on L(x₀) (p. 16).
  - Assumption 5.2: model Hessians are uniformly bounded, "For simplicity of the presentation" (p. 16). This one is admitted to be for convenience.
  - Without loss of generality, the constants are taken large enough that full linearity on a small ball carries over to larger balls (Lemma 3.2, p. 8; Lemma 3.4, p. 9).
  - The abstraction admits impractical members, and the paper says so: M = {f} itself, and finite-difference Taylor models rebuilt "from scratch" for every x and Δ (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The first-order skeleton (p. 15–21):
  - Lemma 5.1: the criticality loop terminates unless ∇f(x_k) = 0. Otherwise ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≤ (κ_eg + 1/μ)α^{i−1}Δ → 0 (p. 15–16).
  - Lemma 5.2: if the model is fully linear and Δ_k ≤ min{1/κ_bhm, κ_fcd(1 − η₁)/(4κ_ef)}‖g_k‖, the iteration is successful, via |ρ_k − 1| ≤ 4κ_efΔ²/(κ_fcd‖g_k‖Δ) (p. 16–17).
  - Lemma 5.3: ‖g_k‖ ≥ κ₁ for all k ⇒ Δ_k ≥ κ₂ (p. 17).
  - Lemma 5.4: finitely many successes ⇒ lim ‖∇f‖ = 0. The proof uses a uniform bound N on consecutive model-improving iterations (p. 17–18).
  - **Lemma 5.5** (the DFO-specific step): Δ_k → 0 always. The criticality step gives ‖g_k‖ ≥ min{c, μ⁻¹Δ_k}, and each success decreases f by a Cauchy fraction (p. 18).
  - Lemmas 5.6–5.7 give Thm 5.8 (lim inf). Thm 5.9 gives the lim result. It uses two interleaved subsequences k_i < ℓ_i with ε ≤ min{ε₀/(2(2 + κ_egμ)), c}, and the telescoping bound ‖x_{k_i} − x_{ℓ_i}‖ ≤ 2/(η₁κ_fcd)·[f(x_{k_i}) − f(x_{ℓ_i})] (p. 19–21).
  - Remark 5.1: keeping x fixed at model-improving iterations is needed only for the lim result (p. 21).

  The second-order proof (p. 23–28):
  - Prop. 7.1 is an eigenvalue perturbation bound, |λ_min(∇²f) − λ_min(∇²m)| ≤ κ_ehΔ (p. 23), and Lemma 7.2 gives |σ − σ^m| ≤ κ_σΔ (p. 24).
  - Lemma 7.4 splits into a gradient-dominant case and a curvature-dominant case (p. 24–25).
  - Thm 7.11 uses the forced increase on S₊ and a geometric-series bound ‖x_{k_i} − x_{ℓ_i}‖ ≤ γ_inc/(γ_inc − 1)·Δ_{j*} (p. 27–28).

  There are no rates and no complexity bounds (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The paper has no numerical section.
- **D6 Positioning**: The CGT book *Trust-Region Methods* [2] is the reference throughout. The criticality step comes from Conn–Scheinberg–Toint [3] (p. 2). Powell [12] is the practical model, with two trust regions (p. 2), and his geometry-improvement step is noted as the analogue of the criticality step (p. 15). Yuan's non-convergence example [14] motivates the radius rules (p. 2), and Thomas [13] is cited for lim-type results (p. 2). The priority claim is hedged with "As far as we are aware": the first comprehensive analysis of second-order global convergence for derivative-free trust-region methods (p. 3). The paper also answers CGT's remark that such an analysis can be derived simply: the details are "not as trivial as one might expect" (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The introduction lists the new features against the prior methods (p. 2–3). A notation table names the constants with mnemonic subscripts (κ_fcd for "fraction of Cauchy decrease", and so on) (p. 4). The scope is stated plainly: "We provide no analysis for local rates of convergence" (p. 3). The practicality of the model-improvement algorithm is argued in prose, "Hence we claim that this procedure is reasonably efficient in practice", with no numbers (p. 12). Footnotes explain parameter choices (p. 14, p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory: a general framework with first- and second-order lim results. The limits are stated:
  - no local rates. Fully linear models from n+1, 2n+1 or (n+1)(n+2)/2 − 1 points are treated alike, although their local rates differ (p. 3);
  - fully quadratic models may need O(n²) points (p. 3);
  - O(n⁶) linear algebra per iteration is a problem when n is about 100 (p. 12);
  - with derivatives, forcing Δ → 0 may "jeopardize the fast rates of local convergence" (p. 2).

  There is no future-work section (none found in the text).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The bridging stance is the same: a framework "in the spirit of" practical methods, with their theory retained (p. 2). The analysis does not care how the models are built (p. 1–2). But the glue is the criticality step plus certified model quality, not sufficient decrease: simple decrease is accepted when the model is fully linear (p. 2, p. 14).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (a deterministic precursor). The requirement that the model be fully linear at every iteration is relaxed: it only has to be possible to make it fully linear within a finite, uniformly bounded number of iterations (p. 3). SKILL.md's Method 3 lists *models that are fully linear at every iteration* as an expensive requirement. This paper is the deterministic first step of that relaxation, which the later papers make probabilistic.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The trust-region skeleton is kept. What changes is what counts as an acceptable model (the fully linear/quadratic class) and as success (simple decrease with a certified model) (p. 7–9, p. 14–15). The lim and second-order results are re-derived.
- Method 2 ✗ contradiction (era). No complexity or rates (p. 3).
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (genre). No numerics and no code.
- New-pattern candidate: *a criticality step ties the radius to stationarity, so Δ → 0 becomes the stopping test* (p. 2; Lemma 5.5, p. 18).
- New-pattern candidate: *certify-and-repair component abstraction* (Def. 3.1, p. 7–8), shared with S010 and S020.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Abstract a component by the property the proof needs (Taylor-like bounds with uniform constants) *plus* a clause that a finite certify-or-repair algorithm exists. Then test the abstraction on degenerate members (M = {f}, finite differences) to show it is neither vacuous nor too narrow (p. 7–8).
- [D2/D4] Criticality step: when the model gradient is ≤ c, improve the model on shrinking balls until Δ ≤ μ‖g‖. This gives ‖g_k‖ ≥ min{c, Δ_k/μ}, which forces Δ_k → 0 (Alg. 4.2, p. 14; Lemma 5.5, p. 18).
- [D4] Classify iterations four ways (successful / acceptable / model-improving / unsuccessful), and control the model-improving ones through a uniform bound N on consecutive occurrences (p. 15, p. 17).
- [D4] A monotonicity lemma: a model accurate on a small ball stays accurate on larger balls with the same constants, provided the constants are enlarged without loss of generality. Prove it with the integral mean value theorem applied order by order (Lemma 3.4, p. 9–10).
- [D2] For second-order results, increase the radius only when it is small relative to the stationarity measure (Δ_k < βσ^m_k), and bound the displacement with a geometric series (p. 22, p. 28).
- [D7] Put a table of constants with mnemonic subscripts before the analysis (p. 4).
