### S067 · Stochastic trust-region and direct-search methods: A weak tail bound condition and reduced sample sizing (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2024, DOI 10.1137/22m1543446)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: F. Rinaldi, D. Zeffiro (both at Padova, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period (Vicente's affiliation, p. 1).
- It makes the nonsmooth DFO trust-region method of Liuzzi, Lucidi, Rinaldi & Vicente 2019 stochastic ([25]; the scheme is "a minor modification of the Basic DFO-TRNS", p. 15).
- It continues the probabilistic-models line of Bandeira, Scheinberg & Vicente 2014 ([5], p. 2).
- SKILL.md already cites it in Heuristic 3 and Workflow D, but from a talk abstract.

**Read**: full text. The Appendix A.1 proofs were read, though several formulas are garbled in the extraction. The Appendix A.2 problem table was read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a *cost* in the literature. Stochastic trust-region methods [7, 9, 36] and direct-search methods [2, 13, 14] need O(Δ_k⁻⁴) samples per iteration under finite variance. They use sufficient-decrease power 2, except [13], which allows a power greater than 1 (p. 4).

  A second gap is regularity. Model-based stochastic DFO assumes a Lipschitz gradient and probabilistically accurate gradient estimates. The paper notes that such an estimate is not available for nonsmooth f (p. 3).

  The problem is min f(x), with f locally Lipschitz, possibly nonsmooth, and inf f finite. Only a stochastic oracle f̃(x) = F(x, ξ) is available (p. 2). The motivating settings are statistical-learning losses and Monte Carlo simulation (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: One probabilistic condition is placed on the *reduction estimate* F_k − F_k^g, the quantity the acceptance test uses, and not on each function estimate (p. 3, p. 6). The condition is tied to the sufficient-decrease power q > 1:
  - acceptance is f_k − f_k^g ≥ θδ_k^q (2.1);
  - the tail bound is at scale αΔ_k^q (Assumption 2.1, p. 5).

  Lowering q towards 1 lowers the number of samples needed (Cor. 2.5, p. 8). The algorithms are deliberately simple (p. 4):
  - **Algorithm 1**: one direction g_k on the unit sphere and two estimates. Accept on sufficient decrease; expand by τ̄ ∈ [1, 1+τ] or shrink by (1−τ) (p. 10).
  - **Algorithm 2**: the Basic DFO-TRNS with two changes. It uses the new radius update, and its linear term g_k need not lie on the unit sphere. The ratio is ρ̄_k = (f_k − f_k^s)/(θ‖s_k‖^q) (p. 15).

  What is kept: the deterministic skeletons and the step-size logic. What changes: exact values become estimates, and the power 2 becomes q (p. 4, p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Tail bound (Assumption 2.1, p. 5)**: for some ε_q independent of k, P(|F_k − F_k^g − (f(X_k) − f(X_k + Δ_kG_k))| ≥ αΔ_k^q | F_{k−1}) ≤ ε_q/α^{q/(q−1)} for every α > 0. This is a power-law tail (p. 6). The conditioning σ-algebra covers the past up to the choice of G_k (p. 5).
  - **Known noise constant**: an upper bound on ε_q must be known. The reason is that θ must exceed a threshold (3.1) that depends on ε_q and on the step-update factors (p. 6, p. 11).
  - **Sample sizes with i.i.d. averaging**: suppose the noise has a finite r-th moment with r = q/(q−1) (2.9). Then Assumption 2.1 holds with O(Δ_k^{−2q}) samples for q ∈ (1, 2] (Thm 2.4, p. 7). With q = 1 + ε/2 this is O(Δ_k^{−2−ε}) (Cor. 2.5, p. 8).
    - The case q > 2 has a different exponent, which is garbled in the text file (p. 7).
    - For r < 2 the moment assumption is weaker than finite variance; for r > 2 it is stronger (p. 7).
  - **Common random numbers**: this route needs a known random number generator and correlated errors, E|F̄(x,ξ) − F̄(y,ξ)|^r ≤ D_r‖x−y‖^r (2.10). Then O(Δ_k^{2−2q}) samples suffice, i.e., O(Δ_k^{−ε}) (Thm 2.8, Cor. 2.9, p. 8). Condition (2.10) holds in two cases:
    - uniform Lipschitz continuity of F(·, ξ), which the paper calls standard in the analysis of zeroth-order methods (Prop. 2.6, p. 8);
    - a Gaussian-process noise model (Prop. 2.7, p. 8).
  - **Convergence theorems**: they also need three things (Thm 3.3, p. 13):
    - iterates with accumulation points (a compact set, as in [2], p. 13);
    - f Lipschitz near the limit points;
    - directions dense on a subsequence of unsuccessful iterations.
  - **Trust-region version**: it also needs ‖B_k‖ ≤ ‖G_k‖/(ρΔ_k), which is a weaker form of the DFO-TRNS assumption (Assumption 4.1, pp. 15–16).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The outline states a two-step analysis (p. 4): (i) Σ E[Δ_k^q] < ∞, so the step size goes to 0 almost surely; (ii) Clarke stationarity along unsuccessful iterations.
  - **Step (i) (Thm 3.1, pp. 11–12)**: the potential is Φ_k = f(X_k) − f* + ηΔ_k^q with η = θ/τ̄_q. The proof shows E[Φ_k − Φ_{k+1} | F_{k−1}] ≥ εΔ_k^q, then concludes "as in [13, Theorem 3]" (Dzahini).
    - The trick is to write the true decrease as (θ − Z_k)Δ_k^q. A success then implies an estimate error of at least Z_kΔ_k^q (p. 12).
    - By the tail bound, the expected loss from false successes is therefore at most a constant ε_f times Δ_k^q (p. 12).
    - The paper says this is how Assumption 2.1 lets one argument cover both successful and unsuccessful steps (pp. 10–11).
    - The threshold θ > (3.1) makes ε > 0 (p. 11).
  - **Lemma 2.3 (pp. 6–7)**: extends the tail bound from a constant α to an F_{k−1}-measurable random threshold. The proof uses discrete approximation, Beppo Levi and dominated convergence.
  - **Step (ii) (Lemma 3.2, pp. 12–13)**: apply the tail bound with threshold Δ_k/m and sum using step (i). Borel–Cantelli then gives that, almost surely, the estimate error is eventually at most Δ_k/m. On unsuccessful iterations this yields lim inf (f(X_k + Δ_kG_k) − f(X_k))/Δ_k ≥ 0. Clarke stationarity follows from density and Lipschitz continuity (Thm 3.3, pp. 13–14).
  - **Transfer to trust regions**: ‖S_k‖ ≥ ρΔ_k (Lemma 4.2, p. 16) lets the direct-search proof run with ‖S_k‖ in place of Δ_k (Thms 4.4, 4.6, pp. 16–18). Under a stronger Hessian bound, Ŝ_k → −Ĝ_k: the trust-region scheme becomes "at the limit a search along a direction" (Prop. 4.8, p. 18).
  - **Sample sizes**: Markov/Chebyshev on the r-th moment of an average, with Rosenthal's inequality for r ≥ 2 or von Bahr–Esseen for 1 < r < 2 (App. A.1, pp. 26–28).
  - **Dominance of the rivals' conditions**:
    - the StoMADS variance condition implies Assumption 2.1 with q = 2, by conditional Chebyshev (Prop. 2.10, p. 9, p. 29);
    - β-probabilistic accuracy (2.13), holding for every β in an interval, also implies it, via the event inclusion (2.14) (Prop. 2.12, p. 9, p. 30).
  - **Rates**: none. Remark 5.1 notes that q → 1 may raise iteration complexity. In the smooth deterministic case the complexity is O(ε^{−q/(q−1)}) [37]. The nonsmooth trade-off is left open (p. 20).
- **D5 Experiments**: The test set is 96 nonsmooth unconstrained problems (Table 1, p. 30), each run 10 times, so |P| = 960 (p. 19).

  Protocol (p. 19):
  - data and performance profiles on function (sample) evaluations;
  - budget 10000(n_p + 1);
  - γ_p ∈ {10⁻², 10⁻⁴};
  - the profiles use the true f, while the algorithms see only noisy f.

  The noise is Gaussian with standard deviation 0.1. The average of p_k samples is simulated by adding N(0, 1/√p_k) noise (p. 20).

  There are two studies:
  - **q = 2 vs q = 1.5 (SDS2 vs SDS1.5)**: sample sizes p_k = ⌈0.01δ_k⁻⁴⌉ vs ⌈0.01δ_k⁻³⌉. A correlated-error version uses ⌈0.01δ_k⁻²⌉ vs ⌈0.01δ_k⁻¹⌉. q = 1.5 wins in both (p. 20; Figs. 1–2, pp. 21–22).
  - **Against StoMADS**: three methods are compared (pp. 21–22):
    - SDS+ alternates coordinate directions with random ones once δ_k < 0.5;
    - STR uses a minimum-Frobenius-norm model on {x_k ± δ_ke_i}, rebuilt every iteration;
    - StoMADS uses O(Δ_k⁻⁴) samples.

    SDS+1.5 is best for γ_p = 10⁻², and STR1.5 is best for γ_p = 10⁻⁴ (p. 22; Fig. 3, p. 23).

  The parameters were tuned by grid search: τ = 0.001, τ̄ = 1.001, θ = 0.5, δ_0 = 2 (p. 20). Remark 5.2 discloses the gap with the theory. The theory requires θ > 4 (q = 2) or θ ≳ 9 (q = 1.5), yet both methods perform badly for θ > 1 (p. 20).

  The tested SDS+ is a variant of the analysed algorithm. The paper says the convergence result "extends to this variant in a straightforward way" (p. 21). No code release is mentioned (not found in the text).
- **D6 Positioning**: The review is split into three lines (pp. 2–3):
  - model-based: Larson–Billups, ASTRO-DF, Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg, Blanchet et al.;
  - direct search: Dzahini, StoMADS and its constrained extension;
  - zeroth-order methods: Nesterov–Spokoiny, Ghadimi–Lan, Lin–Zheng–Jordan.

  Rivals are restated, not attacked. The recurring criticism is that a probabilistically accurate gradient is unavailable for nonsmooth f (p. 3, p. 9).

  The new condition is shown to be implied by the rivals' conditions (Props. 2.10, 2.12, p. 9). Remark 2.11 extends the analysis to StoMADS itself (p. 9). The central proof step is borrowed from Dzahini [13] (p. 11). Self-citations are [5], [6], [11], [25] and [37] (pp. 24–26).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Structure**: the headline numbers O(Δ_k⁻⁴) → O(Δ_k^{−2−ε}) are in the abstract (p. 1). §1.2 "The contributions of this manuscript" states the goal in its first sentence (p. 3). Section 2 proofs are moved to the appendix "to improve readability" (pp. 4–5).
  - **Priority claim**: there is one, "the first time global convergence is proved for a stochastic derivative-free trust-region algorithm for non-smooth" problems (p. 4).
  - **Hedging**: the numerics are hedged ("suggests", "appear", "seems", p. 22). A conjecture is labelled as one (p. 20). The conclusion calls the single-condition result "Surprisingly" (pp. 22–23).
  - **Editing slips**:
    - after the in-text proof of Lemma 2.3 there is a leftover sentence saying the proof is technical and in the appendix (p. 7);
    - "CRN (common number generator)" (p. 8);
    - typos "algoritmic" (p. 1) and "trus-region" (p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are theory and algorithms (pp. 3–4, pp. 22–23):
  - theory: a weaker condition, per-iteration sample bounds, and a trade-off between noise moment and sample size;
  - algorithms: two simple ones, with a claimed first convergence result for stochastic nonsmooth DFO trust regions.

  Limits the paper states:
  - the bound on ε_q must be known (p. 6);
  - the θ threshold is far from what works in practice (p. 20);
  - the iteration-complexity price of q → 1 is open (p. 20).

  Limits visible from the text but not stated: there are no rates, and the per-iteration sample count is never turned into a total sample count.

  Future work (p. 23):
  - nonsmooth random local models (piecewise linear, or random smooth functions as in Bayesian optimization);
  - composites that are a maximum of smooth functions;
  - constraints;
  - global optimization;
  - numerics for nonsmooth trust-region versions.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Put the accuracy requirement on the quantity the acceptance test uses (here the difference estimate). Scale it with the same power q that defines sufficient decrease, so the sample size falls with q (pp. 5–8).
- [D4] Use the potential Φ_k = f(X_k) − f* + ηΔ_k^q (pp. 11–12):
  - write the true decrease as (θ − Z_k)Δ_k^q;
  - note that a false success needs an estimate error of at least Z_kΔ_k^q;
  - bound the expected loss by a constant times Δ_k^q through the tail bound.

  Successful and unsuccessful steps are then handled in one inequality.
- [D4] From Σ E[Δ_k^q] < ∞, use Borel–Cantelli to show that the estimate errors are almost surely eventually below Δ_k/m. Then run the deterministic Clarke-derivative argument on unsuccessful iterations (pp. 12–14).
- [D4] Before claiming a weaker assumption, prove that each rival assumption implies yours, by conditional Chebyshev or event inclusion (p. 9, pp. 29–30).
- [D4] To move a direct-search proof to trust regions, derive ‖S_k‖ ≥ ρΔ_k from a Hessian bound ‖B_k‖ ≤ ‖G_k‖/(ρΔ_k). Then replace Δ_k by ‖S_k‖ throughout (pp. 15–17).
- [D5] Build the profiles on the true f while the solvers see only noisy values. Count each of 10 repeated runs as a separate problem (p. 19).
- [D5] State the gap between the theoretical and the tuned parameter values in a numbered remark next to the experiments (Remark 5.2, p. 20).

### S070 · Bilevel optimization with a multi-objective lower-level problem: Risk-neutral and risk-averse formulations (Optimization Methods and Software 2024, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2024.2318707)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. Giovannelli, G. D. Kent (all three authors at Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period, funded by AFOSR award FA9550-23-1-0217 (p. 21). It builds on two earlier works (p. 5, p. 20):
- the same group's bilevel stochastic gradient (BSG) method [19] (Giovannelli, Kent & Vicente, Lehigh technical report, 2022);
- the stochastic multi-gradient method of Liu & Vicente [27].

Its only self-citations are [19] and [27]. Vicente's own earlier bilevel papers, the 1994 bibliography (S002) and the 2006 bilevel–multicriteria paper (S022), are not in the reference list (pp. 21–23). **Read**: full text (23 pp.).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a cell nobody has filled. The abstract says bilevel problems with conflicting lower-level (LL) objectives "have received little attention in the deterministic case and have never been studied from a stochastic approximation viewpoint" (p. 1). Three applications motivate it (p. 2):
  - sensor relocation for border defence (multi-objective LL);
  - renewable-energy design (multi-objective upper level, UL);
  - fair ML (both levels multi-objective).

  The problem is BMOLL (1.2): one UL objective f_u and q > 1 LL objectives. The UL constraint is x ∈ X, with X closed and convex; the LL is unconstrained (pp. 1–2).

  The difficulty is conceptual: "min" is ambiguous when the LL has many Pareto solutions (p. 2). Prior work (p. 3):
  - deterministic work uses weighted sums with the weights as UL variables [1, 9, 29, 30], or ε-constraints [36];
  - the only stochastic method [20] addresses a different problem.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key move is to look at y as a parameter rather than as a variable (p. 3). This brings in the optimistic / risk-neutral / risk-averse menu. For each reading, the BSG skeleton is kept (Alg. 1: approximate LL solve, adjoint gradient, projected step, p. 5); only the objective and its gradient change.
  - **Optimistic (known, §4)**:
    - Under Assumption 3.1, the weak Pareto set equals the set of weighted-sum minimizers (Prop. 3.1, p. 6).
    - So (4.1) is equivalent to a single-objective bilevel problem over (x, λ), with λ in the simplex Λ (Prop. 4.1, p. 7).
    - BSG-OPT projects onto X × Λ (Alg. 2, p. 8).
    - The new part is the stochastic setting (p. 20).
  - **Risk-neutral (§5)**:
    - The target is min E_{y∼P(x)} f_u(x, y) (5.3).
    - It is replaced by a "companion" problem that averages over weights λ ∼ Λ instead (5.4) (p. 9).
    - BSG-RN discretizes Λ into N points and samples mini-batches of Q (pp. 9–11).
  - **Risk-averse (§6)**:
    - The target is min_x max_{y∈P(x)} f_u (6.2).
    - P(x) is written algebraically as weighted-sum stationarity (6.3) and lifted to (y, λ) ∈ P̃(x) (6.5), with an equivalence result (Prop. 6.1, p. 12).
    - A subgradient comes from a Danskin-type formula for an x-dependent feasible set (Fiacco [15, Cor. 4.11], p. 12).
    - BSG-RA is a stochastic subgradient method (Alg. 4, p. 13).

  Each new formulation is first motivated by the special case P(x) = P for all x (p. 9, p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Smoothness**: f_u is C¹ and the LL functions are C² (Assumption 4.1, p. 7).
  - **Convexity (Assumption 3.1, p. 6)**: the LL objectives are strictly convex in y for all x ∈ X, and P(x) ≠ ∅. This makes the weighted-sum parametrization exact and gives P = P_s (Prop. 3.1, p. 6). Uniform convexity gives nonemptiness and compactness (Remark 3.1, p. 6).
  - **LL solution (Assumption 4.2, p. 7)**: y(x, λ) exists, and the *stochastic* Hessian estimates are positive definite at all points.
  - **Risk-averse case (Assumption 6.1, pp. 12–13)**: KKT and LICQ hold for (6.6), and P̃(x) is nonempty and compact.
  - **Gap in the risk-neutral companion**: the paper does not say how a distribution on Λ relates to a distribution on P(x), and it does not claim that (5.4) is equivalent to (5.3) (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No convergence theorem is proved. For the smooth optimistic and risk-neutral cases, the paper says theory can be obtained as an extension of the BSG results [19]. The theory for the nonsmooth risk-averse case "is left for future work" (p. 5). Devices used:
  - equivalence propositions proved by contradiction (Prop. 4.1, p. 7; Prop. 6.1, p. 12);
  - adjoint gradients by implicit differentiation of Σ_j λ_j∇_y f_ℓ^j = 0 with respect to both x and λ, where ∇²_{λy} f_ℓ is the stacked LL gradients [∇_y f_ℓ^1, …, ∇_y f_ℓ^q]^⊤ ((4.5)–(4.7), p. 8; (5.9)–(5.11), p. 10);
  - lifting the Pareto set into a constraint set in (y, λ) (pp. 11–12);
  - the Danskin/Fiacco subdifferential ((6.8)–(6.9), pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Code and test problems**: Python, with code on GitHub (BMOLL_OPT_RN_RA) (p. 13). The UL is quadratic (7.1). The LL problems are JOS1 and SP1 (both separable) and a new GKV1, which is separable or not depending on the matrices (Table 1, p. 14).
  - **Three settings (p. 15, p. 17)**:
    - deterministic separable with n̄ = 1, so the (x, y) plane and the LL fronts can be drawn;
    - deterministic non-separable with n̄ = 50;
    - stochastic non-separable with n̄ = 50, with Gaussian noise on the gradients (std 0, 1, 2) and on the Hessians (std 0, 0.1, 0.2).
  - **Implementation details**:
    - The adjoint systems are solved by factorization at this size; conjugate gradients are suggested for large problems (pp. 13–14).
    - The LL is solved with an increasing-accuracy rule: thresholds 0.1 and 0.9, at most 30 LL iterations (pp. 14–15).
    - The risk-averse inner problem (6.6) uses SciPy's constrained trust-region solver (p. 15).
    - Armijo line search is compared with fixed steps. Decaying steps did worse and are not reported (pp. 14–15).
  - **Ablations**: Q ∈ {10, 20, 40, 500}, with 95% confidence intervals over 10 starting points (Fig. 5, pp. 17–18). In the stochastic runs, step sizes come from a grid search, with 10 seeds and 95% confidence intervals (p. 17).
  - **Results**:
    - all methods find the optimal solutions of Problems 1–3 (p. 15);
    - mini-batching Λ reaches the same value as using the full set (p. 17);
    - BSG-RN is the most robust to noise (p. 17).
  - **Plots and baselines**: each method is plotted on its own objective, and LL Pareto fronts are shown at x_OPT, x_RN and x_RA (p. 15; Figs. 1–4). There is no external baseline and no problem collection.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents itself as the stochastic, multi-reading extension of deterministic weighted-sum work [1, 9, 29, 30]. That earlier work is reframed as "the known optimistic formulation" (p. 3; §4, p. 6). Gu et al. [20] is distinguished in detail: several LL problems, a robust UL, and no UL Pareto front (p. 3). Two conventions are borrowed from the stochastic-gradient survey [2]: no stopping rule (p. 5), and larger batches for Hessian estimates than for gradients (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Opening**: the abstract names the gap first, then the contribution. There is one "To the best of our knowledge" (p. 3).
  - **Roadmap**: the organization paragraph says the p > 1 cases will be developed "from known building blocks" (p. 3).
  - **Fixed order**: every formulation follows the same sequence: intuition in a frozen case → formulation → equivalence proposition → gradient formula → algorithm box.
  - **Deferral and hedging**: theory is deferred in one sentence (p. 5). The only hedge in the numerics is "does not seem" (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are two new formulations, three algorithms (stochastic BSG-OPT, BSG-RN, BSG-RA) and code (pp. 3, 13, 20). Limits:
  - there is no convergence theory (p. 5);
  - the tests are small and synthetic, with n, m ≤ 50 (p. 13);
  - a multi-objective UL with a risk-averse LL needs a multi-objective constrained solver, so "further research is needed" (p. 21).

  Future work:
  - p > 1, q = 1 through an adjoint multi-gradient QP (8.2) (p. 20);
  - p, q > 1 by combining the pieces (pp. 20–21).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (outside DFO). The BSG skeleton is kept, and only what is minimized changes with each reading (pp. 4–5, 8, 11, 13). Section 8 extends the work by assembling known building blocks (p. 20).
- Heuristic 4 / Workflow E ⚠ variant. The paper does scalarize the LL with weighted sums, but never with weights fixed in advance:
  - optimistic: the weights become variables;
  - risk-neutral: the paper averages over them;
  - risk-averse: it maximizes over them.

  Strict convexity makes this parametrization exact (Prop. 3.1, p. 6). The failure named in Workflow E's checkpoint, fixed weights missing nonconvex parts of the front, is ruled out by an assumption, not by a dominance list.
- Method 2 ✗ contradiction. There are no rates and no counts, and the theory is deferred (p. 5).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The code is released (p. 13). But there are only three synthetic problems, no collection, no profiles and no external baseline (pp. 14–17).
- Methods 1 and 3: no link.
- New-pattern candidate: *enumerate the readings of an ambiguous formulation* (optimistic / risk-neutral / risk-averse, pp. 2–3, 8–13).

### S088 · Full-low evaluation methods for bound and linearly constrained derivative-free optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10589-024-00596-2)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: C. W. Royer (Paris Dauphine-PSL), O. Sohab (Lehigh; corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period. It is the constrained sequel to Berahas, Sohab & Vicente [9], the unconstrained full-low evaluation paper, cited as OMS 38, 2022 (p. 2). The Low-Eval step is the probabilistic feasible-descent direct search of Gratton, Royer, Vicente & Zhang [22] (COAP 2019), including their dspfd code (p. 16). Funding: AFOSR award FA9550-23-1-0217 and ANR PRAIRIE (p. 24). **Read**: full text; the problem tables in Appendix A (pp. 29–33) were skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry combines an extension gap with a practical constraint:
  - Full-low evaluation (FLE) did well in the unconstrained case [9] (p. 2).
  - Simulation codes "often enforce hard constraints on their parameters", typically bounds or linear relations, which must hold for the simulation to finish (p. 2).

  The two ingredients trade off (pp. 1–2):
  - Finite-difference quasi-Newton is strong on smooth problems, but its cost "scales at least linearly with the dimension".
  - Direct search is cheaper and more robust to noise and nonsmoothness.

  The problem (2.1) is min f subject to Ax = b and ℓ ≤ A_I x ≤ u. A has full row rank, and W is an orthonormal basis of its null space (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 is "identical to that of Berahas et al. [9]", except that the starting point must be feasible (p. 5). Only the two step routines change, so that iterates stay feasible:
  - **Full-Eval (Alg. 2, p. 6)**:
    - compute an FD gradient g_k and a direction p_k;
    - project, x̄_k = P_F[x_k + p_k];
    - run Armijo backtracking along x̄_k − x_k.

    The step is abandoned, with a switch to Low-Eval, as soon as β < γα_k (switching condition (3.3)). Convexity of F keeps the iterates feasible (p. 6).
  - **Low-Eval (Alg. 3, p. 7)**: direct search with a forcing function ρ, polling in approximate tangent cones T(x, ξ). Steps of length at most ξ in these cones stay feasible (Lemma 2.1, p. 5).
    - The switch back to Full-Eval is user-defined.
    - The implementation allows as many unsuccessful Low-Eval attempts as the preceding Full-Eval step had backtracks, "a balanced distribution of effort" (p. 7).
  - **Practical instance, Full-Eval (p. 15)**:
    - The direction is p_k = −W H_k W^⊤ g_k with BFGS. It keeps Ax = b and needs finite differences only in the null space, so n − m evaluations instead of n.
    - The BFGS update is skipped if s^⊤y < 10⁻¹⁰‖s‖‖y‖.
    - Parameters: γ = 1, ρ(α) = min(10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁵α²), β̄ = 1, τ = 0.5.
  - **Practical instance, Low-Eval (p. 16)**: polling follows dspfd:
    - the subspace part of the cone is replaced by one random direction and its negative;
    - a random fraction of the pointed-cone generators is sampled;
    - the generators come from the double description method.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Smooth analysis**:
    - f is bounded below and ∇f is L-Lipschitz (A4.1–4.2, p. 7).
    - A4.3 is a direction condition: an angle κ and norm equivalence (p. 8).
    - A4.4 is gradient accuracy, ‖∇f(x_k) − g_k‖ ≤ u_g‖q_k^g‖ with u_g < κ(1 − c). The paper notes that this restriction on u_g is new compared with the unconstrained case (p. 8).
    - The stationarity measure is q(x) = ‖P_F[x − ∇f(x)] − x‖. It is chosen over step-size-based measures "Since our theory relies on that of projected gradient techniques" (p. 3).
  - **Nonsmooth analysis**:
    - a forcing function (A4.5);
    - A4.6, which requires ‖q_k^g‖ > ε_g on successful Full-Eval iterations, taken "As in the unconstrained setting" (p. 11);
    - bounded iterates, and f Lipschitz near x*;
    - refining directions dense in T(x*) (p. 12).

    Stationarity is in the Clarke–Jahn sense (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Lemma 4.1 (pp. 8–9)**: a lower bound β_min on accepted backtracking steps, from Taylor's theorem, the angle condition and gradient accuracy.
  - **Theorem 4.1 (p. 10)**: at most ⌈L_1(f(x_0) − f_low)ε⁻²⌉ *successful Full-Eval* iterations occur before ‖q‖ ≤ ε. The telescoping sum keeps only successful Full-Eval iterations. All other iterations do not increase f and are dropped (p. 10). This matches the unconstrained rate [9].

    Evaluations are not counted. A Full-Eval iteration costs up to n + log(β_min/β̄)/log(τ) + 1 evaluations. But the numbers of Low-Eval iterations and unsuccessful Full-Eval iterations are not bounded, and bounding them is declared outside the scope (p. 11).
  - **Lemma 4.2 (pp. 11–12)**: because β ≥ γα_k and A4.6 holds:
    - a successful Full-Eval step decreases f by at least cκγε_g²α_k/U_p;
    - a successful Low-Eval step decreases f by at least ρ(α_k).

    Summability then forces α_k → 0 along unsuccessful Low-Eval iterations. The paper singles out stopping the line search when (3.3) fails as the feature that made the results rigorous (p. 15).
  - **Theorem 4.2 (pp. 12–13)**: the standard refining-direction argument for Clarke–Jahn stationarity, citing [4, 5, 41]. Here [41] is Vicente & Custódio 2012, the discontinuous-functions paper whose 2024 counterexample SKILL.md mentions. This paper works with f Lipschitz near x* and does not mention the counterexample.
  - **Algorithm 4 / Prop. 4.1 (pp. 13–14)**: a criticality-step loop shrinks the FD parameter until h_k ≤ u'_g‖q_k^{h_k}‖, which enforces A4.4. The loop terminates finitely unless q_k = 0; the proof uses the FD error bound ½√n L h_k. The idea comes from the criticality step of model-based trust-region methods (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Setup**: MATLAB R2019b, with code on GitHub (sohaboumaima/FLE) (p. 14).
  - **Solvers (p. 17)**:
    - constFLE, the new method;
    - its two halves run alone: constBFGS (Full-Eval only) and dspfd (Low-Eval only);
    - NOMAD, with its default progressive barrier;
    - MATLAB patternsearch, with its default GPS setting.
  - **Test sets (pp. 17–18; Tables 1–7, pp. 29–33)**:
    - 41 bound-constrained CUTEst problems (n = 2–20);
    - 76 linearly constrained problems (n = 2–24, up to 2000 inequalities), 40 of them with at least one inequality;
    - noisy versions f = φ(1 + ξ), ξ ∼ U(−ε_f, ε_f), following Moré–Wild; the value of ε_f is not stated in the text;
    - nonsmooth versions made by moving constraints into an ℓ1 penalty: 52 bound-constrained and 107 general problems. "1/2B" marks problems where only half the bounds are penalized;
    - 14 linearly constrained minimax problems.
  - **Metric and budget**: performance profiles on function evaluations (pp. 18–19).
    - Convergence test: f(x_0) − f(x) ≥ (1 − τ)(f(x_0) − f_L), with τ ∈ {10⁻³, 10⁻⁵} (10⁻¹ and 10⁻³ for the noisy problems).
    - Budget: 100(n + 1) evaluations, described as "what is need for 100 simplex gradients".
  - **Results**:
    - constFLE is best on the smooth sets (Figs. 1–3, pp. 19–21).
    - On the ℓ1 sets it is the most efficient. On the bound-constrained ℓ1 set, NOMAD is the most robust (p. 20, p. 22).
    - It loses on minimax, where NOMAD is best and Low-Eval second; the text does not say where constFLE ranks (p. 22).
    - It loses on noisy problems, where NOMAD (bounds) or patternsearch (linear constraints) is best. With bounds, constFLE sits between its two halves (pp. 23–24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] When you hybridize an expensive, accurate step with a cheap, robust one, stop the expensive step's line search as soon as its step length drops below γ times the cheap method's step size. This single rule makes both step types give summable decreases in the same parameter α_k (p. 6; Lemma 4.2, pp. 11–12).
- [D2] With linear equalities, compute FD gradients only along a null-space basis W (n − m evaluations) and use the direction −W H W^⊤ g. The equalities then hold automatically (p. 15).
- [D4] To bound the iterations of a hybrid method, telescope over the one step type with a provable decrease and drop the others by monotonicity (p. 10).
- [D4] Turn a gradient-accuracy assumption into an algorithm: a criticality loop that shrinks the FD parameter h in proportion to the current stationarity estimate, plus a finite-termination proposition (pp. 13–14).
- [D5] Build nonsmooth test sets from a smooth collection by moving constraints into an ℓ1 penalty. Penalize only half of the bounds so that the linear constraints still matter (pp. 17–18).
- [D5] Include each component of the hybrid as a solver of its own (constBFGS, dspfd), so the profiles show whether the combination beats its parts (p. 17).
