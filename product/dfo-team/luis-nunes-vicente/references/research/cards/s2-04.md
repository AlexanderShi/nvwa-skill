### S082 · Modeling binary stars: age, helium abundance, and convection parameters (Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 2012, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2966.2012.21516.x)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. M. Fernandes (astronomer, first author), A. I. F. Vaz · **Position in trajectory**: the 1996–2010 direct-search line used as an instrument. PSwarm (Vaz & Vicente 2007) is coupled to the stellar evolution code CESAM. The same pipeline was applied earlier to 115 single stars (Fernandes, Vaz & Vicente 2011 = S075; p. 2) and is applied here to binaries. **Read**: full (9 pages).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: A stellar model has six unknowns (mass M, age t, X, Y, mixing length α, overshooting ov), but spectroscopy and photometry give four observables (T_eff, L, Z/X, g). The calibration is therefore underdetermined. The field closes the gap by fixing solar ΔY/ΔZ and a solar α, which the authors say observations do not necessarily support (p. 2). Entry: the two stars of a binary share age and composition, which adds constraints. Question: do solar-scaled values hold for seven FGK binaries selected from Torres et al. (2010) (pp. 2–3)?
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: No new algorithm. The 2011 methodology is reused: a least-squares misfit between CESAM outputs and observations, each term divided by its observational error (eq. (1), p. 3), with bounds (Table 2, p. 4). PSwarm is the solver because the objective is simulation-based, expensive and derivative-free, and has non-unique global minimizers (p. 3). Mass is left free although it is known, so that the method's accuracy can be checked (p. 4). The new part is a protocol around the solver. Each star is solved 35 times, giving 35 × 35 run pairs per binary. Only pairs with the agreement metric θ < 1 are kept (θ combines age within 500 Myr and X, Y within 0.01; eq. (3)). Pairs whose objective is ≥ 1 for either star are also dropped, and the survivors are averaged (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Both stars of a binary are assumed coeval, with a common initial composition (p. 2). Convection follows mixing-length theory with free α, and ov = 0 for M < 1.1 M⊙ (footnote, p. 3). The input physics is fixed. An analytic equation of state is chosen because of the computing cost (p. 4). A primordial helium of 0.23 is kept for consistency with the 2011 work, although recent values point to 0.25 (p. 4). Each star is fitted individually and the two are matched afterwards (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No theorems. Error bars come from 8 extra estimations per star, each moving one observable to ±δ (10 runs each, 1120 runs in all). They are reported as an RMS deviation (Table 3) and as a sensitivity per observable (Table 4) (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: 14 stars. Recovered masses against Torres et al.: slope 0.9627, R² = 0.9916 (Fig. 1, p. 5). Binary-by-binary comparison with the literature (pp. 5–6). A bias check against the earlier 115-star sample, where the fitted α fell both below and above the solar value (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is astrophysical. Only VZ Hya and α Cen are compatible with solar models in both components. α tends to decrease with mass, overshooting tends to increase with mass, and solar ΔY/ΔZ is compatible for all systems within the errors (p. 8). Limits are stated concretely. The models ignore rotation, which a lower α may mimic; the authors flag this for further study (p. 8). The α–mass plot mixes stars of different age and composition (p. 7). The overshooting trend lies within large error bars (p. 7). The optimizer's cost and any parallel use are not discussed beyond the solver requirements (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Taste #6 (anchored in an application) ✅ evidence (pp. 2–5): a Vicente solver is the engine of a paper in a domain journal.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence, downstream (p. 3): PSwarm, the swarm-in-the-search-step product of Method 1, is chosen because the problem needs global, derivative-free search.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 4–5, 7): the solver has been released and is reused, but validation is by recovering a known quantity (the mass), agreeing with the literature and a bias check, not by a problem collection or profiles.
- new-pattern candidate: theory paper / application paper split (already in the pool). The solver paper (2007) and the application papers (2011, 2012) are separate, and the application paper describes the solver in one paragraph (p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: multistart consensus filter for non-identifiable inverse problems (pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When an inverse problem has more unknowns than observables, run the global solver many times. Keep only the runs that (i) fit within the error bars (objective < 1 in error-normalized units) and (ii) agree on parameters that linked objects share physically (θ < 1). Report the average of the survivors (pp. 4–5).
- Error bars without derivatives: re-solve with each observable moved to its ±1σ extreme, one at a time. Report the RMS change of each estimate and a table of sensitivity per observable (p. 5, Tables 3–4).
- Leave a known quantity free (here the mass) so that recovering it validates the whole pipeline (p. 4; Fig. 1, p. 5).
- Before reporting a trend (sub-solar α), check on an earlier, larger sample whether the method is biased toward it (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "Our numerical experience has also shown that this problem has non-unique global minimizers." (p. 3)
- "Thus, to properly solve it, one must select a solver capable of global optimization without the use of derivatives (and, preferably, in a parallel environment, given the cost of the numerical simulations)." (p. 3)

### S090 · Inexact solution of NLP subproblems in MINLP (Journal of Global Optimization 2013, DOI 10.1007/s10898-012-0010-5)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Li (Coimbra PhD student with an FCT scholarship, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the 2011–2013 period of new problem classes, outside DFO: derivative-based decomposition for convex MINLP. **Read**: partial (see the batch header): pp. 1–8 and pp. 9–22, with the proof parts listed above skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Outer approximation (OA: Duran–Grossmann, Fletcher–Leyffer) and generalized Benders decomposition (GBD: Geoffrion) alternate NLP subproblems with an MILP master problem. Their equivalence and finite-termination theory assumes the NLP subproblems are solved exactly (p. 2). Entry: what becomes of the cuts, the equivalence and finiteness when the NLPs satisfy only inexact KKT conditions? These are (1)–(2) for feasible subproblems and (3)–(5) for the ℓ∞ feasibility problem NLPF (pp. 2–3). One case is excluded up front: inexactness in classifying NLP feasibility (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Keep the OA and GBD loops (Algorithms 2.1 and 3.1, pp. 11 and 15, written after Fletcher–Leyffer, p. 10) and change only the cuts. The objective cut uses the perturbed gradient (∇_x f, ∇_y f − r^j). The constraint cut has right-hand side t^j_i = s^j_i/λ^j_i. The infeasibility cuts use ∇_y g_i − v^k/(1 − w^k) with a right-hand side a^k_i built from z^k, w^k and u^k (master problem with (7)–(8), p. 4). With zero residuals the master problem reduces to the standard one (p. 4). Objective linearizations are also added at infeasible points, following Bonami et al. (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and g are convex and C¹, and X is a bounded polyhedron (p. 2). The approximate NLP solutions are feasible and the inexact multipliers are nonnegative (p. 22). Assumptions 2.1–2.2 bound the residuals by a fraction (τ, σ_i, β_i, η_i ∈ [0, 1]) of the linearization gap, e.g. ‖r^l‖ ≤ −τ[(∇f^l)ᵀ(x^j − x^l; y^j − y^l) + f^l − f^j]/‖y^j − y^l‖ (p. 5). The bounds must hold for every pair of discrete assignments, which, the authors note, requires inexact solutions at all possible assignments (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 2.1 (p. 6): the master problem has the same optimal value as the perturbed MINLP P̄, the best pair over feasible assignments with their approximate solutions (eq. (6), p. 3). Part I multiplies the infeasibility cuts at x^k by µ^k and sums them. By (3)–(5) the left side vanishes and the right side equals −u^k < 0, so x^k is cut off (pp. 6–7). Part II shows that (y^j, f(x^j, y^j)) is feasible in the master problem. Cauchy–Schwarz plus Assumption 2.1 leave (1 − τ) × (convexity gap) ≤ 0 (pp. 7–8). Finiteness (Theorem 2.2, p. 11) follows because no discrete assignment repeats. Remark 3.1: inexact OA cuts still imply inexact GBD cuts, so OA's lower bounds stay stronger (p. 14). The exact results come back as Corollaries 2.1 and 3.1 (pp. 9, 14). Appendix A.1 shows that an inexact KKT point is an exact solution of a perturbed NLP (p. 22).
- **D5 Experiments**: MATLAB fmincon and CPLEX cplexmilp on 19 AMPL problems from MacMINLP and CMU-IBM (Table 1, p. 17). The NLP tolerance tightens with the change in the upper bound (eq. (29), p. 16). Exact cuts (e.cuts) are compared with inexact cuts (i.cuts), reporting iterations N and the count C of Assumption 2.1–2.2 inequalities violated during the run (p. 16). The assumptions are not enforced. With e.cuts, OA cycled on several problems where i.cuts did not, and i.cuts usually took fewer iterations (p. 18). GBD needed more iterations and violated more inequalities (pp. 18, 21).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Theory for inexact OA and GBD (equivalence and finiteness), plus numerical evidence that the cuts should change when the NLPs are solved inexactly (p. 20). Limits are stated concretely. Imposing the assumptions would require NLP solves at all assignments, which would defeat the purpose of inexactness (p. 20). The theory needs convexity, feasible approximate solutions, nonnegative multipliers and a correct feasibility classification (p. 22). A larger test set and use inside MINLP solvers are out of scope (p. 20). Grossmann's remark that inexact OA sits between exact OA and the extended cutting-plane method is credited (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, outside DFO (pp. 4–6, 11, 15): the OA and GBD loops are untouched. Only the cut, the object that decides which points remain valid, absorbs the residuals. The exact theory is re-derived, and the exact case returns as a corollary.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pp. 5, 20): the requirement to solve subproblems exactly is relaxed, but to a deterministic residual bound that cannot be checked in practice. Method 3's step 3 instead turns its threshold into a concrete rule.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 16–21): public AMPL collections, but only tables of N and C, no profiles and no mention of released code.
- new-pattern candidate: disclosed theory–implementation gap (pool): the numerics ignore the assumptions and count their violations (pp. 16, 20).
- new-pattern candidate: nest the known theories as special cases (pool): Corollaries 2.1 and 3.1 (pp. 9, 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Represent inexactness as a perturbed problem. An inexact KKT point of an NLP is an exact KKT point of an NLP with an extra linear term −rᵀ(y − y^j) and shifted constraints, so the exact theory applies to the perturbed problem (Appendix A.1, p. 22; eq. (6), p. 3).
- Keep a perturbed cut valid by bounding the slope perturbation by a fraction τ of the convexity gap. The remaining (1 − τ) share keeps the cut below the function at the other points (Assumption 2.1, Fig. 1, pp. 5–8).
- When a theoretical condition cannot be imposed, run without it and report, per problem, how many of its inequalities were violated during the run (the C column, pp. 16–21).
- Tie the inner tolerance to outer progress: min{10⁻², max{10⁻⁶, 10^(−θ)·|UBD_{p−1} − UBD_{p−2}|}} (eq. (29), p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "Our treatment of inexactness does not, however, address inexactness in the classification of NLP subproblems feasibility." (p. 2)
- "Such requirement would then undermine the practical purpose of saving computational effort aimed by the NLP subproblem inexactness." (p. 20)

### S061 · Efficient cardinality/mean-variance portfolios (System Modeling and Optimization, IFIP Advances in Information and Communication Technology 2014, DOI 10.1007/978-3-662-45504-3_6)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. P. Brito (Coimbra) · **Position in trajectory**: the DMS line (S003, 2011) carried into finance, in the 2011–2013 period. **Read**: full text of pp. 1–15; pp. 16–22 contain references and figure captions only.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: A cardinality constraint card(w) ≤ K turns Markowitz's QP into an MIQP that has to be solved again for every K (pp. 5–6). DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal found that no model consistently beats the naive 1/N strategy (pp. 1, 5). Earlier tri-objective studies did not test out-of-sample performance (p. 6). Entry: make cardinality an objective (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Biobjective problem (5): minimize −µᵀw/wᵀQw (expected return per unit of variance, parameter-free) and card(w), subject to eᵀw = 1 and bounds, with short selling allowed (pp. 2, 7). Because card is discontinuous, it is solved with DMS (dms version 0.2), used off the shelf (pp. 7–8, 13). Appendix A lists the modifications (p. 14). The cardinality becomes a count of |w_i| > ε with ε = 10⁻⁸. The bounds become symmetric (±10). The equality is removed by substituting w_N, because direct search copes poorly with equality constraints. The budget rises from 20000 to 2,000,000 evaluations, the step tolerance drops from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁷, the cache is off, and the initial list is the origin alone (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Mean and covariance are estimated from historical returns: continuous returns in-sample, discrete returns out-of-sample (p. 8). Returns are linear in the weights and variance is the risk measure. The paper cites fat tails as a reason to try other measures later (p. 13). No convergence claim is made beyond noting that DMS has some convergence theory even for discontinuous functions (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: None (no theorems).
- **D5 Experiments**: FTSE 100 sets of 12, 24 and 48 securities (2003–2007), Fama/French FF10/17/48 (1971–2011), and a 2003–2010 FTSE set that includes the crisis (pp. 7–8, 11). Baselines: 1/N; maximum return per variance (6); minimum variance without short selling (7); a cardinality-constrained minimum-variance MIQP for every K, solved with CPLEX (8) (pp. 8–9). Rolling-sample out-of-sample tests with the Sharpe ratio, 50-bp proportional transaction costs, and the Sharpe ratio net of costs (pp. 9–11). p-values for the Sharpe differences against 1/N were computed but not reported, since the differences were not statistically significant (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A direct way to get the whole cardinality/mean-variance front, for all cardinalities, in one run. Many sparse efficient portfolios beat 1/N in out-of-sample Sharpe ratio (pp. 11–13). The limits, most of them stated, are these. Net of transaction costs, the portfolios do not beat 1/N on FTSE data, and the MIQP portfolios do better on the net Sharpe ratio (p. 12). The Sharpe differences are not statistically significant (p. 10), which the abstract, where such portfolios overcome the naive strategy out-of-sample, does not mention (p. 1). The problem structure was ignored: the derivatives of the smooth first objective were unused and the poll was not run in parallel (p. 13). FF100 needed on the order of 10⁷ evaluations (p. 12). Future work: any black-box measure of risk and profit (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, and Heuristic #4 (pp. 6–7, 12–13): the K-parameterized constraint becomes a second objective, and the front is computed without aggregation. The ℓ1-penalty approach of Brodie et al. is contrasted as needing a tuned parameter and as lacking a stated efficiency property (p. 12).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 8–14): the released solver (dms) is reused and the parameters changed for the application are documented. Evaluation uses domain metrics (Sharpe ratio, costs) on six data sets, without profiles.
- Taste #6 ✅ evidence (finance application, pp. 7–13).
- new-pattern candidate: reformulate to reuse existing DFO solvers (pool): Appendix A (p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate: promote the swept parameter to an objective (pp. 6–7, 12–13).
- new-pattern candidate: state where you lose (pool): the net-of-cost results and the MIQP comparison (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When users sweep a model parameter (here K), make the underlying quantity an objective and compute one Pareto front that covers all its values (pp. 6–7, 12–13).
- To give a discontinuous objective to a direct-search solver: use an ε-thresholded count, a symmetric box, and remove the single equality by substitution so that only bounds remain (p. 14).
- Out-of-sample protocol for a portfolio method: rolling window, Sharpe ratio, Sharpe ratio net of proportional costs, a rerun on a crisis period, and 1/N as the benchmark (pp. 9–11).
- List every solver parameter changed from its default: budget, tolerance, cache, initial list (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "Direct multisearch was applied off-the-shelf to determine the cardinality/mean-variance efficient frontier." (p. 13)
- "It is unclear what sort of efficient or nondominant property their portfolios satisfy." (p. 12)

### S071 · A surrogate management framework using rigorous trust-region steps (Optimization Methods and Software 2014, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2012.719508)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Gratton · **Position in trajectory**: a bridge between the search/poll direct-search line (1996–2010) and derivative-free trust regions (S006; the DFO book [7]). **Read**: full (20 pages; references skimmed).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The surrogate management framework of Booker et al. (1998) places surrogates or heuristics in the optional search step of direct search. The poll is the rigorous step that ensures decrease for small steps (p. 2). No such framework existed for derivative-free trust-region methods, which have no mesh, so a search step there must use sufficient decrease (p. 2). Problem: unconstrained min f without derivatives (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 2.1 (pp. 3–4). The search step accepts x if f(x) ≤ f(x_k) − ρ(Δ_k); it then keeps or increases Δ and skips the TR step. Otherwise one step of a trust-region method is taken. Algorithm 2.2 (pp. 5–6) instantiates this with the derivative-free TR method of [6]: a criticality step, a fraction of Cauchy decrease, a ratio test with η0 and η1, a model-improvement step, and radius reduction only when the model is fully linear. There are four types of TR iteration (pp. 6–7). A second contribution is to rewrite the TR convergence proof in direct-search form (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f has a Lipschitz gradient on the enlarged level set L_enl(x0) (Assumption 3.1, p. 7). There is a fully linear class of models with a model-improvement algorithm (Definition 3.1, p. 8). f is bounded below and the model Hessians are bounded (Assumptions 3.2–3.3, p. 8). ρ satisfies ρ(Δ) → 0 ⇒ Δ → 0 (Lemma 3.2, p. 9). The search step only has to evaluate f at finitely many points (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Lemma 3.2 shows Δ_k → 0 (pp. 9–10). With finitely many successes, the radius shrinks at least once every N iterations. With infinitely many, both kinds of successful decrease (ρ(Δ_k), and η1 times the Cauchy decrease) must tend to zero, and Δ can only grow by the factor γ_inc. Theorem 3.1 gives liminf ‖∇f(x_k)‖ = 0 (pp. 10–11). The radius is reduced infinitely often, only in TR steps, and in each of the three reducing cases ‖g_k‖ = O(Δ_k). This rewriting is impossible for derivative-based TR methods, whose radius can be shown to stay bounded away from zero (p. 3). The lim result (Theorem 3.2, pp. 11–13) needs ρ(Δ) = γ_ρΔ, a search step inside B(x_k; γ_fac Δ_k) and a model that is fully linear there. This is the analogue of direct search's requirement that the search step be essentially empty for a lim result (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: Nonlinear least squares, 53 problems from the Moré–Wild set. The base solver is the interpolation-based TR method [3], without any geometry safeguards. The search step is a Gauss–Newton TR step whose Jacobian comes from n simplex gradients over the n closest sample points; it is accepted if f ≤ f_k − 10⁻⁵Δ_k², and Δ is then doubled. Data profiles use budgets N = 100 and 200 and τ = 10⁻¹, 10⁻⁵ (pp. 13–17). The search step helps. It has no effect at very small budgets because the 2n+1 initial points are spent first (pp. 15–16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: An algorithmic framework and a reorganized proof; the numerics are an illustration. Future work: exchanging sample points between the search and TR steps, which is application dependent (p. 18); replacing the rigorous step by the Scheinberg–Toint self-correcting geometry method (p. 18); applying the same principle to derivative-based TR methods (p. 18). No complexity bound.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 2–4, 9–11): a free search slot glued by sufficient decrease. Convergence comes from the rigorous step, and the radius is never reduced in a search step.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 3–4): the TR skeleton is kept. The acceptance test for the added step is sufficient decrease in ρ(Δ_k), because a trust region has no mesh.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 14–16): an on/off ablation of one solver on data profiles (Method 5 step 1), but no external baselines, and releasing code is not mentioned.
- Method 2: no link (asymptotic results only).
- new-pattern candidate: transplant the proof skeleton across algorithm families (pp. 2–3, 9–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Add a search step to any TR method. Accept its point only if f ≤ f_k − ρ(Δ_k), never shrink Δ after a search step, and carry out the analysis on the rigorous steps only (Algorithm 2.1, Lemma 3.2, pp. 3–4, 9–10).
- A short liminf proof for DFO trust regions: prove Δ_k → 0 first, then show that every radius-reducing iteration has ‖g_k‖ = O(Δ_k) and a model-gradient error ≤ κ_eg Δ_k (pp. 9–11).
- A structure-exploiting search step for least squares: scale the n closest sample points by Δ(Y_k), compute m simplex gradients with one matrix, and minimize ‖J_k p + F(x_k)‖² in a ball of radius θΔ_k (pp. 13–14).
- Ablate one addition on data profiles without retuning the base method in its favour, and explain at which budgets the effect cannot show (pp. 15–16).

**Quotes**:
- "the benefits of the search step would even be more noticed, but we did not want to tailor the underlying method to the benefit of this step." (p. 16)
- "the principle of a search or oracle step can also be applied to most derivative-based trust-region methods described in [5]." (p. 18)

### S097 · Globally convergent DC trust-region methods (Journal of Global Optimization 2014, DOI 10.1007/s10898-014-0170-6)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: H. A. Le Thi, H. V. Ngai, P. Dinh Tao (the DCA school), A. I. F. Vaz · **Position in trajectory**: a derivative-based side project in the 2014–2019 period, and a second DC-programming collaboration after S064. **Read**: full text of pp. 1–15. The Table 1 problem list and the profile plots were read as numbers and captions only; references skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: For min f over a closed convex set C, TR steps usually have to reach a fraction of the decrease at the generalized Cauchy point, which may need more than one projection (pp. 1–2). Entry: use DCA, a primal-dual subgradient method for DC programs, as the TR subproblem solver (p. 2). The experiments are limited to bound constraints (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The Taylor quadratic model is split into DC form, m = [ρ/2‖p‖² + χ_D(p)] − [ρ/2‖p‖² − m], with ρ = ‖∇²f(x_k)‖ + ε and D_k = (C − x_k) ∩ B(0, Δ_k) (pp. 4–5). Each DCA iteration is p^{l+1} = P_{D_k}(p^l − ∇m(x_k + p^l)/ρ), i.e. one projection (Algorithm 3.1, p. 5). The ratio test and radius update are standard (p. 5). With p⁰ = 0 and l0 = 1 the step resembles a DCA step on the original problem (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is C² with a Lipschitz Hessian on C, and the model uses the exact Hessian (Theorem 3.1, p. 6; abstract, p. 1). The proof uses that f is bounded below (p. 7), although the theorem statement does not list it (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Convexity of the second DC component plus the projection inequality give m(p^l) − m(p^{l+1}) ≥ (ρ/2)‖p^l − p^{l+1}‖². So the model decrease after l0 inner steps is ≥ ρ‖p_k‖²/(2l0) (eq. (8), p. 6). Against the Taylor error κΔ³/6, |τ_k − 1| ≤ κl0‖p_k‖/(3ρ). Small radii therefore always succeed, and Δ_k ≥ Δ_min > 0. The decrease bound (9) gives ‖x_{k+1} − x_k‖ → 0. Passing the projection optimality condition to the limit gives −∇f(x∞) ∈ N(C, x∞) (pp. 6–7). The result covers every limit point, not just a subsequence. Corollary 3.1 gives the same result with the ratio τ_new = 2l0(f(x_k) − f(x_k + p_k))/(ρ‖p_k‖²) (p. 7). The authors believe a second split with ρ = max{−λ_min, 0} + ε would also converge, but each inner iteration would cost more (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Experiments**: TRDC is written in C for a fair comparison of CPU times. It uses an ℓ∞ trust region and an adaptive ρ^l that doubles from 2⁻²(‖∇²f‖ + ε). The inner loop stops when the model decrease is ≥ 10³‖p‖², a rule the authors call consistent with the theory (p. 8). 271 unconstrained and bound-constrained CUTEr problems (p. 9). Baselines: TRON, IPOPT and Lancelot B, compared on CPU time (p. 11). Performance profiles are shown in two forms: the modified form of [33], which handles zero times, and the original form after removing the easy problems, where some solver needs zero CPU time (pp. 9–15). Some unsuccessful exit flags are overridden when the final objective is close to another solver's (Table 2, p. 13). TRDC is competitive, especially for n ≤ 2000, with about 90% robustness (pp. 11, 13, 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A new minimum requirement on the TR step, different from the Cauchy and generalized Cauchy points, proved by what the authors call a short and clean argument (p. 15), plus a competitive solver. Limits: exact second derivatives are required (pp. 1, 15). The results are first order, with no rate and no complexity bound (p. 6). The tests cover only bound constraints (p. 2). There is no future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 5–6, 15): the TR skeleton and ratio test are kept. What changes is the step-quality requirement (a DCA-step decrease in place of Cauchy decrease), not the acceptance test.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 8–15): a C implementation, a 271-problem collection, the strongest derivative-based baselines, and two profile variants.
- Taste #3 (minimal modification) ✅ evidence (p. 5): DCA is used unchanged, and the minimal effort per iteration is one projection.
- Method 2: no link (asymptotic results only).
- new-pattern candidate: implementable accuracy conditions (pool): the inner stopping test is the inequality the proof uses (p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate: import a collaborator's method as the subproblem engine (pp. 2–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Proximal DC split for a TR subproblem: choose ρ ≥ ‖∇²m‖ so that ρ/2‖p‖² − m is convex. Every inner iteration is then one projection, and the decreases telescope to at least ρ‖p‖²/(2l0) (pp. 4–6).
- A model decrease of order ‖p‖² against a Taylor error of order ‖p‖³ gives a radius floor Δ_min, and with it a result for every limit point rather than a liminf (pp. 6–7).
- Make the inner-loop stopping rule the exact inequality the proof uses, with a free constant, and say so in the paper (p. 8).
- CPU-time profiles when timings round to zero: use the shifted ratio of [33], and also the original profiles on the non-trivial subset; report both (pp. 11–13).

### S057 · Globally convergent evolution strategies for constrained optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10589-015-9747-3)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Y. Diouane (PhD student, supported by TOTAL, p. 1), S. Gratton · **Position in trajectory**: the 2014–2019 ES line. A constrained follow-up to the unconstrained globally convergent ES of Math. Program. 2015 ([17]). **Read**: full text of pp. 1–19, including Tables 3–4; references skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: [17] made a class of (µ/µ_W, λ)-ES, such as CMA-ES, globally convergent. It imposed sufficient decrease on the weighted mean and reduced the step size when that failed, but covered only unconstrained problems (p. 2). ES constraint handling in the evolutionary literature relies mostly on penalties (p. 2). Problem: min f over Ω with unrelaxable constraints (f cannot be evaluated outside Ω), from a feasible start (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 2.1 (p. 4): offspring are y = x_k + σ_k d̃, where the d̃ are computed from the ES directions. Selection and the sufficient-decrease test use the extreme barrier f_Ω (the death penalty of evolutionary computation, p. 3). On success σ_{k+1} = max{σ_k, σ^ES_k}; on failure σ shrinks. There are two instantiations. ES-LC-B adds positive generators of the ε-active tangent cone (ε_k = O(σ_k); Algorithm 3.1 computes them from a QR factorization of the active rows) to the ES directions and lets selection choose (pp. 7–10). ES-LC-P projects all offspring onto Ω, using ℓ2 for bounds and ℓ1 (an LP) for linear constraints. This is the same as using the modified directions d̃ = (Φ(x + σd) − x)/σ (pp. 8–10). An optional search step minimizes an interpolation or regression quadratic in a box of radius θσ_k (pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is bounded below and Lipschitz near the limit point, and the hypertangent cone there is nonempty (pp. 5–6). The directions are bounded. Two conditions must hold on the mean direction a_k: (i) σ_k‖a_k‖ → 0 and (ii) ρ(σ_k)/(σ_k‖a_k‖) → 0 (p. 6). The iterates must be bounded for a convergent refining subsequence to exist (Lemma 2.1, p. 5). The ES distribution C_k is left unspecified (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Lemma 2.1, taken from [17], gives a refining subsequence of unsuccessful iterations with σ_k → 0 (p. 5). Theorem 2.1 gives f°(x*; d) ≥ 0 for the Clarke–Jahn derivative along refining directions in the hypertangent cone, and Clarke stationarity if these directions are dense (p. 6). The proof inserts x_k + σ_k a_k into the lim sup and uses f_Ω(x_k + σ_k a_k) > f(x_k) − ρ(σ_k) (pp. 6–7). Theorem 2.2: when the constraint derivatives are known, density is needed only in the union of the tangent cones at nearby boundary points (p. 7). The key point is that sufficient decrease, not an integer lattice, drives σ to zero. Tangent-cone generators, which may be irrational for nonlinear constraints, can therefore be used (p. 8). The projection approach satisfies (i) through the Lipschitz constant of Φ (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: 114 bound-constrained and 107 linearly constrained AMPL problems (p. 13). Baselines: BCDFO, CMA-ES (with a penalty), MCS and PSWARM, all with default parameters (pp. 11–12). Performance profiles on evaluations (budget 3000, α = 10⁻², 10⁻⁴) and data profiles (budget 1500, α = 10⁻³, 10⁻⁷), averaging 10 runs of each stochastic solver (pp. 13–16). Bounds: BCDFO is the most efficient, and ES-LC-B and MCS are the most robust. Linear constraints: ES-LC-P beats PSWARM and ES-LC-B has the worst performance profile, while PSWARM leads in the data profiles (pp. 14, 16). The search step was kept on for bounds and switched off for linear constraints, where it did not help (p. 12). Nonlinear constraints: ES-EB against MADS-EB (NOMAD 3.6.1 with models disabled) on G1–G13 plus three engineering problems, after a feasibility phase that minimizes Σ max(c_i, 0), with budgets of 2000 and 20000 (pp. 16–20).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A framework, two operational variants and a solver comparison (pp. 1, 19). Limits: the nonlinear tests are described as essentially a proof of concept (p. 3) and as less extensive (p. 19). Only unrelaxable constraints are treated; relaxable constraints, handled with a merit function, are in Diouane's thesis (p. 19). ES-LC-B is weak on linear constraints (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 4, 10, 12): ES generation, the CMA-ES parameters and the ES step size are kept. σ resets to the CMA step whenever that is larger, and the ES directions stay at more than 90% of the selected directions.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, and Heuristic #6 (pp. 3, 7–9): unrelaxable constraints enter only through the extreme barrier in the acceptance test. One theorem covers both ways of conforming the directions once (i)–(ii) are checked.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 11–18): two profile types, the strongest DFO baselines including the unsafeguarded CMA-ES, a search-step on/off ablation, and a report of the variant that loses.
- new-pattern candidate: exploit the problem's own geometry (pool): ε-active tangent-cone generators are added to random directions (pp. 9–10).
- new-pattern candidate: state where you lose (pool): ES-LC-B is worst on linear constraints and PSWARM leads in the data profiles (pp. 14, 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To make a randomized sampler respect constraints without abandoning its distribution, either (a) add positive generators of the ε-active tangent cone to its directions and let selection choose, or (b) project the samples and treat the projection as modified directions. Either way, only two scalar conditions on the mean direction have to be re-checked (pp. 6–10).
- Positive generators for an ε-active set: start with ε = min(0.1, 10σ_k) and halve it until the active matrix has full row rank. Then take D = [Z −Z Y −Y] from a QR factorization of C_kᵀ (Algorithm 3.1, pp. 9–10).
- For extreme-barrier methods with infeasible starts, first minimize Σ max(c_i, 0), and count constraint-violation evaluations (#g) separately from objective evaluations (#f) (pp. 17–19).
- For stochastic solvers in data profiles, average f_L over the runs and average the profile over the runs (pp. 14–16).

**Quotes**:
- "What makes it possible to derive a result like Theorem 2.2 valid for nonlinear constraints is the use of sufficient decrease (which takes care of the need to drive the step-size parameter to zero)." (p. 8)
- "Our experience tells us that the ES directions are still predominant, representing more than 90% of the overall selected directions." (p. 10)

### S043 · Levenberg-Marquardt methods based on probabilistic gradient models and inexact subproblem solution, with application to data assimilation (SIAM/ASA Journal on Uncertainty Quantification 2016, DOI 10.1137/140974687)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: E. Bergou, S. Gratton · **Position in trajectory**: the 2014–2019 probabilistic line. The probabilistic trust-region theory of Bandeira, Scheinberg & Vicente 2014 ([1] = S014) is carried over to Levenberg–Marquardt and data assimilation. **Read**: partial (see the batch header): pp. 1–15 and 19–26 in full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: In weather prediction, 4DVAR is a large nonlinear least-squares problem solved by Gauss–Newton (incremental 4DVAR). Ensemble methods (EnKS) replace the true gradient by a stochastic one (pp. 1–2, 15–16). The LM regularization term globalizes Gauss–Newton while keeping the subproblem structure that EnKS needs (p. 2). Entry: LM with a gradient model that is accurate only with some probability, and subproblems solved inexactly (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Assumption 3.1 (p. 5): with probability at least p, conditioned on the past models, the gradient model lies within κ_eg/Γ_j^α of the true gradient, with α ∈ (0, 2]. Algorithm 3.1 (pp. 5–6) uses the ratio test. On success, γ increases if ‖g_m‖ < η2/γ²; otherwise γ_{j+1} = max{γ_j λ^((1−p_j)/p_j), γ_min}. On failure γ_{j+1} = λγ_j. Here p_j is a guess of the probability, kept in [p_min, p_max]. Following [1], and unlike classical LM, γ increases when the model gradient is small relative to 1/γ² (p. 5). With exact gradients the update reduces to the classical one (p. 6). Three inexact steps are allowed: Cauchy, truncated CG, and inexact normal equations with relative residual ε_j ≤ min{θ_in/γ_j^α, sqrt(β_in γ_j²/(‖J_m‖² + γ_j²))} (Assumption 4.2, p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is C¹ with a Lipschitz gradient on the level set, and the Jacobian models are uniformly bounded (Assumption 5.1, p. 8). The probability guesses satisfy 0 < p_min ≤ p_j ≤ p_max < 1 (p. 5). As a least-squares objective, f is bounded below by zero (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Lemma 5.2 (pp. 8–9): if the gradient model is accurate and γ_j ≥ (κ_j/(1 − η1))^{1/α}, then ρ_j ≥ η1. Lemma 5.3: γ_j → ∞ on every realization (p. 9). Theorem 5.1: liminf ‖∇f(X_j)‖ = 0 almost surely (p. 10). The proof follows [1, Thm 4.2] (pp. 10–11). W_j = Σ(1_{S_i}/p_i − 1) is a submartingale whose increments are bounded and bounded away from zero, so lim sup W_j = ∞ almost surely (Lemma 5.4, from Durrett). B_j = log_λ(b/γ_j) rises by (1 − p_j)/p_j on accurate iterations and falls by at most 1 otherwise. Hence B_j − B_{j0} ≥ W_j − W_{j0}, which contradicts B_j < 0. The p_j inside the γ update is what makes B dominate W.
- **D5 Experiments**: Rosenbrock as least squares, 60 runs with Gaussian gradient noise. Three choices are compared: p̃_j from the χ²(n) distribution (eq. (9); the file prints the symbol as CDF⁻¹), p_j = 1, and p_min = 5·10⁻³. With p_j = 1 the method stagnates (pp. 11–13). Expensive-gradient case: the exact gradient is called with probability p̄ (Algorithm 6.1, Lemma 6.1; pp. 13–15). 4DVAR twin experiment on Lorenz–63 with EnKS and finite-difference tangents, for ensemble sizes N = 4, 40, 80, 400. With p_j = 1 the final objective stays at about 10⁵, while p̃_j reaches 62–305 (Table 2, pp. 23–25).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Almost-sure global convergence of LM with probabilistic gradients and quantified inexactness, plus practical recipes for p_j (p. 25). Limits: ensuring (p_j)-probabilistic accuracy is named as the main difficulty in applying the method (p. 25). Small ensembles (N < n) need pseudo-inverses that the theory does not cover (p. 22). Large realistic data-assimilation problems are future work (p. 25). The result is a liminf only, with no rate (p. 10). Positioning: against Bocquet–Sakov, who nest the minimization inside EnKS and use classical LM without control of the ensemble derivative approximations (pp. 25–26). The text also contains a cross-reference slip: p. 23 cites a Proposition 4.2, which does not exist in the file; the finite-difference rule is Proposition 7.2 (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 5, 9–11): accuracy is required only with a probability conditioned on the past. The proof is a submartingale argument and gives almost-sure convergence.
- Method 3 step 6 ✅ evidence (pp. 11–13, 23–25): the deterministic update (p_j = 1) is run against the probabilistic one on the same problems, and the deterministic one stagnates.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 5–6): the LM skeleton is kept. The only changes are the γ update and its trigger, and exact gradients recover the classical update.
- Taste #6 ✅ evidence (data assimilation, pp. 15–25).
- new-pattern candidate: condition on the past, not independence (pool) (p. 5).
- new-pattern candidate: derive the probability from the noise model or certify it by construction (pp. 11, 13–14, 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Tie the accuracy requirement to the algorithm's own parameter: κ/γ^α, where any α ∈ (0, 2] works because γ ≥ γ_min (p. 5).
- Put the guessed probability into the parameter update, γ_{j+1} = γ_j λ^((1−p_j)/p_j), so that a log-parameter process dominates the submartingale W_j (pp. 6, 10–11).
- Compute p_j from the error model. For Gaussian noise, evaluate the χ²(n) distribution at (κ_eg/(σ_j γ^α))² (eq. (9), p. 11). For an ensemble of size N, the same formula carries a √N (eq. (33), p. 21).
- A mixed oracle with a guaranteed probability: draw U ~ U[0, 1/p̄] and call the exact gradient if U ≤ 1 (Algorithm 6.1, Lemma 6.1, pp. 13–14).
- Express the inexactness tolerance for the normal equations in the regularization parameter, and choose the finite-difference step so that the residual bound holds (Assumption 4.2, p. 7; Proposition 7.2, p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "The algorithm stagnated after some iterations, and could not approximate the minimizer with a descent accuracy." (p. 12)
- "but we presented a number of practical situations where this is achievable." (p. 25)

### S068 · A parallel evolution strategy for an earth imaging problem in geophysics (Optimization and Engineering 2016, DOI 10.1007/s11081-015-9296-8)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Y. Diouane, S. Gratton, X. Vasseur (CERFACS), H. Calandra (TOTAL) · **Position in trajectory**: the 2014–2019 ES line. This is the full-waveform inversion application named in SKILL.md Taste #6, the industrial problem behind Diouane's TOTAL-supported thesis (S057, p. 1). **Read**: partial (see the batch header): pp. 1–8 and 11–20 in full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Full-waveform inversion (FWI; frequency-domain Helmholtz equation, least-squares misfit (4)) is solved by Newton-type methods, which work only from a good, smooth starting velocity model; otherwise the inversion stalls in spurious local minima (pp. 2, 6). Industry builds the starting model by travel-time tomography (p. 2). Entry: compute that starting model by global search on massively parallel machines (pp. 1, 3). Obstacle: about 3.5·10⁶ unknowns, while ES suffer from the curse of dimensionality (pp. 6, 11).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Three contributions (p. 1). (1) Search-space reduction: block duplication plus a DCT magnification whose coefficients preserve the block means. This is a linear map m = Mg with M = KC⁻¹ that needs 320 parameters for the 225×225×70 SEG/EAGE salt model (pp. 8–11). (2) Algorithm 1 (p. 14), the globally convergent ES of [16] with the max/max sufficient-decrease test J(m̃^µ_{k+1}) ≤ J(m^µ_k) − ρ(σ_k). The numerically best variant, mean/mean, is set aside because it needs one extra evaluation that would stall all synchronized clusters (p. 13). The weight update is left off, to change the original ES as little as possible (p. 13). Velocity bounds are imposed by ℓ2 projection, which is globally convergent by [17] (pp. 13–14). (3) An MPI-2 master/cluster implementation in which the wave propagator is a black box (pp. 15–16).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Velocities are bounded in [1500, 4500] m/s (p. 13). The grid uses 10 points per wavelength (eq. (3), p. 4). The sources are known Dirac terms, and the data are synthetic, generated from the true model (p. 16). The paper proves no convergence theorem for the reduced, parallel, max/max algorithm; convergence is inherited from [16] and [17] (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Only Theorem 6.1 (appendix, p. 20): the DCT coefficient matrix C is nonsingular, shown by factoring the determinant and using the properties of DCT-III.
- **D5 Experiments**: CURIE machine, 2048 cores, 24-hour limit (p. 16). At 1 Hz, λ = 512 over 256 clusters; at 2 Hz, λ = 32 over 16 clusters; two evaluations per cluster; ρ(σ) = 10⁻⁴σ² and β = 0.5 (p. 17). At 1 Hz a smooth model recovers the salt dome, and J falls from 2042.113 to 575.8082 in 278 iterations before the time limit stops the run (p. 17). At 2 Hz, warm-started from the 1 Hz result, the run is stuck at a local minimum with J = 123.2841 after 1220 iterations (p. 18). Single runs, with no baseline method.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A parametrization, an adapted ES and a parallel solver, with the claim of a great improvement in automating the inversion (p. 19). Limits are stated concretely. The authors call the test scenario not truly realistic: the source excitation is usually unknown, and real data come from geophones at the surface (p. 19). The 2 Hz failure is attributed to a noisier, more multimodal objective, and a larger population is suggested as the remedy (p. 18). Future work: a realistic test case, and other geoscience problems (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 13–14): the ES is kept almost intact; only the sufficient-decrease test, the step shrink and the projection are added.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (p. 13): the globalization variant is chosen to fit the computing architecture (no serializing extra evaluation), not by the best numerical results.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 16–18): one real application, but single runs with no baselines and no profiles. It supplies the one real application asked for in Method 5 step 6.
- Taste #6 ✅ evidence (pp. 1–3): FWI for Earth imaging.
- new-pattern candidate: state where you lose (pool): the 2 Hz failure and the unrealistic scenario (pp. 18–19).
- new-pattern candidate: fit the algorithm variant to the parallel architecture (p. 13).
- new-pattern candidate: reduce the search space before global search (pp. 6–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If a globalization test needs an extra evaluation that serializes a parallel batch, switch to a test that uses only offspring already evaluated, such as comparing the µ-th best point of the new parent set with that of the old one (pp. 13–14).
- A smooth low-dimensional parametrization of a gridded field: block means plus a DCT, constrained so that each block average equals its parameter. The result is a fixed linear map M = KC⁻¹ that costs far less than one evaluation (pp. 8–9); prove that C is nonsingular (p. 20).
- Frequency continuation: solve at 1 Hz and warm-start the 2 Hz problem from that solution (p. 17).
- Set λ to a multiple of the number of clusters so that every cluster runs the same number of evaluations per iteration (p. 17).
