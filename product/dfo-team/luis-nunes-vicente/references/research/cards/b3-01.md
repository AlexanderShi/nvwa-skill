### S056 · Algorithms for Solving Non-Linear Constrained and Optimization Problems: The State of The Art (COCONUT Deliverable D1, project report, June 8 2001, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement (read as book, chapter level) · **Coauthors**: C. Bliek (ILOG), P. Spellucci (TU Darmstadt), L. N. Vicente (Univ. Coimbra), A. Neumaier (Univ. Vienna), L. Granvilliers, E. Monfroy and F. Benhamou (Univ. Nantes), E. Huens and P. Van Hentenryck (Catholic Univ. Louvain), D. Sam-Haroud and B. Faltings (EPFL) (p. 1). Chapter authorship: Ch. 1 and Ch. 3 Bliek (pp. 5, 48); Ch. 2 Spellucci, with the §2.1 overview credited to Vicente (p. 7); Ch. 4 Neumaier (p. 56); Ch. 5 Granvilliers, Benhamou, Huens (p. 114); Ch. 6 Granvilliers, Monfroy, Benhamou (p. 151); Ch. 7 Sam-Haroud, Faltings (p. 177). · **Position in trajectory**: 2001, at Coimbra (p. 7). It sits after his trust-region interior-point SQP period (corpus S037/S040 1996, S011 1998) and his bilevel bibliography (S002 1994, which this report cites on p. 104). It comes before his interior-point filter paper (S007 2004), his space-mapping papers (S062 2003, S044 2005) and SID-PSM (S008 2007). Vicente's own contribution is a three-page field map (pp. 7–9). The rest shows the multi-community setting (EU COCONUT project, p. 57) that he wrote for.

**Chapter map** (file pages):
- Title page (p. 1). Contents (pp. 2–4).
- Ch. 1 Introduction (Bliek, pp. 5–6). The goal is to summarize algorithms for nonlinear constrained and optimization problems studied in different communities, one area per chapter (p. 5). The project focuses on problems stated as arithmetic expressions (p. 6). The techniques are complementary rather than ranked: linear interval approximations work near a solution and propagation works far from it. This motivates solver cooperation (p. 6).
- Ch. 2 Nonlinear Local Optimization (Spellucci; §2.1 by Vicente, pp. 7–47):
  - §2.1 Vicente's overview: a component taxonomy of local NLP methods (pp. 7–9).
  - §2.2 introduction: history since Kuhn–Tucker 1951; convex problems are routine even in very high dimension, nonconvex ones resist (pp. 9–10).
  - §§2.3–2.4 notation and theory: NLP with C² data (the nonsmooth case is out of scope); MFCQ vs LICQ, including a two-variable example where MFCQ fails at infeasible points; second-order sufficiency (Theorem 2.4.3) (pp. 10–13; p. 12 not read).
  - §2.5 unconstrained: damped Newton, BFGS, partially separable updates, inexact Newton, preconditioned nonlinear CG, L-BFGS; backtracking and trust-region acceptance (pp. 13–18).
  - §2.6 bound-constrained: active set plus gradient projection, with a roundoff critique (pp. 18–20).
  - §§2.7–2.8 linear constraints (pp. 20–28). Active set (p. 20): MFCQ holds globally once redundant linear constraints are removed, and gradient projection is too expensive on polyhedra. p. 22 covers primal-dual reformulations (Friedlander–Martínez–Santos as a bound-constrained problem; Kanzow as an unconstrained one via the Fischer function). Interior-point part: pp. 21 and 23–27 not read. p. 28 closes it: nonconvex QP is NP-hard and these methods do not cover it.

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The report is Deliverable D1 of the EU COCONUT project, which aims to integrate existing systematic approaches into a uniform whole (pp. 1, 57). The book is organized by research community, not by problem. Its premise is that no technique dominates and the techniques complement each other (p. 6). Vicente's §2.1 enters local NLP by listing what current methods are made of. It does not start from history or from a single algorithm. The opening claim is that NLP methods are complex algorithms built from several components, each dealing with one feature of the problem (p. 7). From there the section works through: iterative step-direction computation as the central tool, and one that sets the cost (pp. 7–8); the two meanings of local/global (p. 8); globalization (p. 8); handling the topology of the feasible set (p. 8); solving sequences of parametrized problems (pp. 8–9); performance factors (p. 9); and problem and application classes (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: open a survey or a related-work section with a one-sentence thesis that the field's methods are *assemblies of components*. Then give one paragraph per component, with the 2–3 competing options for each. Every later paper can then be placed as changing component X and keeping Y (pp. 7–9).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design (chapter level)**:
  - **§2.1 (Vicente).** Step directions come from Newton, quasi-Newton/secant, gradient or reduced gradient, or nonlinear CG. SLP and SQP are presented as *frameworks* for computing directions, usually derived from first-order conditions (p. 8).
  - Globalization is either line search or trust region, and both need a merit function. For constrained problems the merit function is the objective plus a penalty on infeasibility. The recent filter replaces it with a multicriteria balance between objective and constraint violation (p. 8).
  - Feasible-region topology is handled by active-set methods (the simplex method is one), interior-point methods (the central path in LP; a quasi-central path and a link to barrier methods in NLP) or projections, which are cheap only for simple sets such as boxes (p. 8). Penalty, barrier and augmented Lagrangian methods are the examples of parametrized sequences (p. 9).
  - **Spellucci's body of Ch. 2** (pp. 13–43) is a catalogue organized by constraint type, from unconstrained to bounds to linear to nonlinear. The design contrast it keeps returning to is merit function vs filter. filterSQP treats NLP as a two-objective vector problem in (f, θ), and a step is accepted if it is not dominated by any filter entry (pp. 37–38).
  - **Neumaier's Ch. 4** classifies global methods by the *guarantee* they offer (heuristic / approximation / systematic), not by mechanism (pp. 67–68). He adds that heuristics and probabilistic choices serve systematic methods by cheaply supplying good local minimizers (p. 68).
  - Transferable technique: classify competing methods by the guarantee each one gives (none; exact for an approximation; exact with predictable work), in the manner of Neumaier's three classes (pp. 67–68). Then place your own method in that grid in one sentence.
- **D3 Assumptions & models**:
  - Vicente assumes deterministic data and names stochastic optimization as the excluded alternative (p. 7).
  - Spellucci assumes h and g in C² (some results need only C¹) and f in C² on an open superset of the feasible set. The nonsmooth case is declared out of scope (p. 10).
  - Constraint qualifications are treated as assumptions on the representation (f, g, h), not on the feasible set (p. 11). MFCQ is also stated at infeasible points, because algorithms need it there. The example with f = −x₁−x₂, the feasible set the quarter circle of radius √2 in the positive quadrant, and g(x) = x shows the problem: LICQ holds near the feasible set, but MFCQ fails for x₁<0, x₂<0, and renowned codes fail from starts there (p. 13).
  - Neumaier's systematic methods guarantee a global minimizer only in exact arithmetic and with predictable work relative to known constants, e.g. Lipschitz constants. For black boxes, stopping must be heuristic (p. 68).
  - Transferable technique: when you state a constraint qualification, check whether your algorithm needs it at *infeasible* iterates. Test from starting points where it fails (the p. 13 example is a ready-made test case).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**: No new analysis; this is a survey. The acceptance devices recorded are:
  - sufficient decrease with the requirement f(x^k)−f(x^{k+1}) → 0 ⇒ ∇f(x^k) → 0, a direction condition |∇fᵀd|/‖d‖ ≥ φ(‖∇f‖), and Armijo backtracking (p. 15);
  - the trust-region ratio test with η (e.g. 10⁻³) and radius update (p. 15);
  - the filter envelope test, θ(x^{k+1}) ≤ (1−γ_θ)θ_j or f(x^{k+1}) ≤ f_j − γ_θθ_j for all filter entries, plus a ratio test that decides the radius update and a purge rule (p. 38).
  
  Two negative results are also recorded. Spellucci's roundoff estimate shows that gradient projection can stop prematurely even for a mildly ill-conditioned Hessian (pp. 19–20). Neumaier notes that stochastic heuristics may converge with probability arbitrarily close to 1 when run arbitrarily long, and that this is not a convergence guarantee (p. 67).
  - Transferable technique: write the acceptance rule of any new method in the same reduction-≥-margin format as the Armijo, ratio and filter tests (pp. 15, 38). Readers can then see immediately which component you changed.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: §2.10 is not the authors' own experiment. It reproduces an excerpt of the COPS study (Dolan–Moré 2000): LANCELOT, LOQO, MINOS and SNOPT with default options, AMPL models (mostly discretized control problems plus three large QPs), and one machine (Sun Ultra Sparc 2, Solaris 7). The excerpt reports CPU seconds and the final objective at the lowest and highest dimension of each problem, with "F" for failure (premature termination or time-out) (pp. 44–46). The verdict, also supported by Mittelmann's benchmarks, is that there is no clear winner and that large-scale reliability lags small and medium scale (p. 46). Earlier in the chapter Spellucci asks for testing on an identical testbed before claiming relative merit (p. 20). Neumaier's §4.23 lists the collections (Floudas et al. handbook, Huyer–Neumaier, Janka, Moré–Garbow–Hillstrom, Hock–Schittkowski, CUTE, Vanderbei's AMPL set) and the Mathematical Programming Society reporting guidelines (pp. 112–113). He also notes that with limited evaluation budgets, comparisons of the relative efficiency of heuristics are essentially absent (p. 72).
  - Transferable technique: for a scalable problem family, report each solver at the smallest and the largest size, with time, final objective and an explicit failure marker, in one table (pp. 45–46). Failures stay visible and cannot be averaged away.
- **D6 Literature positioning**:
  - Vicente positions local NLP by separating it from global optimization, which needs a different treatment (p. 8). He defines global convergence as convergence to a candidate for local optimality from any starting point, and asks for good *global-to-local* behaviour: converge globally, then show a superlinear or quadratic local rate (p. 8).
  - Bliek calls local NLP the oldest and most established of the book's areas (p. 5).
  - Spellucci is openly evaluative. He says he does not share colleagues' enthusiasm for gradient projection (p. 19). He also judges the nonconvex results of one interior-point approach (Conn–Gould–Toint, with a crude regularization) to be a complete disappointment (p. 39).
  - Neumaier states that he makes personal value judgments instead of claiming objectivity (p. 57).
  - Vicente's only citation in the book is Vicente–Calamai 1994 (the bilevel bibliography). Neumaier cites it for linear complementarity constraints from bilevel programs, which are semilinear and therefore MILP-reformulable (pp. 103–104; bibliography p. 222).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: §2.1 has no citations at all (pp. 7–9). Its structure is:
  - a thesis sentence (components);
  - a run of paired contrasts: local vs global; line search vs trust region; merit function vs filter; active set vs interior point vs projection;
  - a closing list of problem classes (unconstrained, QP, linearly constrained, least squares, equality-constrained, box-constrained, convex);
  - application classes (optimal control, optimal design, parameter identification, inverse problems, finance, network and transportation);
  - a forward pointer to less classical techniques popular in engineering: derivative-free methods, response surfaces, surrogate modeling, space mapping (p. 9).
  
  Hedging is low and definitional. The one normative sentence is the global-to-local requirement (p. 8). By contrast, Spellucci and Neumaier write in the first person and give opinions (pp. 19, 57), and Neumaier gives cost rules of thumb that he himself labels unreliable (QP ≈ 5·LP, SQP ≈ 30·QP, finding a global minimizer ≥ 100·SQP, verifying it ≥ 1000·SQP) (p. 94).
  - Transferable technique: end a field overview with a short list of emerging techniques at its edge. Here, derivative-free methods, response surfaces, surrogates and space mapping were named in 2001 (p. 9), and the author's own work followed into several of them. The list signals where the author is heading without claiming results.
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**:
  - The contribution is a state-of-the-art survey spanning communities. Vicente's layer is taxonomic (pp. 7–9).
  - The limitations are specific and technical. Spellucci's conclusion (pp. 46–47) says that one must accept local solutions and that which local solution one gets is hard to control. Restoration needs linearly independent gradients of the violated constraints, and QR/SVD do not scale because of fill-in. Infeasibility cannot be detected from unbounded duals. Detecting nonconvexity from an indefinite Hessian of the Lagrangian is unreliable. Regularizing nonconvex problems in interior-point methods is open, and adding a multiple of the identity is inadequate.
  - Ch. 3: interval-derivative overestimation deserves attention, and mixed-mode Hessians are unstudied (p. 55). Ch. 6: how to budget symbolic vs numeric computation is open (p. 174). Ch. 7: extend set covering to the nonconvex case (p. 190).
  - Trajectory marker (inferred by comparing with later corpus papers, not claimed by the report): several items in Vicente's 2001 lists became his later topics. These are the filter (S007 2004, S077 2008), space mapping (S062 2003, S044 2005), derivative-free methods (S008 2007 onward), finance (S028 2008) and transportation (S080 2023), all named or listed on pp. 8–9. The item he excluded, stochastic data (p. 7), became Method 3 after 2014.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (Generalize the acceptance test, not the algorithm) — evidence ✅ (early, stated). §2.1 already writes NLP methods as separable components: direction, globalization with a merit function, feasibility handling, parametrization (pp. 7–9). It also presents the filter as replacing the merit function with a multicriteria acceptance rule (p. 8; mechanics pp. 37–38). This is the component view that Method 4, step 1 depends on, and dominance-based acceptance is the device DMS later uses.
- Research Taste mark 1 (Rigorous and efficient at the same time) — evidence ✅. The overview's one normative requirement is good global-to-local behaviour: global convergence plus a fast local rate (p. 8).
- Method 3 (Relax deterministic requirements to probabilistic ones) — variant ⚠ (scope marker, not a contradiction). In 2001 Vicente explicitly assumes deterministic data and sets stochastic optimization aside (p. 7). The probabilistic program is a later expansion.
- Method 1 (Heuristic inside a convergent skeleton) — variant ⚠ (context, not Vicente-authored). Neumaier gives heuristics a service role inside systematic methods (p. 68) and notes the missing efficiency comparisons for heuristics (p. 72). This is the same heuristic-plus-guarantee division of labour, in global rather than local optimization.
- Method 5 (Ship the solver, profile it on a collection) — evidence ✅ (context, not Vicente-authored). The 2001 norm in the book is a multi-solver comparison on a named AMPL collection, reporting failures (pp. 44–46), plus pointers to collections and reporting guidelines (pp. 112–113). There are no performance profiles yet.
- New-pattern candidate: **component-taxonomy field map**. Survey a field as interchangeable algorithm components with 2–3 options each, before any contribution (pp. 7–9). It should be checked against S038 (2017, *Methodologies and software for derivative-free optimization*).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Open a related-work section by stating that methods in this area are assemblies of components, then give one paragraph per component with its competing options (direction / globalization and acceptance / feasibility handling / parametrization / performance factors) (pp. 7–9).
- State the global-to-local requirement explicitly (convergence from any start, then a fast local rate) as the yardstick for your own method (p. 8).
- Use the filter envelope test (θ reduced by a factor (1−γ_θ) *or* f reduced by γ_θθ_j against every filter entry, plus a ratio test for the radius) as a template when the acceptance test has to balance two criteria (p. 38).
- Add a constraint-qualification stress test: start the solver where MFCQ fails but the feasible set is benign, e.g. the p. 13 quarter-circle example.
- For scalable test problems, tabulate smallest and largest sizes with time, final objective and an explicit "F" for failures, per solver (pp. 45–46).
- Classify rival methods by the guarantee they give (heuristic / approximation / systematic) and place your method in that grid (pp. 67–68).
