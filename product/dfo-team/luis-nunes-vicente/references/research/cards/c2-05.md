# Paper cards · batch c2-05 (core)

**Batch intent**: See how Vicente's Lehigh group (with T. Giovannelli, J. Tan and O. Sohab, 2023–2026) carries the DFO and multiobjective toolbox into machine-learning-facing stochastic optimization on three fronts: smoothing moved from direct search to stochastic gradient descent for compositional objectives (S107), neural networks tested as surrogates inside a DFO solver (S079), and set-valued modelling reduced to stochastic multiobjective optimization for robust learning (S101). Four questions: (a) does Method 4 (keep the skeleton, change one object) survive the move from direct search to SGD and multi-gradient methods, and what replaces the step-size-driven smoothing reduction of Heuristic 5? (b) What happens to the Method 2 yardstick when the benchmark is an ML compositional rate rather than the gradient method? (c) What does a negative-result paper from this group look like, and how does it use Method 5? (d) Does the c05 candidate *theory paper / application paper split* recur? S107 and S101 cite each other.
**Focus dimensions**: D2 (what changes relative to the base method), D3 (where the assumptions are placed and what they cost), D4 (proof skeleton, S107 only; the other two have almost no proofs), D5 (protocol), D6 (benchmark choice), D8. D1 and D7 are also filled because all three papers are core.
**Material roles**: S107 core (arXiv v2 dated 15 May 2026; read in full, including Appendices A–C) · S079 core (J. Global Optim. 2026; read from arXiv v1 2311.12253 dated 22 Nov 2023, in full, including Appendices A–B; the published version was not read) · S101 core (arXiv v1 2603.17691 dated 19 Mar 2026; read in full, including Appendices A–D). ### S107 · Non-smooth stochastic gradient descent using smoothing functions (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2507.10901)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. Giovannelli (Cincinnati), J. Tan (Lehigh) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh era (AFOSR and ONR funded, p. 27). The paper carries the smoothing idea of Garmanjani & Vicente's direct-search paper (ref. [18], p. 4; card S034 in s01) from derivative-free direct search to stochastic gradient descent. It supplies the SSG method that S101 uses as a component (S101 pp. 15, 25–26). Its non-convex proof draws on the group's inexact bilevel stochastic gradient paper, ref. [20] (p. 11). **Text read**: arXiv v2 dated 15 May 2026, pp. 1–37 in full, including Appendix A (p. 31), Appendix B (pp. 31–33) and Appendix C (pp. 34–37).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper treats two stochastic compositional problems, min ϕ(E[ψ(x,ξ)]) (1.1) and min E[ϕ(ψ(x,ξ))] (1.2), where ψ is differentiable and the outer ϕ may be non-smooth (pp. 1–2). The entry is a structural gap. SCGD (Wang–Fang–Liu [48]) handles non-smoothness in the *inner* function, whereas here it sits in the *outer* one (p. 4). The authors also know of no stochastic compositional method "that iteratively updates the smoothing parameter" (p. 4). The motivating instances are super- and sub-quantile objectives and the L1 expected risk E|x⊤u + b − v| (p. 2), plus a fairness covariance of the form |E[ψ]| (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: position the contribution by *where* the non-smoothness sits in a composition (inner or outer) relative to the closest method, and say it in one sentence (pp. 4, 16).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Replace f by a smoothing function f̃(·, µ) (Definition 1, p. 2). Run plain SGD on f̃ and drive µ_k to zero on a prescribed schedule (Algorithm 1, p. 7). The algorithm "only differs" from SGD in Step 3, µ_{k+1} ≤ µ_k (p. 7). Unchanged: the SGD step, one sample per step, no momentum and no variance reduction. Changed: the gradient is that of f̃ at the current µ_k. The schedule is not adaptive. The rate analysis fixes it in advance: µ_k = k^{−a} and α_k = k^{−b} with (a, b) = (1/4, 3/4) in the convex case (p. 10), and µ_k = α_k^{(2−d)/3} in the non-convex case (p. 11). There are two instantiations. For (1.1), two independent samples ξ¹, ξ² give the estimator ϕ̃′(ψ(x,ξ¹),µ)∇ψ(x,ξ²) (Algorithm 2, p. 17). For the finite-sum version of (1.2), a mini-batch average of ϕ̃′(ψ(x,ξ_i),µ)∇ψ(x,ξ_i) is used (Algorithm 3, p. 21). Appendix C adds an SCGD-like moving average y_{k+1} = (1−β_k)y_k + β_kψ(x_k,ξ¹) for the strongly convex version of (1.1) (Algorithm 4, p. 34). The smoothing examples are (2.2) for |t|, quadratic on [−µ/2, µ/2], and (2.3) for the hinge loss; mollifier convolution is given as the general construction (pp. 5–6).
  - Transferable technique: when adding one parameter to a standard method, write the algorithm box so that the only new line is that parameter's update, then state that the method only differs in that line (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: All assumptions are placed on the smoothed objective f̃, "rather than the original non-smooth objective f" (p. 7), and are then verified for the two problem classes in §§4–5.
  - *Core assumptions* (p. 7): unbiased smoothing gradients (A3.1), second moment ≤ G²/µ² (A3.2) and accuracy |f − f̃| ≤ Cµ (A3.3).
  - *Convex case*: f̃ is convex (A3.4) and the iterates are bounded (A3.5). Boundedness is justified by switching to a projected update onto a bounded convex set X (Remark 3.1, p. 8).
  - *Non-convex case* (p. 11): ∇f̃ is Lipschitz with constant C0/µ (A3.6); Σα_k = ∞ and Σα_k^d < ∞ (A3.7); |f̃(x,µ′) − f̃(x,µ)| ≤ C1|µ′ − µ| (A3.8). Results for true subgradients also need A3.9: within distance E1µ of x there is a subgradient that lies within E2µ of ∇f̃ (p. 14). Appendix A offers "strong evidence" for A3.9 when f = ϕ(ψ) (p. 31).
  - *Strongly convex case*: the modulus is c/µ (Assumption B.1). The paper admits that (2.2)–(2.3) satisfy it only "when x is relatively close to the kink point" (p. 31).
  - *Costs the paper states for the verification step*. Unbiasedness in case (1.1) needs ϕ̃′ to be linear. That requires restricting the sampling of ξ¹ so that ψ(x_k, ξ¹) lies in the smoothing region almost surely while preserving the mean, and "this argument does not extend directly to the hinge case" (pp. 18–19). Convexity in §4 needs an affine ψ (p. 17). Convexity for the L1 finite-sum case is argued only when the inner values are always nonnegative (p. 21). Appendix C further assumes that E[ϕ̃′(ψ(x),µ)∇ψ(x,ξ)] ∈ ∂f(x) for all µ (Assumption C.4, p. 35).
  - Reader's observation (not in the paper): the derivative of (2.2) is 2t/µ on |t| ≤ µ/2 and ±1 outside, and the derivative of (2.3) lies in [−1, 0] (p. 6). Both smoothings therefore have derivatives bounded by 1 whatever µ is. The paper justifies Assumption 4.2 instead by saying that the derivative is a multiple of 1/µ (p. 18). The G²/µ² scaling in A3.2 is looser than the paper's own examples require.
  - Transferable technique: state the analysis assumptions on an abstract smoothed objective, then give one section per problem class that verifies them, with one proposition per assumption (Propositions 4.1–4.3, pp. 18–19; Propositions 5.1–5.3, pp. 22–23).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - *Convex case* (Theorem 3.1, pp. 8–10). The proof chains the standard projected-SGD distance recursion (3.4) and the convexity of f̃ (3.5). It then divides by α_k and telescopes, using the bounded-iterate term 2M²/α_T (3.6). The accuracy bound Cµ, applied at x_k and at x*, takes f̃ back to f (3.7). Power-sum bounds (3.8)–(3.9) and a best-iterate bound (3.10) finish it. The result has three terms, 2Cc1/T^a + M²/T^{1−b} + c2G²/(2T^{b−2a}). They are balanced by maximizing min{a, 1−b, b−2a} "using an LP formulation", which gives (a, b) = (1/4, 3/4) and the rate 1/T^{1/4} (p. 10). The convex proof uses only unbiasedness, convexity, A3.2, A3.3 and A3.5. The smoothness of f̃ (A3.6) is never used (pp. 8–10).
  - *Non-convex case* (Theorem 3.2, pp. 11–13). A descent lemma with Lipschitz constant C0/µ_k is combined with a µ-change term C1(µ_k − µ_{k+1}) that comes from A3.8 (3.14). Taking expectations and telescoping gives Σα_kE‖∇f̃‖² ≤ const + (C0G²/2)Σα_k²/µ_k³ (3.18). The choice µ_k = α_k^{(2−d)/3} makes α_k²/µ_k³ = α_k^d, which is summable by A3.7 (pp. 12–13). Corollary 3.1 gives min E‖∇f̃‖² = O(T^{−(1−b)}), which can be made arbitrarily close to T^{−1/2} (p. 13). Corollaries 3.2–3.3 transfer the result to the nearby true subgradients g(y_k) through ‖a+b‖² ≤ 2‖a‖² + 2‖b‖² and A3.9. The price is the restriction d ∈ (1, 7/5] and a best rate of T^{−2/7+ε} (pp. 14–16).
  - *Strongly convex case* (Appendix B). With step α_k = 1/((k+1)c(µ_k)) and modulus c(µ) = c/µ, a weighted telescoping (B.5)–(B.6) and the bound ln T ≤ T^s/s give a rate that the paper calls arbitrarily close to 1/T^{1/2} (pp. 31–33).
  - *Appendix C*. The Lyapunov function is J_k = E‖x_k − x*‖² + Λ_kE‖y_k − ψ(x_{k−1})‖², with Λ_k = Θ(α_k/(β_kµ_k²)). The tracking lemma is imported from [48, Lemma 2(a)], and the rate is O(T^{−(2/3−4a)}) (pp. 35–37).
  - Transferable technique: when two schedules (smoothing µ_k and step α_k) enter a bound as competing powers of T, write the exponents as linear functions of (a, b) and maximize the smallest one as a small LP (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: couple the smoothing schedule to the step size (µ_k = α_k^{(2−d)/3}) so that the error term α_k²/µ_k³ collapses to a single summable power α_k^d (pp. 11–12).
  - Transferable technique: when a parameter µ changes between iterations, split f̃(x_{k+1},µ_{k+1}) − f̃(x_k,µ_k) into two parts. Bound the µ-change part with a Lipschitz-in-µ constant and handle the x-change part with the descent lemma (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Scope*: four UCI datasets and the finite-sum case only (Algorithm 3), with a linear inner model (pp. 24–26). The strongly convex algorithm and the (1.1)-type algorithm are not tested.
  - *Hinge loss*: QSAR Biodegradation and Breast Cancer Wisconsin, with labels mapped to ±1 (p. 24). Batch size 64, α_k = α0k^{−3/4} and µ_k = µ0k^{−1/4}, the convex-optimal pair of Theorem 3.1. Epochs 800/150, α0 1.2/1.0, µ0 4/2 (Table 1, p. 24).
  - *L1 loss*: Automobile Price and Yacht Hydrodynamics, batch size 128, α0 = 1, µ0 = 5, 800 epochs (p. 25).
  - *Protocol*: one random 75/25 split with standardization (pp. 24–25). The first baseline is a "non-smooth SGD" that ignores the kink. The second is SCGD [48], run directly on the non-smooth objective as "a compositional subgradient baseline"; the authors note that this lies outside SCGD's usual assumptions (p. 24). All methods use the same α_k = α0k^{−3/4} (pp. 24–25). The plots show test-loss curves (Figs. 1–2). The text mentions no repeated runs, seeds or error bars (pp. 24–26).
  - *Results*: SSG reaches the lowest final test loss on all four datasets and gives smoother curves. On QSAR it decreases more slowly at first (p. 24). The authors explain the smoothness mechanistically: samples near the kink no longer flip the subgradient from one mini-batch to the next (pp. 25–26).
  - Reader's observation: the baselines use the step exponent 3/4 chosen for SSG's convex rate, not the Θ(1/√t) step that the paper itself recalls for subgradient SGD (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: set the experimental schedules to the exponents your theorem declares optimal, and say so in the setup (p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: explain a qualitative difference between curves (smoothness) with a sample-level mechanism (active-set flips near the kink) rather than only reporting it (pp. 25–26).
- **D6 Positioning**: A history of GD and SGD rates from Cauchy (1847), Robbins–Monro and Polyak sets out the rate table (p. 3). The paper surveys the compositional literature (Ermoliev, SCGD, SVRG/SARAH/SAGA variants, prox-linear methods; pp. 3–4) and the smoothing literature (Nesterov, randomized, Moreau and Gaussian smoothing, and the group's own direct-search smoothing [18]; p. 4). Its rate claim is that it matches SCGD's T^{−1/4} in the convex case and comes close to SCGD's non-convex rate. Each time it adds that SCGD treats a different problem, one that is "complementary" to this paper's (pp. 10, 16). A faster alternative is anticipated and set aside: a saddle-point reformulation has O(T^{−1/2}) primal–dual rates, but it is not convex–concave when ψ is nonlinear (Remark 3.2, pp. 10–11). Lin–Chen–Peña's smoothing stochastic gradient method for composite optimization [28] is cited only as an accelerated proximal method for the additive form (p. 4).
  - Reader's observation: in the finite-sum case (1.2), unbiased stochastic subgradients are available, and the paper recalls on p. 3 that stochastic subgradient descent attains O(1/√T) for non-smooth convex objectives. The 1/T^{1/4} convex rate is compared with SCGD, not with this bound.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution paragraph (§1.3, p. 5) announces one algorithm, three regimes, two verified problem classes and "illustrative" numerics. Each assumption is introduced as "mild" and followed by a promise that it "will be shown to hold" in §§4–5 (p. 7). The strongly convex case goes to an appendix because it "has less applicability" (p. 7). Theorem 3.1, Corollary 3.3, Theorem B.1 and Theorem C.2 are each followed by a comparison with SCGD (pp. 10, 16, 33, 37). The numerics are "preliminary", and SSG "can be competitive for certain classes of problems" (p. 1). The conclusion draws one design lesson from the exponents: stronger curvature allows faster µ-decrease, which must be matched by faster step decay (pp. 26–27).
  - Transferable technique: end the paper with the design rule that the exponents imply (here, faster smoothing needs faster step decay), not only with the rates (pp. 26–27).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a simple algorithm, rates in three regimes, verification of the assumptions for two compositional classes, and a preliminary numerical study (p. 5). Stated limits: the unbiasedness argument does not cover the hinge case of (1.1) (p. 19); the non-convex rates concern the smoothing gradient and nearby subgradients, not stationarity of x_k itself (p. 16); double-expectation problems are not considered (p. 27). Future directions are variance reduction (SVRG, SAGA) and applications such as CVaR (p. 27). Limits that are visible but not stated: there is no code link; the numerics cover only the finite-sum class; the convex analysis does not exploit smoothness (D4). See also the reader's observations under D3 and D6.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The skeleton (SGD) is kept and only the object changes: f becomes f̃(·, µ_k), with one extra update line (p. 7). The price appears in the abstract: "at the price of increasingly higher Lipschitz constants" (p. 1).
- Heuristic 5 ⚠ variant. The smoothing idea of [18] (p. 4) moves from direct search to SGD, but µ is reduced on a fixed schedule tied to α_k (pp. 8, 11). Heuristic 5 reduces µ when a step-size indicator becomes small.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. Rates are counted in stochastic gradient evaluations (p. 1) and set next to a benchmark, but the benchmark is a neighbouring compositional method (SCGD), not the (sub)gradient method for the same class (pp. 10, 16). Step 4 (when the bound is worse, name the price explicitly) is only partly done: the gap to the O(1/√T) subgradient rate recalled on p. 3 is not discussed.
- Method 5 ⚠. The paper uses real datasets and the strongest named compositional baseline, but it has no problem collection and no profiles, reports no repeated runs and gives no code link (pp. 24–26).
- New-pattern candidate: *exponent balancing* (already a candidate from S034 and S032 in s01). It appears here as an explicit LP over the two schedule exponents (p. 10) and as the coupling µ_k = α_k^{(2−d)/3} (p. 11).
- New-pattern candidate: *abstract assumptions, then verify per instance*. The assumptions are stated on f̃ (p. 7), then verified with one proposition per assumption for each problem class (§§4–5, pp. 16–23).
- New-pattern candidate: *theory paper / application paper split* (from c05). S107 is the method-and-rates paper; S101 imports its SSG without theory (S101 pp. 15, 25–26). The two cite each other as technical reports (S107 ref. [21], p. 28; S101 ref. [18], p. 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Find the best pair of schedule exponents by maximizing the smallest exponent in a multi-term rate bound (LP, p. 10).
- Set the smoothing parameter to a power of the step size so that the error series reduces to Σα_k^d (pp. 11–12).
- Draw two independent samples so that a product estimator ϕ̃′(ψ(x,ξ¹))∇ψ(x,ξ²) is unbiased when ϕ̃′ is linear in the relevant region. State the sampling restriction that makes this true (pp. 17–19).
- Transfer a rate from smoothing gradients to true subgradients with an assumption that a subgradient within O(µ) exists nearby, and show that this assumption implies gradient consistency (pp. 14, 31).

**Quotes**:
- "it only differs from the traditional stochastic gradient algorithm in the update of the smoothing parameter" (p. 7)
- "Accordingly, our method smooths the outer function while preserving the compositional structure, whereas SCGD-type methods rely on auxiliary tracking sequences to approximate the inner expectation." (p. 16)

### S079 · The limitation of neural nets for approximation and optimization (Journal of Global Optimization 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10898-024-01426-9)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. Giovannelli, O. Sohab (both at Lehigh ISE at the time, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a Lehigh-era empirical study that tests machine-learning surrogates against the group's own DFO tools. These are quadratic interpolation and regression models (the CSV book and the geometry papers, refs [17–19], p. 8) and the Full-Low Evaluation (FLE) method of Berahas, Sohab & Vicente (OMS 2023, ref. [9], pp. 17–19, 29–31). The paper is the negative-result counterpart of Method 1: a new heuristic component is plugged into an existing solver and does not help. **Text read**: arXiv v1 dated 22 Nov 2023, not the published 2026 version. Pp. 1–31 were read in full, including Appendix A (problem tables, pp. 29–30) and Appendix B (FLE review, pp. 29–31).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Neural networks are popular surrogates in engineering, but the authors are "not aware of any papers evaluating the accuracy of the approximations produced by neural networks or using neural networks within optimization algorithms to approximate the objective function being minimized" (p. 2). The question is framed as an assessment, not as a method proposal: can NNs serve as surrogates for approximation (value, gradient, Hessian) and inside DFO (p. 2)? The comparison class is the DFO community's own tools, quadratic interpolation/regression and RBF models (p. 2). The scope is smooth unconstrained f. Derivatives are computed only to score the approximations (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when a neighbouring field's popular tool is being adopted in yours, write the paper as an accuracy audit against your field's incumbent tools, on your field's test collections (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: The study has three stages (contributions 1–3, pp. 2–3).
  - *(i) Choose the activation*. Five NNs (ReLU, ELU, SiLU, Sigmoid, Tanh) are treated as "solvers" in performance profiles. The measure is the number of training iterations needed to reach a fraction (1−τ) of the best loss decrease (2.2) (pp. 5–6).
  - *(ii) Compare local approximation accuracy*. Four model types are compared (pp. 11–12, 15):
    - the natural quadratic basis (3.1);
    - basis (3.4), which applies the activation to the cross terms and is used for determined interpolation;
    - basis (3.5), which drops the cross terms and adds s(x_i), giving 3n+1 terms fitted by regression on the same (n+1)(n+2)/2 points;
    - Gaussian RBF (3.6).

    NNs are then trained on the same sample set. Bases that apply s to the squared terms were tried and dropped because they were not better (pp. 11–12).
  - *(iii) Plug surrogates into FLE*. FLE's FD-BFGS "Full-Eval" step normally uses a forward-difference gradient, which costs n extra evaluations per iteration (p. 18). FLE-S (Algorithm 2, pp. 19–20) makes three changes:
    - it uses the surrogate gradient instead, once the dataset holds ζ(n+1)(n+2)/2 points;
    - it adds one random point in B(x_k; 0.1) per iteration to improve the geometry of the sample;
    - it adds only the first line-search point to the dataset for interpolation models, to avoid aligned points, and all of them for NNs.

    Unchanged: the BFGS update with its curvature safeguard (4.2), the Armijo backtracking (4.3), the direct-search Low-Eval step and the switching rules (pp. 18, 30–31).
  - Transferable technique: when you plug a learned model into an existing solver, change only one quantity (here the gradient estimate g_k) and leave the solver's safeguards and switching rules alone, so that the comparison isolates the model (Algorithm 2, p. 20).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Problem class*: f is continuously differentiable and noiseless (p. 3). The paper describes the FE step as effective in the "smooth, non-noisy case" (p. 19).
  - *Sampling*: sample points are uniform in B(x0; 1) around the CUTEst starting point, then shifted and scaled into B(0; 1) (pp. 6, 11).
  - *NN setup*: two hidden layers of 4n neurons; Adam for 300 epochs; ReduceLROnPlateau with factor 0.8 and patience 15; He or Xavier initialization; mini-batch sizes set by dimension (p. 6). Inside FLE-S, the NN is trained for 5 epochs at its first call and 1 epoch afterwards, with learning rate 10⁻² chosen by grid search (pp. 20–21).
  - *No theory*: there is no convergence claim for FLE-S, and the paper contains no theorems (checked pp. 1–31).
  - Reader's observation: the learning-rate scheduler monitors the empirical risk on the testing dataset (p. 6), so the test set also steers training.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: None. There are no lemmas or theorems. The analytic content is limited to definitions: the interpolation/regression systems (3.2), the RBF saddle-point system (3.7) (pp. 10, 12), the relative error metrics (3.8)–(3.13) (pp. 13, 15) and the FD-BFGS step (pp. 17–18).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Test collections*: two CUTEst sets. The first has 38 problems with user-defined n ∈ {20, 40, 60}. The second has 53 problems (n from 2 to 50) with detected negative curvature, taken from Gratton–Royer–Vicente [33] (p. 5; Tables 1–2, pp. 29–30).
  - *Activation study*: performance profiles on the training and testing sets at τ ∈ {10⁻², 10⁻⁵} (Figs. 1–4, pp. 7–10). SiLU is best on testing data, ReLU is best on training data for the 38-problem set, and the sigmoidal activations are worst (p. 8). The training problem was solved five times per problem (p. 5); the text does not say how the five runs enter the profiles (pp. 5–8).
  - *Approximation study*: relative errors of value, gradient and Hessian at x0, shown as box plots clipped to [0, 5] and displayed on [0, 1] (pp. 13, 15). Fig. 5 uses n = 20 only for the 38-problem set (p. 14). Basis (3.5) is the most accurate on the 38-problem set; all bases are similar on the 53-problem set; RBF is weaker on curvature (pp. 13, 15). The check was repeated at points between the origin and x0 without changing the conclusions (p. 15).
  - *NN accuracy*: ReLU-type activations are best for values and gradients. Hessians are inaccurate except with SiLU, and sigmoidal gradients are close to zero (pp. 15–17).
  - *Optimization study*: FLE is compared with FLE-S using the natural basis, basis (3.5) with Sigmoid, RBF, and NNs with ReLU and with SiLU. Performance profiles count function evaluations to reach f(x0) − f(x) ≥ (1−τ)(f(x0) − f_L), with τ ∈ {10⁻², 10⁻⁵} (Figs. 9–10, pp. 21–23).
  - *Result*: no FLE-S variant beats FLE, even though FLE-S saves n evaluations per model-based iteration. The NN variant is more efficient on larger n at τ = 10⁻² but less robust, and it deteriorates at τ = 10⁻⁵ (p. 21). Further variants (surrogate in the line search, a surrogate-minimizing search step) also failed and were omitted (p. 23).
  - *Reporting*: the code is public on GitHub (p. 3). The abstract's "high training cost" (p. 1) is not quantified; no timings are reported (pp. 1–31, searched).
  - Transferable technique: give every surrogate candidate exactly the same sample set (same size (n+1)(n+2)/2, same ball, same shift and scale) before comparing accuracy (pp. 6, 12, 15).
  - Transferable technique: treat model variants (activations, bases) as solvers in performance profiles, with a convergence test defined relative to the best value any variant attains (2.2) (pp. 5–6).
  - Transferable technique: when a component is supposed to save evaluations, measure the whole solver in function evaluations against the unmodified solver. The saving and the loss of accuracy then net out in one number (p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper sets itself against engineering practice with NN surrogates (refs [50, 55, 56, 68], p. 2) and against learning-to-optimize work in which RNNs predict steps (p. 2). It builds on the DFO model literature (Conn–Toint, Powell's UOBYQA and NEWUOA, the CSV book, ORBIT; pp. 8, 10, 30). It also builds on recent findings that FD-BFGS is strong for smooth noiseless DFO (Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal; Berahas–Cao–Choromanski–Scheinberg; Shi–Xuan–Oztoprak–Nocedal; p. 17). The baseline, FLE, is the authors' own solver, which they call "state-of-the-art" (pp. 3, 17). Advice on activations is taken from the Goodfellow et al. textbook (pp. 3–4).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The title and the headings of §3 and §4 state the verdict: "The limitation of neural nets" (pp. 1, 8, 17). The contributions are numbered, point to their sections and are phrased as "The evidence provided shows" (pp. 2–3). An informal one-sentence summary of the negative result follows contribution 3 (the "magical/search step" sentence, p. 3). A positive by-product stays visible in a negative paper: cross terms can be dropped (pp. 1, 15). Hedging is mild ("seems to waive", p. 15). The conclusion lists the omitted negative experiments (p. 23). No future work is stated (pp. 21–23).
  - Transferable technique: in a negative-result paper, put one positive by-product in the abstract so that the reader also gets something to use (p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are empirical: an activation recommendation (SiLU), an approximation audit, an observation about bases (cross terms can be dropped once an activation is added), and a negative result for NN surrogates inside FLE (pp. 2–3, 21–23). Stated limits: sampling in the unit ball makes the (3.1)/(3.4) approximations inaccurate, and the error shrinks with a smaller ball (pp. 13, 15). The paper also argues, without testing it, that a more complex NN would need more training points and "would still not be competitive" (p. 21). Limits that are visible but not stated: one NN architecture is used for all problems (p. 6); only smooth noiseless problems are tested (p. 3), which is exactly where FD-BFGS is known to be strong (p. 17); n ≤ 60; there are no timings.
  - Reader's observation: basis (3.5) has no cross terms, so its model Hessian is diagonal. The paper does not relate the basis's good accuracy to the (partial) separability of the test functions, although it lists partial separability as a feature of the first set (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (negative evidence). The solver skeleton is kept: FLE's FD-BFGS full-eval step plus a direct-search low-eval step with sufficient decrease (pp. 30–31). A learned component is inserted. Step 6 of Method 1, the comparison with the unwrapped version, is carried out, and the verdict is negative across the collection (pp. 21, 23). **Transferable techniques**:

### S101 · Stochastic set-valued optimization and its application to robust learning (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2603.17691)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. Giovannelli (Cincinnati), J. Tan (Lehigh) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the newest link in the Lehigh line on multiobjective methods for ML. It reuses the stochastic multi-gradient (SMG) method and its Pareto-front version PF-SMG (Liu & Vicente, ref. [31]; card S016 in c05 covers the companion paper [32]). It also takes the knee idea of the Pareto-sensitivity report ([17]) and the SSG method of S107 ([18]). It brings set-valued optimization (Kuroiwa, Jahn, Eichfelder) into ML. **Text read**: arXiv v1 dated 19 Mar 2026, pp. 1–27 in full, including Appendices A (proof, pp. 23–24), B (SMG, p. 25), C (SSG, pp. 25–26) and D (PF-SMG, p. 27).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Two gaps are combined. In robust ML, the bilevel, min–max and regularization approaches "fall short" because they reduce variability "to a single measure: the empirical risk" (p. 3). In set-valued optimization (SVO), only deterministic methods exist (a Newton method, a polyhedral method, Jahn's enumerative comparisons, the descent method of Bouza et al.), and there are "no stochastic formulations" (p. 3). The problem is ⪯_K-min S(x) under the set approach with Kuroiwa's set-less relation, specialized to hyperbox images (pp. 2, 5, 8). The set approach is chosen because "it aligns well with the robust learning applications" (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when importing a formalism into a new field, pick the variant that fits the target application (here the set approach rather than the vector approach), and say so at the start (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The design is a chain of reductions.
  1. SVO with hyperbox images is equivalent to vector optimization with 2k objectives (F_ub, F_lb) (Theorem 3.1, p. 9).
  2. So IVO (k = 1) is bi-objective in (f_lb, f_ub), and RVO (k = 2) has four objectives (Corollaries 3.1–3.2, pp. 9–10).
  3. The objectives are chosen as f_lb = subquantile and f_ub = superquantile of the loss ψ(x, ξ) (4.9). For RVO there is one such pair for accuracy and one for fairness (4.13) (pp. 11–12).

  The tail levels interpolate between known models. (p, p̄) = (1, 0) gives f_lb = f_ub = E[ψ], which is ERM and is solved by SGD (p. 11). (p, p̄) = (0, 1) gives min and max over a compact support. That is a deterministic min–max-type problem, solved with multi-subgradient or multi-gradient sampling methods (pp. 11–12). The superquantile uses the Rockafellar–Uryasev form (4.7), and the subquantile uses a new mirror form (4.8) (p. 11).

  The solver is PF-SMG (Appendix D, p. 27), with each objective smoothed as in SSG (Appendix C, pp. 25–26). The auxiliary variable η is not optimized. It is set to the empirical p-quantile by sorting (p. 15). A single model is then chosen as the knee: the nondominated point with the smallest interior angle to its two neighbours (p. 15). For RVO, x_RVO is built in three steps (p. 15):
  - project the 4-D front onto the accuracy plane and the fairness plane, and find a knee in each;
  - derive weights for each knee from the extreme points;
  - add the two knee solutions with those weights.
  - Transferable technique: design a parametric family whose corners recover the standard models (ERM at one corner, the worst case at the other). The new model is then an interpolation you can explain, not a replacement (pp. 11–12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Vectorization needs S(x)+K and S(x)−K to be closed and convex (Theorem 2.1, p. 8), which hyperboxes satisfy. ψ is continuously differentiable, and ξ has a distribution independent of x (p. 11). The tail-mean characterizations (4.5)–(4.6) need a continuous distribution; (4.7)–(4.8) hold in general (pp. 10–11). No convergence assumptions for the algorithm are stated because no convergence result is given (checked pp. 1–27). Remark 5.1 motivates the model with a sketched one-dimensional example. The ERM minimizer yields training losses that are "either very low (risk of overfitting) or very high (risk of underfitting)". The min–max solutions sit at the ends of the front. An intermediate point is "expected to perform more effectively on validation data" (p. 14). The paper asserts, but does not show, that the set-valued empirical risk "considers both the expected value and variance of the loss" (p. 13).
  - Reader's observation: Appendix C describes the quantile objectives as having the form ϕ(E[ψ]) with ϕ = max{0, ·} (p. 25; S107 says the same on its p. 2). The empirical objectives (4.11)–(4.12), however, put the max inside the average, which is the E[ϕ(ψ)] form (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There is one new proof (Appendix A). Lemma A.1 states that Q_{−Z}(1−u) = −Q_Z(u) at every continuity point u of Q_Z. It is proved from F_{−Z}(t) = 1 − F_Z((−t)⁻) and a two-sided inequality (pp. 23–24). Theorem A.2 then argues as follows. Q_Z has at most countably many discontinuities, so the identity holds almost everywhere, and the change of variables v = 1−u gives S_p[Z] = −S̄_{1−p}[−Z]. Applying Rockafellar–Uryasev to −Z at level 1−p and negating gives (4.8) (p. 24). Everything else is imported: the vectorization theorem from Jahn [22] (p. 8), and the hyperbox comparison from [21]/[11], together with Theorem 3.1, whose proof "follows directly" according to [11] (p. 9). The conclusion presents the subquantile result as filling "a theoretical gap in the analysis of lower-tail risk measures" (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: derive the lower-tail ("mirror") version of a classical characterization by applying the classical result to −Z together with a quantile-reflection lemma, rather than proving it from scratch (pp. 23–24).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Setup*: binary classification with logistic regression on Adult (IVO and RVO) and German Credit (IVO) (pp. 16–18). Common settings are 1500 iterations, an initial step size of 1.3 with the same discount rate for all methods, and N_rep = 30 test replications (p. 16).
  - *Shift and metrics*: distribution shift is created by sub-sampling the positive or negative class of the single initial test set, which changes the positive fraction (p. 15). The robustness metric is the sample variance of the test loss across replications (p. 16). RVO uses a covariance proxy for disparate impact and a new FRPA metric: the variance of the fairness loss divided by mean accuracy (p. 18).
  - *Baselines*: ERM trained by SG, a single SMG solution (p. 15) and, for RVO, the knee of a plain accuracy–fairness bi-objective problem, the "BI knee" (p. 18).
  - *Results*: x_IVO has uniformly smaller replication variability than x_ERM on Adult (p. 16) and smaller variability on German Credit (pp. 17–18). The RVO knee has the lowest FRPA at all shift levels, and the BI knee beats ERM (p. 18).
  - *Not reported in the text* (searched pp. 1–27): the tail levels p and p̄; smoothing parameters and batch sizes; PF-SMG settings; accuracy values; repeated train/test splits; code. The abstract's "competitive accuracy" rests on figures that cannot be read in the text file.
  - Reader's observation: for IVO, each model's variance is computed with its own loss: ℓ for ERM, and the weighted sub/superquantile loss for x_IVO, with λ taken from the knee (p. 16). The comparison therefore does not use a common evaluation loss.
  - Reader's observation: the text says the SMG point illustrates that "extreme behavior may arise from a fixed scalarization" (p. 16), whereas Appendix B describes SMG as re-solving the weights λ_k at every iteration (p. 25).
  - Transferable technique: to test robustness to label shift without new data, build many test sets by class-rebalanced sub-sampling of one held-out set, and report the variance of the metric across them (pp. 15–16).
- **D6 Positioning**: The SVO lineage is set out carefully (p. 3):
  - Kuroiwa's set relations;
  - the book by Khan, Tammer and Zălinescu;
  - the survey by Hamel et al.;
  - Jahn's vectorization, which "plays the same role that scalarization plays for VVO";
  - Eichfelder–Rocktäschel's error bounds;
  - Eichfelder–Gerlach's reducible classes.

  The novelty claim rests on the book and the survey (p. 3). Robust ML is sorted into adversarial robustness (min–max or bilevel), robustness to imbalance (bilevel weights) and robustness to overfitting (regularization, cross-validation, DRO) (pp. 2–3). It is criticized as a class, not paper by paper (p. 3). The group's own prior work is reused rather than discussed: SMG [31], fairness [32], Pareto sensitivity [17], SSG [18]. There is one citation slip: "the book by Khan et al. [26]" on p. 3 points to a big-data conference paper, while the SVO book is [25] (p. 21).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A paragraph headed "Our contributions" (pp. 3–4) is followed by a roadmap (p. 4). Strong claims are made in the first person: "We claim that any ML application problem involving two objectives" can be formulated as a stochastic RVO problem (p. 14). Geometric intuition carries the motivation (Figs. 3–4 and Remark 5.1, pp. 13–14). Hedging is concentrated in the numerics ("typically exhibit", "expected to"; pp. 16, 19). The conclusion opens with a modelling lesson: the value of the framework depends on the choice of lower and upper objectives (see the second quote, p. 19). It closes with three concrete future directions (pp. 19–20).
  - Transferable technique: open the conclusion with the modelling lesson (what the framework's value depends on) before the list of results (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a modelling framework (stochastic HVO with sub- and superquantile objectives), one theoretical result (the subquantile characterization) and numerics on two datasets (pp. 3–4, 19). Stated limits and future work (pp. 19–20):
  - more criteria (precision, recall, other fairness measures), leading to higher-dimensional hyperboxes;
  - other knee rules that target stability;
  - data-driven tuning of the tail levels, which "act as hyperparameters".

  Limits that are visible but not stated: there is no convergence theory for the smoothed PF-SMG pipeline; η is replaced by a sorted empirical quantile (p. 15); x_RVO is a weighted sum of two parameter vectors (p. 15) and is not shown to be Pareto optimal (reader's observation); there is one model class (logistic regression) and there are two datasets.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ variant. To reach set-valued objectives, the solvers (SMG, PF-SMG) are left untouched and the problem is reformulated into their native class (MOO) by vectorization (Theorem 3.1, p. 9; Appendices B–D, pp. 25–27). What changes is the objective vector, not the acceptance test.
- Heuristic 4 / Workflow E ✅ evidence. PF-SMG keeps a nondominated list (p. 27), the text warns against a fixed scalarization (p. 16), and the knee is chosen a posteriori (p. 15).
- Heuristic 8 ⚠ variant. The knee is an angle-based rule (p. 15), not the Pareto-sensitivity formalization cited as [17] on the same page. x_RVO is an a-posteriori weighted aggregation of two knees (p. 15).
- Taste #6 ✅ evidence. The work is anchored in fairness and credit scoring (Adult, German Credit; pp. 16–18), continuing the line of card S016.
- Method 5 ⚠. There are two datasets, no profiles and no code link, and hyperparameters are missing (pp. 16–18). Taste #5's statement that the Lehigh papers ship GitHub code does not hold here (searched pp. 1–27).
- Methods 2 and 3 are not exercised: the paper has no rates and no probabilistic arguments.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Reduce a set-valued objective with box-shaped images to a finite multiobjective problem through its lower and upper corner vectors (Proposition 3.1 and Theorem 3.1, p. 9), then reuse any MOO solver.

- Obtain a mirror characterization through −Z and a quantile-reflection lemma (pp. 23–24).
