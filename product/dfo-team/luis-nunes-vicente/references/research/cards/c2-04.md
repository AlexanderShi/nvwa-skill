### S092 · Why is soccer so popular: Understanding underdog achievement and randomness in team ball sports (Journal of Sports Analytics 2025, DOI 10.1177/22150218251324281)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. N. Alleck, T. Giovannelli, R. Mitchell, O. Remen (all at Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period. It is the only paper of the three with no optimization model, algorithm or theorem (checked pp. 1–26). It acknowledges the same AFOSR award FA9550-23-1-0217 (p. 19) as S096 (p. 24) and S102 (p. 19), and it shares a coauthor (Giovannelli) with S096. **Text read**: arXiv v1 (arXiv:2404.06626), pp. 1–26 in full, including Appendices A–B (Tables 7–10, pp. 20–26). Figures appear only as captions in the text file (pp. 8, 10–12, 17–19).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is a popular claim. A non-scholarly book [14] says soccer is the most random sport and that its randomness is what makes it popular, and [2] confirmed soccer's randomness against four North American leagues (pp. 1–2). [12] quantified randomness for MLB, NBA, NFL and NHL but left soccer out, and [16] studied randomness within English Premier League matches (p. 2). The gap named is breadth and explanation: earlier studies covered "a limited range of team ball sports" and had not systematically identified the randomness factors behind underdog wins (p. 2). The research question: how is the likelihood that a weaker team beats a stronger one influenced by randomness factors inherent to each sport (p. 2)? The scope is men's major international competitions in 12 sports (Table 1, p. 3). American football, baseball and tennis are excluded for lack of international competitions or because teams are small (p. 2). The title asks why soccer is popular, but popularity is never measured. It enters only through the cited literature (pp. 1–2; checked pp. 1–26).
  - Transferable technique: when entering a new field, take a widely repeated but loosely supported claim, find which cases the scholarly literature actually covers (here, which sports), and make the uncovered cases your scope (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: A four-step pipeline.
  1. Match scores are scraped from Wikipedia into a dataset D_e for each edition (2.1) (pp. 3–4).
  2. Each edition gets a ranking by lexicographic sorting on matches played, wins, draws, losses (ascending) and total score. Matches played comes first because teams that reach the final stages play the most matches (p. 5). Editions are combined into a weighted ranking with decay, wr(i) = (N − c(i,R)) + λ·wr_prev(i) with λ ∈ [0,1] (2.4) (p. 5).
  3. A team is "weak" in a match when its rank in the weighted ranking is worse by more than a sport-specific threshold τ (3.1) (p. 6). The underdog achievement score UAS_e is the fraction of matches involving a weak team in which the weak team won or drew (3.2) (p. 6). It is averaged over editions (3.3), and a pooled version (3.4) is also given (p. 7). τ is set to the median rank difference of each sport (p. 7, Table 3 p. 9).
  4. A "randomness model" of 14 factors in three groups (physical environment, player, team) gives each factor a formula or a categorical coding (Table 5 and pp. 12–15). The factors are min–max normalized (p. 12) and related to UAS by PCA and Pearson correlations (pp. 15–18).

  A first definition of weakness (top p% vs bottom p%) was tried and dropped. Bottom teams "rarely, if ever" beat top ones, and at p = 50 the wins that do occur are noise from mid-table teams (p. 6). As printed in this preprint, (3.1) reads c(i,W) ≤ c(j,W) − τ. With position 1 at the top of the list (p. 5), that inequality would mark i as the *stronger* team, so its direction appears to be a typo (p. 6). The published version was not checked.
  - Transferable technique: define "weak" using only information available before the event being scored (here, the ranking up to the previous edition), so the label cannot leak the outcome (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: set a per-group threshold at the median of that group's own distribution, so that groups with different sizes and spreads are compared on the same footing (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Team strength comes only from the competitions' own results. Elo ratings and betting odds are listed as alternatives used in the literature (p. 6). The weighting assumes that past editions matter with geometric decay λ, and λ = 1 (no decay) is used for all statistical tests "For simplicity" (p. 9). Draws count as underdog achievement (3.2) (p. 6). The authors state, as a belief, that every factor increases randomness (pp. 9, 12). Some factors are coded by judgement: ball geometry in three classes, and ball bounciness in categories from 0 (ice hockey) to 11 (basketball) (pp. 13–14). Cricket has no goal, so its goal-based ratios are set to the average of the other sports (pp. 14–15). The PCA and the correlations have one row per sport, i.e. 12 observations for 14 factors plus UAS (p. 12). Only men's events are used, because more data is available for them (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & technique**: No theorems. The statistical devices are:
  - box plots of rank differences per sport (Figs. 1–2, pp. 7–8);
  - three UAS variants (3.2)–(3.4) and three λ values (Fig. 3, Table 3, pp. 7–9);
  - 95% confidence intervals (Fig. 4, p. 11);
  - a Laney p′ control chart, included to show that the result does not depend on the metric (pp. 7, 9);
  - a Kruskal–Wallis test (p = 2.47·10⁻¹⁰) followed by Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction, with only the significant pairs reported (p. 9, Table 4 p. 11);
  - PCA, where the first two components explain 56% of the variance (p. 16; Fig. 7, p. 18) and the loading vectors are doubled in length for visibility (p. 16);
  - a Pearson correlation heatmap (p. 18).

  Four numbered observations (Observation 1–4) read the PCA biplot sport by sport (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: report the nonparametric omnibus test first, then only the post-hoc pairs that survive a multiple-comparison correction, in one table (p. 9, Table 4 p. 11).
- **D5 Data & results**: The editions used for each sport are listed in Table 7 (p. 22). For soccer these are the FIFA World Cups of 1930–2014 (p. 22). The text does not say why 2018 and 2022 are absent. The median rank difference ranges from 2.5 (water polo) to 7 (soccer) (p. 7). UAS at λ = 1 is highest for water polo (0.37), soccer (0.36), field hockey (0.31) and ice hockey (0.30), and lowest for roller hockey (0.05), rugby (0.07) and lacrosse (0.08) (Table 3, p. 9). The same four sports stay on top and the same three at the bottom for every λ (p. 7, Table 3 p. 9) and in the p′-chart (p. 9). Thirteen pairs differ significantly, and soccer differs from cricket, lacrosse, roller hockey and rugby (Table 4, p. 11). UAS correlates most positively with goal size per goal defender (GS/NPG). It correlates most negatively with NRAM/NRPM, player inexperience (PI) and ball geometry (BG) (p. 18). The authors read the negative correlations as factors with a weaker effect on randomness. They conclude that the factors with the most impact are GS/NPG, NP/FS, PBD, PBH and BB (p. 18). That reading is not tested (p. 18). The code is on GitHub (footnote, p. 3), and the match-score dataset is a Lehigh technical report [1] (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: show that a ranking is not an artefact of one metric by reporting it under several definitions (per-edition mean, pooled ratio, control chart) and for several values of the nuisance parameter λ (pp. 7–9).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper sets itself apart from the dominant sports-medicine literature [15] (p. 1). It agrees with [2, 14] that soccer is among the most random sports (p. 2) and with [12] on ice hockey (p. 2). It contrasts its static factors with the dynamic, in-match randomness variables of [16] and [10], and it leaves out motivation factors such as match location and current score (p. 9). Two of its motivating references are non-scholarly books ([13], [14], pp. 1–2). No optimization literature is cited (references, pp. 19–20).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: There are four contribution bullets: data, score, model, analysis (pp. 2–3). An optimization-style notation table (sets, ordered lists, sorting functions; Table 2, p. 4) formalizes a sports dataset. The abstract's claim is strong: the randomness model "can explain" underdog achievement (p. 1). That claim rests on a correlation analysis over 12 sports (pp. 15–18). Hedges appear in the interpretation ("perhaps due to", p. 16; "may appear surprising", p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a dataset, a score, a factor model and a descriptive statistical analysis (pp. 2–3, 18–19). There is no limitations section. Stated future work: replicate the study on professional-league and collegiate competitions, and extend it to team sports without a ball (p. 19). Several limits are visible but not stated as such:
  - the correlation analysis rests on 12 observations;
  - the factor signs are assumed and then partly reinterpreted (pp. 12, 18);
  - popularity is not measured;
  - (3.1) as printed runs in the wrong direction (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 8 ✅ evidence, outside optimization. The informal notions "underdog" and "randomness" become a computable score (3.1)–(3.4) and a 14-factor table (pp. 5–7, 12–15). Unlike S096, the restrictions of the formalization are not stated up front.
- Method 5 ✅ partial evidence (Taste mark 5, a usable artefact): code on GitHub (p. 3) and a dataset technical report (p. 19). Profiles, baselines and solvers do not apply.
- Methods 1–4: not applicable. There is no algorithm, acceptance test or complexity result (checked pp. 1–26). The paper widens SKILL.md's *DFO core vs the ML pivot* inner tension: here the pivot goes all the way into empirical sports analytics.
- New-pattern candidate: **negative example for the rejected alternative** (already in the pool from c01). The top/bottom-p% definition of weakness is reported together with the reason it fails (p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: **robustness over definitional choices**. The result is reported under three metrics and three λ values (pp. 7–9). See also S096 (p. 16) and S102 (p. 18).
- New-pattern candidate: **formalize an informal notion into a computable score** (with S096; SKILL.md has it only as Heuristic 8). Here the notions are "weak team" and "underdog achievement", formalized in (3.1)–(3.4) (pp. 6–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Leakage-free labels: define weakness from the ranking up to the previous edition only (p. 6).
- An exponentially decayed aggregate ranking wr(i) = (N − c(i,R)) + λ·wr_prev(i), with results reported at λ = 0, 0.5 and 1 (p. 5, Table 3 p. 9).
- A per-group median threshold for when a team counts as ranked significantly lower (pp. 6–7).
- Kruskal–Wallis followed by Dunn–Bonferroni, with only the significant pairs tabulated (p. 9, Table 4 p. 11).
- Release the code and the dataset with the first preprint (pp. 3, 19).

**Quotes**:
- "Note that in (3.2), weak teams are identified using a weighted team ranking that incorporates all past editions except the current one to avoid biasing the results." (p. 6)
- "The negative correlations observed with UAS may appear surprising because they suggest that higher values of the factors decrease the underdog achievement, while we would expect that higher values of the factors increase randomness in match outcomes." (p. 18)

### S096 · Pareto sensitivity, most-changing sub-fronts, and knee solutions (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2501.16993)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. Giovannelli (U. Cincinnati), M. M. Raimundo (UNICAMP) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the multiobjective line after DMS (2011) and the stochastic multi-gradient and fairness work (2021–2022). It reuses the sensitivity machinery of the group's bilevel papers ([33–36], pp. 4, 20). It cites Liu & Vicente's Sharpe predictor [48] as the bi-objective-only alternative (p. 2). SKILL.md already uses it as the case for Heuristic 8 and as a limitation of Method 4. **Text read**: arXiv v3 (23 March 2026), pp. 1–29 in full. There is no appendix. Figures appear only as captions in the text file (pp. 7, 14–15, 17–18, 22–23).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: A full Pareto front is expensive and redundant to compute, especially with many objectives, and decision makers usually want a few representative solutions (p. 2). Knee solutions are a popular choice, but they have only a verbal definition: points where a small improvement in any objective causes a large deterioration in at least one other (pp. 1–2). There is no accepted quantitative definition, and Deb & Gupta observe that most methods return intermediate points rather than knees in the verbal sense (p. 3). Each of the three existing families fails in a named way:
  - angle-based methods work only for two objectives and need a precomputed set of nondominated points and a user threshold (p. 3);
  - utility-based methods need a predetermined set of Pareto points and many scalarized solves (p. 3);
  - normal-boundary-intersection (NBI) methods tend to return points in the intermediate region (p. 4).

  The approach is also placed against a priori, a posteriori and interactive decision methods: it needs no explicit preferences, handles any number of objectives, does not approximate the efficient set and involves no interaction (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: tabulate the competing families by the specific extra input each one needs (a number of objectives, a precomputed front, a user threshold), then design against all of them at once (pp. 2–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Pareto solutions are parametrized by weighted-sum weights λ in the simplex Λ, giving x(λ) (p. 4). For q = 2, a knee in the verbal sense is where |df₂/df₁| and |df₁/df₂| have the same size (Deb & Gupta §6). It is therefore a minimizer of max{|df₂/df₁|, |df₁/df₂|}, but computing these slopes directly would require the whole front (pp. 7–8). The replacement uses the sensitivities ∇_λ f_i(x(λ)).
  - **Maximal-change function.** MCF(λ) = max_{i≠j} ‖∇_λ f_i(x(λ))‖ / ‖∇_λ f_j(x(λ))‖ (3.1). A knee is x(λ*), where λ* minimizes MCF over Λ (3.2) (Definition 4, p. 8).
  - **Sensitivity matrix.** It comes from differentiating the first-order conditions: ∇_λF = −Gᵀ(Σλ_i∇²f_i)⁻¹G with G = (∇f_1, …, ∇f_q) ((5.2)–(5.3), p. 12). With constraints, it comes from the KKT system K(w(λ)) = 0, with ∇w = −∇_λK ∇_wK⁻¹ and an extraction matrix L ((6.4)–(6.7), pp. 19–20), following the group's bilevel paper [35].
  - **Outer solve.** Problem (3.2) has dimension q, is nonsmooth and nonconvex, and its subgradients would involve third-order derivatives. It is therefore solved by DFO, using Nelder–Mead or DIRECT (p. 8). Each MCF evaluation solves the weighted-sum problem (Step A), forms ∇_λF (Step B) and takes ratios of column norms (Step C) (Algorithm 1, p. 9). Division by zero is avoided with max{‖·‖, eps} (p. 8).
  - **Most-changing sub-fronts.** These use the ellipsoidal neighbourhood E_α(λ_c) = {λ : ‖∇_λF(x(λ_c))^†(λ − λ_c)‖ ≤ α} (4.1), whose major axis is aligned with the direction of maximum change (p. 10). Two motivations are given. One is a one-dimensional picture: intervals that widen with the slope. The other is a steepest-ascent neighbourhood E_β (4.2), which implies (4.1) with α = κ/β when the condition number κ is moderate (p. 10).
  - **Quality metric.** MCM (4.3) is the product over the objectives of the sub-front's range divided by the full range. It lies in [0,1] and is unaffected by the objectives' scales. It is the metric the literature calls the overall Pareto spread, under a new name (pp. 10–11).
  - **Neighbourhood size.** α is set by an adaptive rule over a fine discretization Λ_m of the simplex (p. 11).

  What is kept: the weighted-sum scalarization and off-the-shelf solvers (advantage 5, p. 5). What is added: an outer optimization over λ and the sensitivity layer.
  - Transferable technique: when a definition needs derivatives of one output with respect to another along a solution set, differentiate both with respect to the parameter that generates the set (here λ) via the implicit function theorem, and take ratios (pp. 8, 12).
  - Transferable technique: when the derived outer problem is low-dimensional and nonsmooth, solve it with a DFO method, and use derivatives only inside each function evaluation (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Each f_i is twice continuously differentiable (p. 1). Assumption 5.1 requires that for every λ a stationary point of the weighted sum exists at which Σλ_i∇²f_i is nonsingular. Strong convexity is sufficient (p. 12). Assumption 6.1 requires C² constraints and a KKT point satisfying LICQ, strict complementarity and SOSC. Via Fiacco, this gives unique multipliers and a C¹ map w(λ) (p. 20). The authors note that the assumption subsumes the Kuhn–Tucker constraint qualification used in MOO textbooks (p. 21). The restrictions the authors admit are:
  - only scalarized methods are covered, and first- and second-order derivatives are needed (pp. 1, 4);
  - weighted sums need convex objectives to cover the whole front (p. 24);
  - stochastic gradients and nonconvex ML losses fall outside the assumptions (Remark 3.1, p. 9);
  - each MCF evaluation solves a weighted-sum problem, which may be expensive, and NBI can then be preferable (p. 9).

  Example 5.1 shows that λ_c is not unique for a given x_c when n < q − 1, so the sub-front depends on which λ_c is used (p. 13). All the unconstrained test problems are strictly convex (p. 13). DAS1 and DO2DK have convex fronts although some of their objectives are nonconvex (p. 21).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There is no theorem, lemma or convergence statement. "Theorem" appears only in citations to Rudin, Nesterov, Miettinen, Nocedal–Wright and Fiacco (pp. 12, 19–20). The analysis consists of three pieces:
  1. the implicit-function derivation of ∇_λF, unconstrained (pp. 11–12) and constrained (pp. 19–20), with the remark that the unconstrained matrix is symmetric and singular because (5.1) makes the gradients linearly dependent (p. 12);
  2. the κ/β link between the two neighbourhoods (p. 10);
  3. Example 5.1 (p. 13).

  The neighbourhoods are compared "numerically rather than analytically", because a theoretical study is out of scope (p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: use a one-line example (n = 1, q = 3, three shifted quadratics) to show a non-uniqueness that changes the method's output (Example 5.1, p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: The code is Python 3.12.7, run on a laptop (p. 6).
  - **Unconstrained setup.** The problems are ZLT1, GRV1, VFM1, ZLT1_q and GRV2 (Table 1, p. 13), with the random coefficients and matrices of GRV1 printed (p. 13). The outer solvers are SciPy's Nelder–Mead and DIRECT with default parameters and projection onto Λ, and the inner solver is BFGS (p. 14). The initial λ₀ is chosen "arbitrarily" and listed (p. 14). The plots show the weight, objective and decision spaces with the iterates, plus MCF and MCM histories (Figs. 2–6, pp. 14–15).
  - **Unconstrained results.** NM and DIRECT reach the same MCF minimum on all unconstrained problems (p. 16). The knees lie in the intermediate region except for VFM1, where the knee is on the upper boundary. The authors read it as an edge knee: without a bulge in the front there is no interior knee in the verbal sense (p. 16). α = 0.1 is fixed, except for GRV2, which needs the adaptive rule with γ = 0.4 because a fixed α gives empty or whole-simplex neighbourhoods (p. 16).
  - **Neighbourhood comparison.** Sizes are matched by the fraction of Λ_m each neighbourhood covers. E_α has the highest MCM on all four problems, e.g. ZLT1: E_α 0.1529, ball 0.0895, E_β 0.0837 (Table 2, p. 19). E_β beats the ball "nearly all the times" (p. 17).
  - **Constrained runs.** The problems are DAS1, DO2DK (n = 30, r ∈ {0.5, 1}) and VFM1constr (Table 3, p. 21). The inner solver is SLSQP, and the adaptive α is used throughout (p. 22). Knees are interior for DAS1 and for DO2DK with r = 1, and on the boundary for r = 0.5 and for VFM1constr (p. 22).
  - **Local vs global minima.** On VFM1constr, DIRECT and NM disagree: MCF is nonconvex and NM stops at a local minimizer, so knees "can have a local or global nature" (p. 23). On the same problem the MCF minimizer is not an MCM minimizer, because an ill-conditioned Jacobian (two eigenvalues near zero) inflates the ellipsoid (pp. 23–24).
  - **What is missing.** There is no numerical comparison with angle-, utility- or NBI-based knee methods (checked pp. 13–24). NBI appears only as a stated expectation that it would return an interior point whether or not the front has a bulge (pp. 16, 22). No timings are reported, and the text gives no repository link (checked).
  - Transferable technique: when comparing neighbourhood (or region) shapes, match their sizes by the fraction of a fixed discretization they contain, and report that fraction next to the metric (p. 17, Table 2 p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: run a local solver (NM) and a global solver (DIRECT) on the same outer problem. Agreement supports the reported optimum; disagreement exposes nonconvexity (pp. 16, 23).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper claims the name. Sensitivity analysis in MOO goes back to the late 1980s [57, 67] with set-valued perturbation maps, but "None of these papers use the term Pareto sensitivity" (p. 4). It links to hypergradient computation in bilevel and multilevel optimization, including the group's own [33–36] (p. 4). Deb & Gupta [22] is used twice: as the source of the equal-slopes characterization (p. 8), and for the edge-knee definition that the paper improves on, which covers only two objectives and needs a user parameter (p. 5). The main contrasting method is NBI, from Das [18] and Das–Dennis [19] (pp. 4, 16, 22); Dennis was Vicente's PhD advisor. DFO is cited through the group's own book [16] and survey chapter [17], and through Audet–Hare and Larson–Menickelly–Wild (p. 8). The overall Pareto spread is renamed MCM because, the authors say, the term spread is not relevant to their context (pp. 10–11).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Six advantages are listed up front (p. 5). The abstract states the restriction in plain words (Quote 1, p. 1). The paper coins four names: Pareto sensitivity, snee, MCF and MCM (pp. 4–5, 8, 11). One claim is strong: the decision maker "is guaranteed that trading among objectives is optimally balanced" (p. 4). The content is carried by definitions, derivations, remarks and plots rather than theorems (pp. 7–24). Footnotes explain NM and DIRECT for readers outside DFO (pp. 8–9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is conceptual and computational: a quantitative definition of a knee, a procedure to compute it, and a construction of sub-fronts (pp. 4–5, 24). The stated limits are scalarization, the need for derivatives, the convexity that weighted sums need, stochastic gradients being out of scope, the cost of the inner solves, and neighbourhoods that are not analysed (pp. 1, 9, 24). Future work (p. 24):
  - an ε-constraint version, which avoids the convexity requirement but needs global solutions of constrained problems;
  - "techniques that do not rely on scalarized methods".

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 8 ✅ evidence. The verbal definition is formalized as an optimization problem (pp. 3–4, 7–8; Definition 4, p. 8), and the restrictions are stated in the abstract (p. 1). This is the case SKILL.md cites.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. This confirms the note under Method 4's Limitations: no acceptance test is generalized, a weighted-sum scalarization is kept, and derivatives are required (pp. 1, 4, 24). It does not contradict warning sign 2 (*Aggregating objectives before the front is known*): λ is swept as a parameter and chosen by optimization, not fixed by preference (pp. 4–5).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. DFO is not wrapped around a heuristic here. It is the outer solver of a derived q-dimensional problem (pp. 8–9).
- Method 5 ⚠ partial. The experiments use synthetic problems, two outer solvers and a neighbourhood ablation. There is no competing knee method, no problem collection and no profiles (pp. 13–24). SKILL.md's Taste mark 5 lists a "snee" GitHub repository, but the v3 text links none. It says only that all code was implemented in Python 3.12.7 (p. 6). The repository was not checked here.
- Inner tension *Direct-search skeleton vs borrowing derivatives and models* ✅ evidence (pp. 1, 4, 12, 20).
- New-pattern candidate: **negative example for the rejected alternative** (in the pool from c01). Remark 5.1 rejects minimizing MCM instead of MCF, and the VFM1constr failure is shown later (pp. 16, 23–24).
- New-pattern candidate: **DFO as the outer solver of a low-dimensional derived problem** (pp. 8–9, 14).
- New-pattern candidate: **formalize an informal notion into a computable score** (with S092) (pp. 7–8).
- New-pattern candidate: **robustness over definitional choices**. Three neighbourhood shapes with matched sizes, and two outer solvers (pp. 16–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a scalarized MOO solution map: ∇_λF(x(λ)) = −Gᵀ(Σλ_i∇²f_i)⁻¹G, or −∇_λK ∇_wK⁻¹L·G with constraints. Its columns give each objective's sensitivity to the weights (pp. 12, 20).
- Turn equal trade-off slopes into min over λ ∈ Λ of max_{i≠j} ‖∇_λf_i‖/‖∇_λf_j‖, with the denominator floored at machine eps (p. 8).
- To pick out the direction of maximum change, use a pseudo-inverse-Jacobian ellipsoid {λ : ‖J^†(λ − λ_c)‖ ≤ α} instead of a ball (pp. 10, 19).
- A scale-free coverage metric: the product over objectives of (range on the sub-front)/(range on the full front) (p. 11).
- Set the neighbourhood size adaptively as γ times the mean ‖J^†(λ_i − λ_c)‖ over a discretization, choosing γ so that a target fraction of points falls inside (pp. 11, 16).

**Quotes**:
- "Our approach is still restricted to scalarized methods, in particular to the weighted-sum or epsilon-constrained methods, and requires the computation or approximations of first- and second-order derivatives." (p. 1)
- "However, a widely accepted (quantitative) definition for such solutions is lacking, and identifying knee solutions in high-dimensional objective spaces remains challenging." (p. 3)

### S102 · Sequential test sampling for stochastic derivative-free optimization (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2509.14505)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Ding (Lehigh), F. Rinaldi (Padova) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: It follows Rinaldi, Vicente & Zeffiro's weak tail bound (SIAM J. Optim. 2024, [23]), and it is a stochastic version of probabilistic-descent direct search (Gratton, Royer, Vicente & Zhang 2015, [15]). - **D1 Problem & entry**: The setting is noisy, unbiased observations F(x,ξ) of a smooth f with no derivatives available (p. 2). Many DFO methods accept a step through a sufficient-decrease test against cδ², and checking that test under noise can require a large estimation cost (p. 1). To the authors' knowledge, all stochastic DFO methods under finite variance need Θ(δ⁻⁴) samples per iteration (p. 2). Their own tail-bound paper [23] lowered this to O(δ^{−2q}) under a stronger moment assumption. They add that this "does not however necessarily lead to a reduction in the overall computational cost", because the weaker threshold cδ^q raises the iteration complexity to O(nε^{−q/(q−1)}) ([12]) (p. 3). The diagnosis is that all these schemes use fixed sample sizes (Quote 1, p. 3). A sequential scheme [1] exists, but its regret bound translates into roughly O(nε⁻⁶) (p. 3). S102 describes the moment assumption of [23] as "a bounded q/(1−q) moment" (p. 3), whereas SKILL.md's Method 3 has q/(q−1). For q ∈ (1,2], q/(1−q) is negative, so S102's wording looks like a typo. [23] itself was not read here.
  - Transferable technique: open by auditing the cost accounting of your own previous result (per-iteration savings against total cost) and make the gap you find the problem (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Reframing.** Deciding whether to accept a step becomes a test on the sign of the mean of Y = cδ² − (F(x,ξ^x) − F(x+δd,ξ^d)) (2.2). H0 is E[Y] ≤ 0 (accept the step) and H1 is E[Y] > 0 (p. 5). This is not an estimation problem: an accurate estimate of E[Y] is wasted when E[Y] is far from 0 (p. 5).
  - **Test 2.1.** Draw i.i.d. copies of Y until the partial sum leaves [b_l, a_l] (p. 7). The usual fixed-sample Test 2.2 is the special case a_l = ∞, b_l = −∞ for l < m and a_m = b_m = 0 (p. 7). By Markov's inequality it needs m = σ²C⁻² samples, i.e. Θ(δ⁻⁴) when C = Θ(δ²) (p. 7).
  - **Asymmetric accuracy (Definition 3, p. 6).** A truly acceptable step may be rejected with probability up to 1/2 (2.5). A truly unacceptable step may be accepted with probability at most C/µ (2.6), so the larger the violation µ, the smaller the allowed error.
  - **Gaussian case.** Constant boundaries a = −b = c′ ≥ σ²/(2eC) make Test 2.1 a sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) and C-accurate (p. 9).
  - **Sample size (Proposition 2.1, p. 10).** The expected sample size is approximately bounded by σ²/(4e²s²)·δ⁻⁴·min{1, esδ²/|cδ² − (f(x) − f(x+δd))|}. This is O(δ⁻⁴) in the worst case, O(δ⁻³) when the decrease is Θ(δ), and O(δ^{−2−r}) when it is Θ(δ^r) with r ∈ (0,2] (pp. 1, 10, 19).
  - **Algorithm 1 (p. 11).** Probabilistic-descent direct search with *one* direction per iteration, drawn uniformly from the unit sphere. If the test accepts H0, the step is taken and ∆ is expanded by γ; otherwise ∆ is shrunk by θ. The required accuracy is C_k = c∆_k²(1 − θ²)/(2(γ² − θ²)), and running the test requires an estimate of σ.

  What is kept: probabilistic descent's step-size logic and random direction. What changes: only the acceptance test.
  - Transferable technique: when an algorithm makes a binary decision from noisy data, pose it as a test of the sign of a mean rather than as estimating the mean, and let a sequential test stop early on easy decisions (pp. 5, 7).
  - Transferable technique: show that the standard scheme is a special case of yours (a fixed sample is a sequential test with degenerate boundaries), so yours is never worse in the order of δ (pp. 7, 10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Base assumptions.** The noise is unbiased (1.1), and f is C¹, bounded below, with an L_f-Lipschitz gradient (p. 2; Assumption 3.2, p. 13). The observations at x and x+δd are independent (p. 5).
  - **Sample-size result.** It needs Y to be Gaussian (§2.4, p. 9). It rests on Wald's approximate expected sample size ((2.9), p. 9), which is why Proposition 2.1 says "approximately bounded" (p. 10).
  - **Complexity theorem.** It assumes that a C-accurate test that ends properly exists for every C > 0 (Assumption 3.1, p. 10). The paper constructs such a test only for Gaussian Y (pp. 9–10).
  - **Known variance.** The noise variance, or an upper bound on it, is assumed known (pp. 18–19).
  - **Step-size factors.** They must satisfy 3 log γ + 11 log θ > 0 (Theorem 3.6, p. 16). This pushes θ close to 1: the experiments use θ = 0.95 and γ = 1.3 (pp. 17–18).

  The authors call the Gaussian setting "relatively limited" (p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The analysis has two parts.

  *Test theory.*
  1. Lemma 2.1: acceptance probabilities are monotone in µ. The proof couples the test with a copy whose boundaries are shifted to a_l − lΔµ and b_l − lΔµ, plus a change of variables (p. 8).
  2. Lemma 2.2: hence (2.5) needs to be checked only at µ = 0 (p. 8).
  3. Lemma 2.3: a symmetric density with symmetric boundaries gives exactly 1/2 (p. 9).
  4. Wald's operating-characteristic bound A^{−h} is rewritten as exp(−2c′µ/σ²) and turned into ≤ C/µ via Proposition A.2 (1/t^x ≤ 1/x for all x ≥ 1 iff t ≥ e^{1/e}) (pp. 9, 22).
  5. Wald's approximate average sample number is bounded via Proposition A.1 (pp. 9–10, 21).

  *Complexity.*
  6. The merit function is Φ_k = f(X_k) − f* + η∆_k² with η = c/(γ² − θ²). Lemma 3.1 shows E[Φ_k − Φ_{k+1} | F_k] ≥ ν∆_k² with ν = (c/2)(1 − θ²)/(γ² − θ²) (pp. 12–13). The proof conditions on a half-step σ-algebra F_{k+1/2}, in which the direction is drawn but the decision is not yet made (p. 11). It then splits into acceptable and unacceptable directions. In the unacceptable case the C/µ bound caps the expected loss from a wrong acceptance at C_k. That is half of the term c(1 − θ²)/(γ² − θ²)∆_k² that a rejection guarantees, which leaves ν∆_k² (p. 12).
  7. Lemma 3.2, imported from [15], says a uniform direction is 1/(7√n)-descent with probability ≥ 3/7 (p. 13). Lemma 3.3 applies the descent lemma to get the threshold δ_ε = 2ε/((7L_f + 14c)√n) (p. 13). Lemma 3.4 gives a success probability ≥ 3/7 · 1/2 = 3/14 when ∆_k ≤ δ_ε (p. 14).
  8. A Bernoulli W_k ∈ {log γ, log θ} with P(log γ) = 3/14 is constructed by thinning the successes (3.15), so that log(∆_{k+1}/∆_k) ≥ W_{k+1} below the threshold (pp. 15–16, Lemma 3.5).
  9. Appendix B extends the renewal–reward supermartingale theorem of Blanchet, Cartis, Menickelly & Scheinberg [5]. That theorem assumed p > 1/2 and steps ±1. The extension allows any p and any a > 0 > b with positive drift p(a − b) + b > 0. Lemma B.1 bounds the expected return time by (a − b)/(pa − pb + b) via a martingale and Wald's identity (pp. 23–25), and Lemma B.2 follows (p. 25). Theorem B.3 is taken from [5] with "no need to repeat the proof" (p. 25).

  The result is Theorem 3.6: E[T_ε] = O(n/ε²) iterations, with explicit constants (p. 16). The bound counts iterations. No bound on the total number of samples needed to reach ε is stated (checked §§2–5 and the appendices).
  - Transferable technique: tie the allowed error probability of a noisy decision to the size of the error, P(wrong accept) ≤ C/µ. The expected damage µ·P is then bounded by C and fits straight into the merit-function decrease (pp. 6, 12).
  - Transferable technique: when the per-iteration success probability is below 1/2, keep the renewal–reward argument by requiring a positive drift of the log step size, p log γ + (1 − p) log θ > 0, in place of p > 1/2 (pp. 16, 22–23).
  - Transferable technique: condition on an intermediate σ-algebra F_{k+1/2} that contains the random direction but not the test outcome. This handles the randomness of the direction and of the test in separate steps (pp. 11–14).
- **D5 Experiments**: The test set is 38 CUTEst problems suggested in [13] (the group's paper on the limitations of neural nets), giving 91 instances with n from 2 to 100 (Table 4.1, p. 17). The noise is additive i.i.d. Gaussian with σ² ∈ {0.01, 1} (p. 17). The budget is 10 000 samples (p. 16). Data and performance profiles use τ = 0.1, and each instance is run 10 times, each run counted as an instance (p. 17). Parameters: CUTEst starting points, δ₀ = 1, c = 0.5, and θ = 0.95, γ = 1.3 for both tests (pp. 17–18). The fixed test uses m = σ_k²C_k⁻² samples; the sequential test uses boundaries ±σ_k²/(2eC_k) (p. 18). Result: the sequential test beats the fixed test, and the gap grows with the variance (p. 18, Figs. 4.1–4.2). Robustness: larger budgets, other τ values, smaller variances and other θ and γ were tried with no significant change in relative performance (p. 18). The only comparison is this internal ablation (Test 2.1 vs Test 2.2 inside Algorithm 1). No other stochastic DFO solver, such as ASTRO-DF, StoMADS or the tail-bound methods of [23], is run (checked §4). The text gives no code link (checked).
  - Transferable technique: compare two acceptance rules inside the same algorithm with identical parameters, chosen to help the baseline. Here a large θ keeps δ away from 0, which limits the fixed-sample blow-up (pp. 17–18).
- **D6 Positioning**: The literature review (pp. 2–3) has two groups:
  - trust-region methods: Larson–Billups [18], ASTRO-DF [24, 17], Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg [7], Blanchet et al. [5] and Cartis–Scheinberg [6], all traced back to the group's probabilistic trust-region paper [3] (p. 3);
  - direct search: StoMADS [2], Dzahini [11], the 2025 direct-search survey [12] and the group's own tail bound [23].

  The statistical lineage is cited from its origins: Dodge & Romig 1929, Wald, Wald–Wolfowitz and Siegmund (pp. 4–6). Common practice is reframed rather than attacked: fixed-sample testing is presented as a restricted case of sequential testing (Quote 2, p. 19). The group's own [23] is the one criticized, on cost accounting (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution section follows the paper's structure (§2 the test, §3 complexity, §4 numerics; pp. 3–4). A separate paragraph argues the flexibility gain in plain words: the fixed scheme samples the same amount "even when the trial step is clearly acceptable or clearly unacceptable" (p. 4). A one-paragraph proof idea precedes Lemma 2.1 (p. 8), and Proposition 2.1 is followed by a prose reading of its two regimes (p. 10). The hedges sit in the conclusion: "we believe that Test 2.1 will still work well for other noises beyond Gaussian" (p. 19), justified by the Brownian-motion approximation of random walks (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a framework (sequential testing of sufficient decrease), a sample-size result, an O(n/ε²) iteration bound via a generalized renewal–reward theorem, and numerics (pp. 3–4, 18–19). The stated limits are Gaussian noise in the proposition and the experiments, and a known variance, with variance estimation (Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal [4]; Moré–Wild [21]) left to implementation (p. 19). Several limits are visible but not stated:
  - the sample-size result is an approximation (p. 10);
  - there is no total sample complexity;
  - the complexity theorem is conditional on Assumption 3.1 (p. 10);
  - only one algorithm, single-direction probabilistic descent, is treated, although the abstract opens with "many derivative-free optimization algorithms" (p. 1);
  - there is no external baseline.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. What is relaxed is the correctness of the accept/reject *decision*, required only in probability given the past ((3.2)–(3.3), p. 11). It becomes a concrete stopping rule, boundaries ±σ_k²/(2eC_k) (p. 18), as Method 3 step 3 prescribes.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (step 4). The guarantee is a bound on the *expected* number of iterations E[T_ε], obtained via renewal–reward (Theorem 3.6, p. 16; Appendix B). It is not almost-sure convergence plus a high-probability bound.
- Method 3 step 5 and the *Using 1 random direction* anti-pattern ⚠ variant. Algorithm 1 polls a single random direction (p. 11), with success probability only ≥ 3/14 (p. 14), and still has a complexity guarantee. The threshold is carried by the step-size factors, 3 log γ + 11 log θ > 0 (p. 16). The authors warn that without this condition the step size may go to 0 at non-stationary points (p. 17). The anti-pattern would be more accurate if it named the threshold set by (γ, θ, number of directions) rather than the use of one direction.
- Heuristic 3 ✅ and Workflow D step 3 ✅. The paper controls the estimated decrease and lets the data decide the sample size (pp. 5, 10).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The skeleton is kept and only the acceptance test is redefined (Algorithm 1, p. 11).
- Method 2 ✅ / ⚠. The O(n/ε²) bound matches the deterministic probabilistic-descent bound (pp. 2, 16), and samples per iteration are compared with Θ(δ⁻⁴) (p. 10). However, the count is in iterations, and no total sample count to reach ε is given, whereas Method 2 asks for evaluations.
- Method 5 ⚠ partial. A collection (91 CUTEst instances) and data and performance profiles are used ✅ (p. 17). The only baseline is the fixed-sample ablation, and no code release is mentioned (pp. 16–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Test sufficient decrease as H0: E[cδ² − (F(x) − F(x+δd))] ≤ 0 with a two-boundary random-walk stopping rule, and recover fixed sampling as the degenerate case (pp. 5, 7).
- Use asymmetric accuracy: P(reject | acceptable) ≤ 1/2 and P(accept | unacceptable) ≤ C/µ, with C = Θ(δ²) (p. 6).
- Prove monotonicity of a sequential test's operating characteristic by coupling with a shifted-boundary test (Lemma 2.1, p. 8).
- Use the merit function Φ_k = f(X_k) − f* + η∆_k² with η = c/(γ² − θ²), analysed through a half-step σ-algebra (pp. 11–13).
- Apply renewal–reward with arbitrary success probability p and step factors (γ, θ), under the drift condition p log γ + (1 − p) log θ > 0 (pp. 16, 22–25).
- Experiment design: the same algorithm and parameters with only the test swapped, two noise levels, data and performance profiles, and robustness sweeps reported in one sentence (pp. 17–18).
