# Paper cards · batch c01 (core)

Batch intent: See how Vicente worked before derivative-free optimization. The batch covers the Coimbra/Waterloo bilevel period (1994 bibliography with Calamai) and the Rice period with Dennis and Heinkenschloss (trust-region interior-point SQP, 1998; inexact trust-region SQP, 2002). Two questions: (a) which habits that SKILL.md attributes to the later DFO work are already present? Candidates are reusing a convergent skeleton and changing one object (Methods 1 and 4), tying accuracy to predicted decrease (the precursor of Method 3), and shipping software or test generators (Method 5). (b) Before worst-case complexity counts existed (Method 2), what did a finished result look like?
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all; core batch). Extra weight on D2 (what was kept from the parent method and what was changed), D4 (how much of the parent proof is reused) and D6 (how earlier theories are nested as special cases).
Material roles: S002 core (1994 bibliography review; survey, no theorems) · S011 core (SIAM J. Control Optim. 1998; read from Rice TR94-45, revised Sept. 1997) · S013 core (SIAM J. Optim. 2002; read from the authors' preprint).

### S002 · Bilevel and multilevel programming: A bibliography review (Journal of Global Optimization 1994, DOI 10.1007/bf01096458)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: P. H. Calamai · **Position in trajectory**: Vicente's pre-PhD period. His affiliation is the Departamento de Matemática, Coimbra, and Calamai's is Systems Design Engineering, Waterloo (p. 1). The work is funded by INIC Portugal and NSERC Canada (p. 1). It is written after his 1992 Coimbra master's thesis on bilevel programming (ref. [159], p. 22). **Read**: full text. The 175-entry reference list (pp. 10–23) was read in full to check self-citations. **Version note**: the file cites items dated 1995 ([104] p. 18, [153] p. 22, [173] p. 22). Together with "updated biannually" (p. 9), this suggests the file is a later update of the bibliography, not the exact 1994 print.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is a literature infrastructure gap, not an open theorem. The paper is "a bibliography of all references central to bilevel and multilevel programming" (p. 1). It says the bibliography should be regarded as "a dynamic and permanent contribution", with reader additions and corrections added periodically (p. 1). The problem class is stated formally before any literature: the BPP min_{x,y} F(x,y) s.t. g ≤ 0, with y solving the lower-level problem min_y f(x,y) s.t. h ≤ 0 (p. 2). The objects are named before the review starts (pp. 2–3):
  - the relaxed feasible set Ω, the lower-level feasible set Ω(x) and the reaction set M(x);
  - the lower-level optimal value v(x);
  - the induced region IR, which is the feasible set of the BPP.

  The stated motivation is that the area is "still a fertile area for research" (p. 4). It also subsumes other important problems (minimax, linear integer, bilinear and quadratic programs) as special instances (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: Open a survey with a formal problem statement plus a named vocabulary (Ω, Ω(x), M(x), v(x), IR) before any citation. Every later classification can then point to which object a method exploits (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & organization**: The organizing idea is a classification scheme rather than an algorithm. The paper is organized as follows:
  - **§2 Properties**: optimality conditions, complexity, related problems (pp. 4–6).
  - **§3 Solution methods, in five classes** (pp. 6–8):
    - extreme-point methods (p. 6);
    - branch and bound (p. 7);
    - complementarity pivot (p. 7);
    - descent methods (pp. 7–8);
    - penalty-function methods (p. 8).
  - **§4 Multilevel programming and applications**: transportation, management, planning, engineering design (pp. 8–9).
  - **§5 How to contribute** (pp. 9–10).

  The convex BPP is singled out: under a constraint qualification, the lower level can be replaced by its KKT conditions to give a one-level program, but even then the induced region is nonconvex and can be disconnected or empty (p. 3). The paper distinguishes three convex subclasses, linear, linear–quadratic and quadratic (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: Classify algorithms by the *structural property they exploit*, for example "global solution at an extreme point of Ω" for extreme-point methods (p. 6) and "complementarity between multipliers and slacks" for branch and bound (p. 7). This is more useful than a chronological order.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Continuous BPPs are the main case, and integer variants are mentioned under branch and bound (p. 7). The extreme-point property is given with its assumption history:
  - Candler–Townsley: no upper-level constraints and a unique lower-level solution;
  - Bard, and Bialas–Karwan: Ω bounded;
  - Savard: "under no particular assumptions".

  The paper notes that the property fails for linear–quadratic BPPs (p. 6). The static Stackelberg problem is separated from the BPP by the non-singleton reaction set case (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: When reviewing a structural result, list how its assumptions were weakened paper by paper, and state where it fails (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No original proofs; it is a survey. The analytical devices it records are:
  - the KKT replacement of a convex lower level (p. 3);
  - the extreme-point property of linear BPPs (p. 6);
  - reductions between problem classes (bilinear → linear BPP by Gallo–Ülkücü; the partial reciprocal via a penalized bilinear program; QP → quadratic BPP in Vicente's thesis [159]) (p. 5);
  - the NP-hardness chain for linear BPPs: Jeroslow; shorter proofs by Bard and by Ben-Ayed–Blair; strong NP-hardness by Hansen–Jaumard–Savard; checking local optimality is NP-hard by Vicente–Savard–Júdice (p. 4).

  The review also records that a counterexample (Clarke–Westerberg; Haurie–Savard–White) invalidated Bard's optimality conditions, so two algorithms built on them "are not convergent" (p. 4). It notes that the Bialas–Karwan–Shaw pivot algorithm cannot compute global solutions, as shown by examples (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: In a literature review, trace each algorithm back to the optimality theory it relies on. A counterexample to the theory then immediately flags which algorithms are unsound (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The paper does point to benchmark infrastructure: "test problem generators" by Calamai and Vicente for linear, linear–quadratic and quadratic BPPs (p. 6; refs [45]–[48], SIAM J. Sci. Stat. Comput. 1993 and ACM TOMS Algorithm 728, 1994, pp. 13–14). It also points to a technique for generating linear BPPs with an exponential number of local minima (p. 4). The comparative efficiency judgments are borrowed, not measured: Hansen–Jaumard–Savard branch and bound "seems particularly efficient" for medium-scale problems, and so does Júdice–Faustino SLCP (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: The review credits pioneers explicitly: Bracken–McGill (1973) for the formulation, Candler–Norton for the name, and Stackelberg for the motivation (p. 3). It evaluates prior work in two ways:
  - **Direct critique by counterexample**: Bard's conditions and the Bialas–Karwan–Shaw pivot method (pp. 4, 7).
  - **Critique of approach**: the nonsmooth-analysis optimality conditions "mostly ignored the special geometry of the BPP" (p. 4). Savard–Gauvin and Vicente–Calamai are then presented as filling that gap (p. 4).

  Self-citations are woven into every section:
  - optimality conditions [160] (p. 4);
  - complexity [48], [162] (p. 4);
  - reductions [159] (p. 5);
  - generators [46]–[48] (p. 6);
  - extreme induced-region points and directions [162] (p. 6);
  - step-size rules for descent [162] (p. 7).

  In all, 8 of 175 references ([45]–[48], [159]–[162]) include Vicente as author (pp. 13–14, 22).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Opinion is signalled openly: "It is our opinion that…" (p. 4) and "We believe that bilevel programming can play an important role…" (p. 9). The problem-class definition and history come first, and the taxonomy and applications come after (pp. 2–9). The scope and inclusion rules are explicit. Covered: theory, algorithms and numerical results, software and generation of test problems, applications and complexity. Included: books, journal articles and technical reports that are broadly available. Excluded: conference and seminar abstracts (p. 9). A full ftp session transcript is given so that readers can obtain the LaTeX and BibTeX sources (pp. 9–10).
  - Transferable technique: State inclusion and exclusion criteria for a survey in one paragraph, and give readers the source files plus a correction channel (pp. 9–10).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution level: infrastructure (bibliography plus classification), not theory. There is no explicit limitations paragraph. The paper does not compare algorithm performance itself (observation from reading pp. 6–8). Future directions:
  - bilevel programming as a tool for analyzing "the step selection subproblem in a trust region algorithm for nonlinear equality constrained optimization" (p. 9, citing Celis–Dennis–Tapia [55]). This anticipates the trust-region SQP work at Rice with Dennis (S011).
  - the discriminant problem (p. 9);
  - multilevel problems with more than two levels, which are much harder (Blair) (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (earliest form; the artefacts are test generators and a living bibliography, not a solver). The generators are offered as the common testbed for comparing algorithms (p. 6). The bibliography's source files are distributed by ftp, with a standing invitation to send corrections (pp. 1, 9–10), which is the same idea as the errata channel in Method 5 step 5. There are no profiles and no experiments.
- new-pattern candidate: **controlled-difficulty test-problem generators**. The Calamai–Vicente generators for linear, linear–quadratic and quadratic BPPs, and the construction of linear BPPs with exponentially many local minima (pp. 4, 6, 13–14).
- new-pattern candidate: **exploit the problem's own geometry**. The review criticizes generic nonsmooth optimality analysis for ignoring BPP geometry. It then presents geometry-based first- and second-order conditions (Vicente–Calamai) and extreme induced-region points and directions (Vicente–Savard–Júdice) (pp. 4, 6).
- Relates to Research Taste warning sign 6 and Heuristic 10 (counterexamples to boundary theory). The review foregrounds a counterexample that made two algorithms nonconvergent (p. 4), and pivot-method examples that disproved a global-solution claim (p. 7). This is evidence that the counterexample lens was part of Vicente's early reading. It is not his own counterexample.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build or cite a *problem generator* with controlled structure (known extreme-point solutions, a known number of local minima) rather than a fixed hand-picked set, so that algorithm classes can be compared on equal terms (pp. 4, 6).
- Organize a review by structural property exploited (extreme points, KKT complementarity, descent in the induced region, exact penalties), and state the assumption under which each property holds (pp. 6–8).
- For each optimality theory surveyed, name the algorithms that depend on it, so a counterexample propagates to them (p. 4).
- Publish a survey as a maintained artefact (source files plus a correction channel) and say so in the abstract (pp. 1, 9–10).

**Quotes**:
- "these aforementioned contributions have mostly ignored the special geometry of the BPP." (p. 4)
- "In most cases these algorithms can be tested and compared using the test problem generators proposed by P. Calamai and L. Vicente [46], [48], [47]" (p. 6)

### S011 · Trust-region interior-point SQP algorithms for a class of nonlinear programming problems (SIAM Journal on Control and Optimization 1998, DOI 10.1137/s036012995279031)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. E. Dennis, M. Heinkenschloss · **Position in trajectory**: Vicente's Rice PhD work. The footnote says it "was developed while the author was a graduate student" at Rice CAAM (p. 2). The thesis is ref. [65] (pp. 47–48). The Rice–Coimbra collaboration grew out of an April 1994 workshop on optimal design and control at Virginia Tech (p. 45). **Read**: full text (§§1–12, pp. 2–45). The reference list was skimmed (pp. 45–48).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min f(y,u) s.t. C(y,u) = 0 and a ≤ u ≤ b, where y are states and u are controls. It arises from discretized optimal control, optimal design and parameter identification governed by ODEs and PDEs (pp. 2–3). The entry point is an **implementation constraint of the application**. Early and many recent SQP algorithms rely on factorizations of the Jacobian, which is not feasible here, because the matrices are too large or not available explicitly. What *is* available is matrix–vector products with C_y, C_u and their transposes, plus solvers for the linearized state equation C_y v = r and the adjoint equation C_y^T v = r (p. 3). The gap: purely local analyses existed without bounds [34, 36, 37, 39]. The paper takes on "the much more difficult issue" of doing this with global convergence and bounds on u (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable technique: Enter a problem by writing down the *exact oracle the application can supply* (here: products with C_y, C_u and their transposes, plus linearized-state and adjoint solves). Then design an algorithm that asks for nothing more (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The step is split in two (pp. 5, 13–14):
  - a **quasi-normal component** s^n = ((s^n)_y, 0), essentially a trust-region-globalized Newton step for the state equation C(y,u) = 0 with u fixed;
  - a **tangential component** s^t = W_k s_u, where W = (−C_y^{-1}C_u; I) spans the null space of J. The u-part s_u is essentially a Newton-like step for the reduced problem min f̂(u) = f(y(u),u).

  Bounds on u are handled by the Coleman–Li affine scaling D(x). Its diagonal depends on the distance to the bound *and* on the sign of the reduced gradient (3.1), pp. 4, 10. The scaling is derived by applying Newton's method to the nonlinear system C(x) = 0, D(x)²W(x)^T∇f(x) = 0 (§4, pp. 11–13). The algorithmic scaling D̂ (4.7) additionally includes the cross term W^T∇²ℓ s^n (p. 12). The tangential subproblem (5.6) minimizes a quadratic with an extra term E_k D̂_k^{-2} inside the trust region ‖D̂_k^{-1}s_u‖ ≤ δ_k, with damping θ_k ∈ [σ,1) to keep u strictly interior (p. 15). There are two trust-region shapes (pp. 15–16):
  - **Decoupled**: separate radii on (s^n)_y and D̂^{-1}s_u. It is cheaper.
  - **Coupled**: it bounds the true size of W_k s_u. It is better when C_y is ill-conditioned, because then κ(W_k) is large and the decoupled version "may underestimate vastly the size of this step component".

  **Kept** from Dennis–El-Alem–Maciel [15]:
  - the composite step;
  - the augmented Lagrangian merit function L = f + λ^T C + ρ C^T C;
  - the ared/pred ratio test;
  - El-Alem's penalty update (pp. 19–20).

  **Changed**: the metric and shape of the tangential subproblem (affine scaling), and interior damping. Algorithm 5.1 is stated on p. 20, and a reduced-Hessian variant (5.24) on p. 19.
  - Transferable technique: Derive the *local* step as Newton's method on a reformulated KKT system (here C = 0, D²W^T∇f = 0). Then globalize that same step with a trust region, so the fast local step is built into the method from the start (pp. 11–13, 20).
  - Transferable technique: When a step has two components with different costs, offer both a decoupled trust region (cheap) and a coupled one (faithful). Give the switching criterion explicitly: the condition number of the null-space basis (p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: A.1–A.6 (p. 21):
  - f and c_i are C²;
  - C_y is nonsingular;
  - f, ∇f, ∇²f, C, J and ∇²c_i are bounded;
  - {W_k}, {H_k} and {λ_k} are bounded;
  - C_y^{-1} is uniformly bounded;
  - {u_k} is bounded.

  Invertibility of C_y automatically gives linear independence of the active constraints, so no separate CQ is needed (p. 9). The step conditions are graded by the result they buy (p. 22):
  - **C.1** (quasi-normal conditions (5.1), (5.4), (5.5), fraction of Cauchy decrease, θ_k ∈ [σ,1)) gives first-order convergence.
  - **C.1–C.3** give second-order convergence. C.2 is a fraction of *optimal* decrease. C.3 requires Lipschitz second derivatives and the exact Hessian with adjoint multipliers.
  - **C.1, C.3 and C.4** give the q-quadratic rate. C.4 requires the damped Newton step to be taken when it fits, with |θ_k − 1| = O(‖D̂_k g_k‖).

  The authors flag which assumptions are technical:
  - the square roots in D are "necessary for the proof of local q-quadratic convergence". Otherwise D^p, p ≥ 1, works (Remark 3.1, p. 10; Remark 8.1, p. 33);
  - nondegeneracy (strict complementarity) is needed only for the local rate (Def. 3.1, p. 10; §9).

  For the application, A.3 and A.5 are shown to hold for suitable discretization parameters (p. 41).
  - Transferable technique: Present assumptions as a *ladder* (C.1 → first order; +C.2, C.3 → second order; +C.4 → q-quadratic). Say explicitly that satisfying a higher rung does not imply the lower one (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **§6 Toolbox** (pp. 21–26):
    - Lemma 6.1: ‖s_k‖ ≤ κ Δ_k, and δ_{k+1} ≥ κ' ‖s_k‖ after a rejection.
    - Lemma 6.2: fraction of Cauchy decrease implies q(s^n) − q(s^n + W s_u) ≥ κ6 ‖D̂g‖ min{κ7‖D̂g‖, κ8 δ}. The proof minimizes a one-dimensional quadratic along the scaled steepest-descent direction, capped at T_k, which is the minimum of the trust-region length and the distance to the bounds.
    - Lemma 6.3: fraction of optimal decrease gives a κ θ²γ δ² bound.
    - Lemma 6.4: bounds on the quasi-normal decrease.
    - Lemma 6.5: |ared − pred| ≤ κ(‖s‖² + ρ(‖s‖³ + ‖C‖‖s‖²)).
    - Lemma 6.6: the penalty parameter is monotone, and pred ≥ (ρ/2)(‖C‖² − ‖Js+C‖²).
  - **Theorem 7.1** (first order, pp. 26–29): proof by contradiction, assuming ‖D̂g‖ + ‖C‖ > tol for all k.
    - Case split: either ‖C_k‖ is at most a constant (7.2) times δ_k, in which case pred ≥ κ δ_k and the penalty parameter need not grow; or it is larger, in which case the normal decrease alone gives pred ≥ κ.
    - Then |ared/pred − 1| ≤ κ δ_k, so an acceptable step is always found.
    - Summing the successful decreases contradicts the boundedness of L_k.
    - The boundedness of ρ_k and L_k and the lower bound on δ_k are *imported* from [15]: "Proof. See Lemmas 7.9–7.13, 8.2 in [15]" (p. 28).
    - A final "standard argument" replaces D̂ by D (pp. 28–29).
  - **Theorem 8.2** (second order, pp. 31–33): the second-order necessary conditions hold even in the degenerate case. The proof uses an indicator function h to identify active indices and shows that the trust-region multiplier γ_{k_j} → 0 (pp. 31–33).
  - **Local analysis** (pp. 34–37):
    - Theorem 9.1: δ_k is bounded away from 0, and eventually every iteration is successful.
    - Lemma 9.2: the scaled-back Newton quasi-normal step (9.9) satisfies C.1.
    - Corollary 9.1: q-quadratic convergence. The key step is proving that the damped Newton step satisfies the fraction of Cauchy decrease, so the global safeguard never blocks it. The rate then follows from Dennis–Schnabel Thm 5.2.1 (pp. 36–37).
  - **Inner solver**: for the quasi-normal CG steps, a classical Powell result gives the Cauchy-type decrease (p. 38).
  - Transferable technique: To get global convergence *and* a fast local rate, prove a **compatibility lemma**: the fast step satisfies the global acceptance conditions (here, the damped Newton step satisfies fraction of Cauchy decrease, pp. 36–37).
  - Transferable technique: When extending a known convergence theory, keep its lemma structure. Re-prove only the lemmas that touch the new object (here the scaled Cauchy bound, Lemma 6.2) and cite the rest by lemma number (p. 28).
- **D5 Experiments**: One application: boundary control of a nonlinear heat equation, the heating of a probe in a kiln (pp. 40–42).
  - Discretization: piecewise-linear finite elements in space and backward Euler in time. With Nt = 100 and Nx = 20 this gives n = 2200 and m = 2100 (pp. 41–42).
  - Implementation and hardware: TRICE, FORTRAN 77, run on a Sun Sparcstation 10 (p. 41).
  - Four variants: decoupled or coupled trust region × reduced or full Hessian, with L-BFGS memory 5 (p. 42).
  - Scaling: problem-scaled inner products (discretized L² and L²(H¹) norms) (p. 41).
  - Stopping rule: ‖D W^T∇f‖ + ‖C‖ < 10⁻⁸ (p. 42).
  - Metrics: iterations, final residuals, the last step norm, the final radius and the final penalty parameter. Tables 11.1–11.2 cover two regularization parameters, and runs took 14–20 iterations with "no rejected steps" (pp. 42–43).
  - Cost: cost per trial step is reported in *linear solves with C_y and C_y^T*. The decoupled reduced-Hessian variant needs three per step and "seems to be the best" (pp. 43–44). The solve count is also derived analytically: 2I(k)+2 or I(k)+2 per step, where I(k) is the number of CG iterations (pp. 39–40).
  - Ablation of the scaling (p. 44): Coleman–Li scaling against Dikin–Karmarkar scaling, which carries no dual information. Dikin–Karmarkar stops in 11 iterations but at the wrong solution, with variables at the wrong bound (Figs. 11.1–11.2).
  - Not done: no problem collection and no profiles. Mesh-independence and applications to other problems are only cited ([11], [30], [31]; pp. 4, 45).
  - Transferable technique: Justify a design choice with a **negative example for the alternative**. Show that the cheaper-looking option (Dikin–Karmarkar) converges faster to a wrong point, and give the mechanism: wrong bound, negative multipliers (p. 44).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper claims to extend two lines at once:
  - first-order results for simple bounds (Coleman–Li [13]) and for equality constraints (Dennis–El-Alem–Maciel [15]) (p. 6);
  - second-order results for Coleman–Li [13] and Dennis–Vicente [19] (p. 6).

  The self-description is strong: the family is "unique in the consequent use of structure…, the use of optimization techniques successfully applied in other contexts…, and the rigorous theoretical justification" (p. 3). Two approaches are set side by side: the black-box (reduced) approach and the all-at-once (SQP) approach. The reduced problem is kept as an interpretive lens, since the SQP gradient and Hessian coincide with Newton on f̂ at feasible points (pp. 5, 8). Prior work is mostly restated, not attacked. The only direct negative verdict is numerical, on Dikin–Karmarkar scaling (p. 44). Self-citations: Dennis–Vicente [18], [19], Heinkenschloss–Vicente [31], the thesis [65], and [66] on state constraints (pp. 45–48).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - **Contribution statement**: the abstract lists the four results in order: first-order global convergence "under very mild conditions", second-order convergence under "more stringent" conditions, the q-quadratic rate, and inclusion of both special cases (p. 2).
  - **Structure**: motivation before algorithm. §2 on structure (pp. 6–8), §3 on the nonstandard form of the KKT conditions (pp. 9–11) and §4 on Newton's method (pp. 11–13) all come before the algorithm in §5 (p. 13).
  - **Remarks** carry the generality claims (Remarks 3.1, 5.1, 8.1, 10.1, 10.2).
  - **Hedging** is low. Promotional sentences are present: "very attractive and powerful tools" (p. 4). There are pragmatic asides: "the above suffices to prove convergence results" for the radius update (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: Spend the early sections showing that the new method uses *the same quantities* practitioners already compute for the black-box approach (pp. 5–8). That argument about adoption cost comes before any theorem.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contributions are on four levels:
  - an algorithm family;
  - theory: first-order, second-order and local q-quadratic results;
  - software: TRICE, beta-tested for electronic distribution (p. 4) and available via the internet (p. 41; ref. [16], p. 46);
  - an application.

  Limits stated concretely:
  - the decoupled approach can perform poorly for ill-conditioned state equations (pp. 4, 16);
  - the q-quadratic rate needs nondegeneracy and the exact Hessian (C.3–C.4, p. 22);
  - a comprehensive study of problem scaling is "beyond the scope" (p. 4).

  Future work (p. 45):
  - bounds on the states [66];
  - an inexact analysis for inexact linear solvers and directional derivatives [31], which became S013;
  - an infinite-dimensional formulation.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The step generator is free: "a variety of methods, including many iterative techniques" (p. 2), such as truncated CG (Algs. 10.1–10.2, pp. 38–39). The only requirement is fraction of Cauchy decrease (pp. 16–17, 22). The fast step (damped Newton, C.4) is taken whenever it fits, and a compatibility proof shows the safeguard never rejects it (pp. 36–37). Difference from Method 1: the safeguard is a trust region plus a merit-function ratio test, not direct-search sufficient decrease, and the "heuristic" is Newton's step.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. It reuses the Dennis–El-Alem–Maciel skeleton (composite step, augmented Lagrangian, ared/pred, penalty update), and the proof "follows the structure of the convergence theory presented in [15]" (p. 26), importing Lemma 7.4 wholesale (p. 28). What changes is the *metric of the tangential subproblem* (affine scaling D̂) and interior damping, not the definition of success (pp. 14–15, 19–20).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The solver is shipped: TRICE, FORTRAN 77, internet (pp. 4, 41). But the evaluation is one application problem with four configurations and an ablation of the scaling, with no collection and no profiles (pp. 42–44).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (pre-complexity era). The results are lim inf, second order and q-quadratic, with no worst-case counts (p. 2). The cost unit is *linear solves with C_y and C_y^T per trial step* (pp. 39–40, 44), the analogue of counting function evaluations.
- new-pattern candidate: **nest the known theories as special cases**. The abstract says the results "include as special cases current results for only equality constraints and for only simple bounds" (p. 2). This is repeated on p. 6 and in the conclusion (p. 44).
- new-pattern candidate: **minimal application oracle / matrix-free interface**. The algorithm requires only products with C_y and C_u and their transposes, plus linearized-state and adjoint solves. This is "information that the user has to provide anyway" for a black-box Newton approach (pp. 3, 5).
- new-pattern candidate: **negative example for the rejected alternative**. Dikin–Karmarkar scaling reaches the wrong bound (p. 44).

**Transferable techniques**:
- State the minimal oracle the application can supply, and make every algorithmic quantity expressible through it. Count cost per trial step in calls to that oracle (pp. 3, 39–40, 44).
- Build the algorithm as Newton on a reformulated KKT system, then globalize. Prove that the Newton step eventually satisfies the global acceptance conditions (pp. 11–13, 36–37).
- Grade conditions by result: basic for first order, stronger for second order, stronger still for the local rate. State which result each set buys (p. 22).
- When two variants trade cost against fidelity (decoupled or coupled trust region), give the quantity that decides between them: κ(W_k) (p. 16).
- Ablate one design component against a plausible alternative, and report a convergence to the *wrong* point, not only iteration counts (p. 44).

**Quotes**:
- "The results given here include as special cases current results for only equality constraints and for only simple bounds." (p. 2)
- "In the presence of bound constraints, this task goes well beyond the mere replacement of matrix factorizations by black-box solvers." (p. 3)

### S013 · Analysis of inexact trust-region SQP algorithms (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2002, DOI 10.1137/s1052623499361543)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M. Heinkenschloss · **Position in trajectory**: This is the follow-up announced in S011's conclusion (S011 p. 45). Its predecessor TR95-18 (1995, revised 1996) covered the interior-point case under stronger assumptions (ref. [20], p. 18; p. 17). Vicente's affiliation is now Coimbra (p. 1). **Read**: full text (§§1–6, pp. 1–17). The reference list was skimmed (pp. 17–18).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min f(y,u) s.t. C(y,u) = 0 (p. 1). The entry point is again the application's linear algebra. Systems C_y z = d and C_y^T z = d (linearized state and adjoint) are costly and "has to be accomplished by iterative methods" such as Krylov, multigrid or domain decomposition. Only approximate solutions with a residual tolerance are available (p. 1). The gap is stated precisely:
  - existing composite-step trust-region SQP theories "rely on the exact solution of linear systems" (p. 2);
  - inexact Newton and local inexact SQP are understood;
  - global line-search reduced SQP exists [23];
  - "The influence of inexact problem information on the global convergence of trust–region SQP methods, however, is to our knowledge not yet studied." (p. 2)
  - Transferable technique: Locate the gap on a grid of known results (inexact Newton → local inexact SQP → global line-search SQP → global trust-region SQP?) so that the missing cell is obvious (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The predicted decrease is split into two parts:
  - **pred(s^n, s_u)**, computed from quantities already available: the approximate model m̂_k with approximate reduced gradient ĝ_k, and the linearized feasibility gain (4.8), p. 9;
  - **rpred(r^t)**, the contribution of the residual r^t of the inexact solve for the tangential y-component (4.9), p. 9.

  Steps are accepted and rejected on pred alone. The inexact y-solve is controlled by two conditions (p. 10):
  - |rpred| ≤ ξ0 · pred, with ξ0 ∈ (0, 1 − η1) (4.10);
  - ‖r^t‖ ≤ ξ3 δ ‖s_u‖ (4.11).

  Both are implied by an explicit residual bound (4.12) and (5.6), pp. 10, 17. The other conditions are:
  - reduced-gradient accuracy ‖ĝ − W^T(Hs^n + ∇ℓ)‖ ≤ ξ1 min{‖ĝ‖, δ} (4.3), where ξ1 is *not* restricted to be < 1 (p. 8);
  - a curvature bound on the approximate reduced Hessian (4.5) (p. 9);
  - fraction of Cauchy decrease on the *approximate* model (4.6) (p. 9);
  - multiplier estimates that are only required to be bounded (p. 11).

  The penalty update uses only quantities "not contaminated by inaccuracies" in (s^t_y)_k (pp. 10–11). **Kept**: Algorithm 3.1 of Dennis–El-Alem–Maciel (pp. 6–7). **Changed**: steps i2.2, i2.4 and i2.6–i2.8 of Algorithm 4.3 (pp. 11–12). The design follows three stated criteria (pp. 7–8):
  1. conditions as weak as possible, allowing cheap information far from the solution;
  2. conditions comparable to the unconstrained black-box conditions;
  3. conditions that are implementable, independent of Lipschitz constants and derivative bounds.
  - Transferable technique: When some part of the step is computed inexactly, split the model decrease into a *computable* part and a *residual* part. Accept on the computable part, and bound the residual by a fraction of it (pp. 9–10).
  - Transferable technique: Keep inexact quantities out of the update of any parameter that can only grow, such as the penalty. Otherwise inexactness inflates it and slows convergence (pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: A.1–A.4 (p. 7):
  - f and c_i are C²;
  - C_y is nonsingular;
  - f, ∇f, ∇²f, C, J and ∇²c_i are bounded, and C_y^{-1} is uniformly bounded;
  - {H_k}, {W_k} and {λ_k} are bounded.

  The user must be able to adjust the level of inexactness (p. 7). The theory assumes C², but "Variants of the algorithms, however, require only first–order derivative information" (p. 1). The reason for requiring an absolute error below both ‖ĝ‖ and δ is explained: it is slightly stronger than the relative condition (4.2), but it frees ξ1 from the requirement ξ1 < 1, which is what makes it implementable (pp. 8, 16).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Theorem 4.5 (p. 12) gives lim inf(‖ĝ_k‖ + ‖C_k‖) = 0 and lim inf(‖W_k^T∇f_k‖ + ‖C_k‖) = 0. The proof reuses [10] with pred replaced throughout by pred(s^n, s_u). "Only a very few steps in the convergence analysis change" (p. 12). The changes are:
  1. ‖s_k‖ ≤ κ7 δ_k, and δ_{k+1} ≥ κ8 ‖s_k‖ on rejection. This follows from the trust-region constraints and (4.11) (p. 12).
  2. New consistency estimates. (4.18) gives |ared − pred − rpred| ≤ κ9 δ‖s‖ + κ10 ρ‖s‖³ + κ11 ρ‖s‖²‖C‖, and (4.19) gives ≤ κ12 ρ δ ‖s‖ (pp. 12–14). The gradient error enters as ξ1 δ ‖s_u‖ through (4.3) (4.24), p. 14. This is where "error ≤ const · δ" makes the extra term O(δ‖s‖).
  3. Combining with (4.10) gives |ared − pred| ≤ … + ξ0|pred|. On rejection this yields a positive constant 1 − ξ0 − η1, so "the analysis in [10] remains unchanged except for the fact that a different lower bound 1 − ξ0 − η1 ∈ (0,1) is used" (p. 14).

  Lemma 5.1 (p. 16) turns (4.3) into a truncation rule for the iterative adjoint solve: ‖e‖ ≤ min{ζ1‖C_u^T ẑ + d_u‖, ζ2 δ}. Both sides can be monitored during the iterations. Because ξ1 is unrestricted, no estimate of ‖C_u^T C_y^{-T}‖ is needed (p. 16). Remark 4.2 reads C_y^{-1} r^t as a "spacer" tangential step bounded by a constant times δ. This links to the spacer-step theory in Conn–Gould–Toint and in Conn–Vicente–Visweswariah [6] (p. 10). Remark 4.1 shows that the new gradient condition also gives lim inf for the unconstrained reduced problem (pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable technique: Absorb a controlled perturbation into the ratio test by *shifting the acceptance threshold*. If |perturbation| ≤ ξ0·pred, the old proof runs with η1 replaced by η1 + ξ0 (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: Write accuracy conditions as error ≤ ξ·min{‖computed gradient‖, δ} with an *unrestricted* constant. The condition is then checkable inside an inner Krylov loop without norm estimates (pp. 8, 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. §5 is an implementation discussion, not a numerical study (pp. 15–17). It covers:
  - quasi-normal steps by Cauchy-decrease Krylov methods, GMRES(l) under a positive-definiteness condition on the symmetric part of C_y, or a scaled-back inexact Newton step (p. 15);
  - the adjoint-solve truncation rule (p. 16);
  - a Steihaug–Toint CG safeguard for the case where the reduced Hessian is applied inexactly: compare m̂ at the first and final CG iterates and keep the better one (p. 16);
  - the residual bound for the y-solve (p. 17).

  The authors say §5 "is not meant to be comprehensive. Rather it is meant to support our claim" that the conditions are implementable (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The positioning has three parts:
  - **Nesting**: "In the absence of inexactness our global convergence theory is equal to that of Dennis, El–Alem, Maciel". If all iterates are feasible, the results relate to Carter [3] and Conn–Gould–Toint [5] on inexact gradients for unconstrained trust-region methods (pp. 1–2).
  - **Differentiation**: "Our analysis and our assumptions on inexactness are different from [23]". The authors' bounds, unlike those of Jäger–Sachs, avoid Lipschitz constants and derivative bounds (p. 2).
  - **Calibration**: §2 reviews the unconstrained inexact trust-region conditions (2.4), relative gradient error < (1 − η2)/2, and (2.5), function error ≤ η0·pred. The new conditions are then designed to reduce to them at feasible iterates (pp. 3–4, 7–8).

  **Self-critique**: the authors' own "initial and somewhat straight forward approach [20, 36]" used condition (4.14). It "often lead to very stringent accuracy requirements", and its penalty update "often lead to large increases in the penalty parameter", especially at nearly feasible iterates (pp. 10–11).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract states the extension, how the accuracy requirements adapt (to feasibility and optimality), how they are enforced (with information already available in matrix-free SQP), and the two special-case recoveries (p. 1). The design criteria are enumerated as "First… Secondly… Thirdly" (pp. 7–8). Hedging is moderate: "Our criterion also seems to be closely aligned with the SQP philosophy" (p. 10). The paper motivates by review. §2 on the black-box problem and §3 on the exact algorithm come before the inexact algorithm in §4, so that the reader can see "what can go wrong" (p. 4). The conclusion explains the narrow scope ("to enhance the clarity of our presentation", p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: Report your own earlier, more naive design and its specific failure (stringent accuracy, penalty blow-up near feasibility) as the motivation for the refined one (pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical and algorithmic: first-order global convergence of composite-step trust-region SQP with inexact derivatives and inexact linear solves, under accuracy rules that can be implemented (pp. 12, 17). The limits are stated by the authors:
  - one problem class and "a limited class of algorithms" (p. 17);
  - an extension to the affine-scaling interior-point trust-region SQP of S011 is "rather straight forward, although tedious", and TR95-18 already has the details under stronger assumptions (p. 17).

  The paper proves only first-order results (Theorem 4.5, p. 12). Second-order and local-rate analyses under inexactness are not addressed. This is an observation from reading, not stated by the authors. Future work: a broader range of problems and of global SQP algorithms (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The skeleton is kept (Algorithm 3.1 → 4.3). Only the *acceptance quantity* is redefined: the ratio uses pred(s^n, s_u), and rpred is controlled separately (pp. 9–12). Most of the proof carries over: "Only a very few steps in the convergence analysis change" (p. 12). The price is stated as a changed constant, 1 − ξ0 − η1 (p. 14).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (deterministic precursor). Exactness of derivatives and linear solves is relaxed to adaptive accuracy conditions tied to δ_k and to the predicted decrease ((4.3), (4.10), pp. 8, 10). Condition (4.3), error ≤ ξ min{‖ĝ‖, δ}, has the same "error ≤ const·δ" shape as the full-linearity requirement that Method 3 later asks to hold only with probability p. This link is an interpretation; the paper has no probabilistic content. The reviewed condition (2.5), function error ≤ η0·pred (pp. 3–4), is the deterministic form of Heuristic 3 ("control the estimated decrease, not every estimate").
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (pre-complexity era). The only result is lim inf first-order convergence (p. 12), with no iteration or solve counts.
- new-pattern candidate: **nest the known theories as special cases**. Exact information recovers Dennis–El-Alem–Maciel, and feasible iterates recover the unconstrained inexact-gradient theory (pp. 1–2). The same move appears in S011, p. 2.
- new-pattern candidate: **calibrate new conditions against the reduced (black-box) problem**. §2 reviews the black-box conditions. The new conditions are required to be "comparable" to them, and Remark 4.1 checks this in the reverse direction (pp. 3–4, 7–9).
- new-pattern candidate: **implementable accuracy conditions**. The conditions use only quantities already computed, with no Lipschitz constants and no norm estimates (pp. 2, 7–8, 16–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before designing conditions for a structured or constrained formulation, write down the known conditions for the equivalent unconstrained or black-box formulation. Require that yours reduce to them when the extra structure is trivial, for example at feasible iterates (pp. 3–4, 7–8).
- Split pred into a computable part and a residual part. Accept on the computable part, bound the residual by ξ0 times it, and run the old proof with the threshold moved from η1 to η1 + ξ0 (pp. 9–10, 14).
- Tie the allowed inexactness to the progress being made: a larger feasibility or optimality gain allows a cheaper inner solve (p. 10).
- Turn the accuracy condition into a stopping rule for the inner iterative solver that uses only monitored quantities (Lemma 5.1, p. 16).
- If the inner model is applied inexactly, keep the Cauchy (first CG) iterate as a fallback and return whichever of the first and final CG iterates has the lower model value (p. 16).
- List explicit design criteria for your conditions (weak far from the solution, comparable with known theory, implementable) and check each one (pp. 7–8).
