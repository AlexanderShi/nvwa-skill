# Paper cards · batch s02 (supplement)

Batch intent: Three supplement papers from 2017–2018, the end of the Coimbra period, each in a different genre. (a) An invited survey chapter: does the group state Methods 1–5 in its own voice, and how does it place rival schools? (b) A short complexity paper that carries the probabilistic-descent proof technology from direct search over to trust-region methods with probabilistic models: how is a proof transferred between companion algorithm classes, and where does the transfer need repair? (c) An application paper that uses the group's own solver (PSwarm) on an engineering problem: what does Method 5 look like when the solver is the tool and not the contribution?
Focus dimensions: supplement short cards: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line), D8, plus one line each for D6/D7 because the digest asks for them. Extra weight on D4 in S027 (proof transfer) and on D1/D5 in S036 (problem reduction and experimental protocol).
Material roles: S038 supplement (survey chapter, MOS-SIAM book 2017) · S027 supplement (IMA J. Numer. Anal. 2018) · S036 supplement (Int. J. Adv. Manuf. Technol. 2018). All three texts are short, and all three were read in full (bibliographies skimmed).

Page references use the `[[page N]]` markers of the extracted text files. In all three files these match the printed page numbers. All three files are preprints: S038 is dated March 14, 2017, S027 June 29, 2017, S036 May 16, 2018 (p. 1 of each). Extraction caveats are given per card.

### S038 · Methodologies and software for derivative-free optimization (Advances and Trends in Optimization with Engineering Applications (MOS-SIAM Series on Optimization), SIAM 2017, DOI 10.1137/1.9781611974683.ch37)
**Role**: supplement (survey chapter) · **Coauthors**: A. L. Custódio (FCT-UNL), K. Scheinberg (Lehigh). The file's author order is Custódio, Scheinberg, Vicente (p. 1); the batch list gives Custódio, Vicente, Scheinberg · **Position in trajectory**: a map of the field, written late in the 2014–2019 probabilistic period. The chapter restates the group's own lines inside a neutral survey: SID-PSM, DMS, PSwarm, the merit-function approach, probabilistic models and descent, and worst-case complexity (WCC). **Read**: the whole text, pp. 1–13 (p. 14 is blank); the bibliography, pp. 15–21, was scanned for self-citations and software entries. The genre has no algorithm statement, no theorem, no experiment and no conclusion section, so those supplement items are marked not applicable. The extraction is clean apart from broken hyphenation.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is the reader's: which DFO method fits the problem at hand? The stated purpose is "an overview of the main classes of state-of-the-art DFO methods, with a focus on the underlying ideas and on the respective classes of problems to which these methods are applicable" (p. 2). The entry is a restructuring of the authors' own 2009 book [53]. The chapter is organised by problem features rather than by method classes, because that structure "directs the reader to the appropriate DFO algorithm suited for a given problem at hand" (p. 2). The motivating setting is engineering functions computed by simulation: expensive, possibly noisy, and so poorly suited to finite-difference derivatives (p. 2).
- **D2 Organising idea**: The sections follow problem features (p. 2):
  - unconstrained problems, split into smooth, nonsmooth and noisy (§37.2.1–37.2.3);
  - worst-case complexity, then probabilistic models and descent (§37.2.4–37.2.5);
  - bound and linear constraints (§37.3);
  - nonlinear constraints, split into unrelaxable, relaxable, and both (§37.4);
  - extensions: global optimization, the search step, parallelism, integer variables, multiobjective problems (§37.5).

  Within §37.2–37.3 the paragraph labels classify methods by where the sample points go: "Sampling and modeling", "Sampling using simplex sets", "Sampling using positive spanning sets", "Sampling along directions" (pp. 2–5, 9–10). Even for direct search, "the geometry of the sample sets continues to play a crucial role" (p. 4). Almost every method paragraph ends with named solvers:
  - DFO, NEWUOA, BC-DFO, ORBIT (p. 4);
  - DFL, HOPSPACK, NOMAD, SID-PSM (p. 6);
  - NOMAD with LTMADS/ORTHOMADS, and IFFCO (p. 7);
  - HOPSPACK, PSwarm (p. 9);
  - BOBYQA, LINCOA, LCOBYQA (p. 10);
  - DFL (p. 11); COBYLA, DFO, NOWPAC (p. 12);
  - DIRECT, MCS, GLODS (p. 12); NOMAD, DMS (p. 13).

  The bibliography opens with 12 software URLs, refs [1]–[12] (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The key objects get compact definitions:
  - fully linear and fully quadratic models, with error bounds in powers of Δ (p. 3);
  - positive spanning set, and forcing function with ρ(t)/t → 0 (p. 5);
  - cosine measure bounded away from zero (p. 5);
  - relaxable, unrelaxable and hidden constraints (pp. 10–11);
  - O(A), with the dependence on n written out "whenever appropriate" (p. 2).

  "Global convergence" means convergence to some form of stationarity from any starting point (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief; mechanisms reported, not proved)**:
  - Sufficient decrease forces unsuccessful iterations. With f bounded below, a subsequence of step sizes then goes to zero. Simple decrease instead needs integer lattices to keep trial points apart (p. 5).
  - Under sufficient decrease "the computation of new points is free of rules", so poll directions may be drawn at random on the unit sphere (RdDS). MADS instead keeps lattices and uses asymptotically dense directions (p. 6).
  - Complete polling gives convergence of the whole gradient sequence (p. 6).
  - Nonsmooth f: dense directions plus Clarke generalized derivatives, or Rockafellar's for discontinuous f (p. 6).
  - Model-based methods: the criticality step and self-correcting geometry (pp. 3–4).
  - Complexity, summarised in one page (p. 8):
    - direct search: O(ε⁻²) iterations and O(n²ε⁻²) evaluations, next to the gradient method's bound;
    - adaptive cubic overestimation with finite differences: O(n²ε^(−3/2));
    - smoothing: O((−log ε)ε⁻³) iterations and O(n³(−log ε)ε⁻³) evaluations;
    - convex case: O(ε⁻¹); r-linear under strong convexity;
    - the n² factor is "approximately optimal".
  - Probabilistic descent: O(nmε⁻²) evaluations with m random directions (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (survey).
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**: The chapter treats rival schools even-handedly:
  - NOMAD is "a reference" for nonsmooth direct search (p. 7);
  - the progressive barrier is "The first general approach" to mixed relaxable/unrelaxable constraints, and the authors' merit-function method [79] is "An alternative" (p. 12);
  - open disputes are given with both sides: geometry-free numerics [70] vs the counterexample [123] (pp. 3–4), and the Nelder–Mead failure example [110] (p. 5).

  24 of the 133 references have Vicente as a coauthor: [18], [19], [30], [31], [50]–[53], [56], [58]–[60], [66]–[68], [73], [74], [77], [79]–[81], [128]–[130] (pp. 15–21).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: One or two sentences per method, definitions in inline parentheses, no theorems, headings named after problem features, and the solver named in the last sentence of a paragraph (pp. 2–13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a survey plus a software map (pp. 2, 15). The stated limit: "Only short descriptions of the methods and algorithms will be given" (p. 2). Gaps are named in passing. For example, nonlinear constraints in model-based trust-region DFO have been handled "mostly in implementations and in a relaxable mode" (p. 12). There is no conclusion or future-work section; the text ends with §37.5 (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence, stated in the group's survey voice:
  - the search step "is optional and does not interfere" with global convergence, and it hosts surrogates and global heuristics [128, 21] (p. 13);
  - a modified CMA-ES "is capable of globally converging to stationary points" [66] (p. 12);
  - the lattice vs sufficient-decrease contrast (pp. 5–6) states, in the survey's voice, Method 1's claim that sufficient decrease rather than a mesh is the glue (see the *Different from standard practice* paragraph of Method 1 in SKILL.md).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (stated). Function evaluations are counted separately from iterations, with the n-factor, and the bounds are set next to the gradient method's (p. 8). The n² factor is called approximately optimal [68] (p. 8).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (restated). Probabilistic models and probabilistic descent are covered, and the number of random directions "can go down to two" (pp. 8–9).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence:
  - relaxable constraints go into a merit function and unrelaxable ones into an RdDS extreme barrier; a constraint can move from relaxable to unrelaxable once it is satisfied (p. 12);
  - Pareto dominance generalizes direct search "without aggregation" (p. 13);
  - smoothing handles nonsmooth f (pp. 7–8).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. Every method class is paired with released software, and the title puts "Software" next to "Methodologies" (pp. 4–13, p. 15).
- Research heuristic 2 ✅, the geometry story (pp. 3–4, p. 8):
  - numerics without geometry control [70];
  - the counterexample and a minimal repair, geometry steps only when the model gradient is small [123];
  - the group's continuation: sparse and probabilistic models [30, 31].
- Workflow A ✅. The chapter's structure is itself a problem-intake → method routing table (p. 2).
- Existing candidate *state where you lose / credit priority in the text* ✅. The rival progressive barrier is credited as first, and the authors' own method is presented as the alternative (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: *survey as a problem-feature routing table*. Organize by what the user knows about f and the constraints, and end each paragraph with a solver (p. 2, pp. 4–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D7] In a survey, organise the sections by the reader's problem features (regularity, noise, constraint type), not by method families. Close each method paragraph with a named, linked solver (p. 2, pp. 4–13, p. 15).
- [D6] Tell a methodological dispute in three beats: numerical challenge, then counterexample, then the minimal requirement that survives (geometry control only when the model gradient is small) (pp. 3–4).
- [D4] Summarise complexity with iterations and evaluations as separate numbers, the n-factor explicit, and the gradient method's bound in the same paragraph (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "In DFO it becomes also important to measure the effort in terms of the number of function evaluations" (p. 8)
- "The search step is optional and does not interfere in the global convergence properties of the underlying methods." (p. 13)

### S027 · Complexity and global rates of trust-region methods based on probabilistic models (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2018, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drx043)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Gratton (Toulouse, IRIT), C. W. Royer (Wisconsin–Madison; supported by a Toulouse doctoral grant), Z. Zhang (Hong Kong PolyU) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This paper completes the S014 → S019 chain. S014 (Bandeira–Scheinberg–Vicente) proved almost-sure convergence for trust-region methods with probabilistic models. S019 (GRVZ 2015) built the counting + Chernoff technique for direct search and sketched the trust-region case in its §6. This paper carries out that sketch and adds the second-order case and results in expectation (pp. 2–3). **Read**: the whole text, pp. 1–17; references skimmed, pp. 17–18. **Extraction caveat**: the ε glyph is dropped throughout (O(ε⁻²) is extracted as O( followed by −2 on the next line), and the norm bars ‖·‖ are extracted as the letter k. Bounds were read from the surrounding structure.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an open item in the group's own programme: "The main purpose of this paper is to establish (with overwhelmingly high probability) the rate" at which these methods drive the criticality measures to zero (p. 2). The motivation is told as a chain (p. 2):
  - deterministic sampling geometry [9, 10, 12];
  - numerics showing that trust regions tolerate models built without strict geometry control [15], though a criticality step remains necessary [25];
  - fully quadratic models cost (n+1)(n+2)/2 evaluations, whereas ℓ1-recovery random models are fully quadratic with high probability from fewer points [1];
  - hence the probabilistic analysis of [2].

  The authors say openly that the road map already existed in [19, §6]. They claim three things beyond it: resolving the "delicate issues" of carrying it out, results in expectation, and the second-order case (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 2.1 is "A simple first-order derivative-free trust-region framework" (p. 5):
  - the step satisfies a fraction of Cauchy decrease (2.1), and ρ_k ≥ η₁ accepts it;
  - on acceptance the radius grows to min{γ₂δ_k, δ_max} if ‖g_k‖ ≥ η₂δ_k, and shrinks to γ₁δ_k otherwise;
  - on rejection the radius shrinks (p. 5).

  The per-iteration rule ‖g_k‖ ≥ η₂δ_k replaces a separate criticality step, "Following [2]" (p. 5). The stated change from [2] is two factors γ₁, γ₂ instead of γ and its inverse, because these parameters are "intimately connected" to the required probability (p. 5). Cross-check against the S014 text: this update is S014's practical Algorithm 4.1 with η₃ = η₂ (S014 p. 14). It is not S014's Algorithm 3.1, which rejects such steps (S014 p. 8). Algorithm 3.1 keeps the same structure. It replaces ‖g_k‖ with σ^m(x_k) = max{‖g_k‖, −τ_k} and requires a step with Cauchy decrease plus eigendecrease (3.1) (pp. 12–13). The model-building step is not specified: the models are random objects produced at each iteration (p. 3), and Gaussian linear interpolation or regression are given as examples (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f ∈ C¹ with Lipschitz gradient (Assumption 2.3) and bounded below (Assumption 2.4) (p. 5);
  - uniformly bounded model Hessians (Assumption 2.1) and fraction of Cauchy decrease (Assumption 2.2) (p. 4);
  - models (p)-probabilistically fully linear, i.e. P(S_k | M₀,…,M_{k−1}) ≥ p (Definition 2.1, p. 4), with p > p₀ = ln γ₁ / ln(γ₁/γ₂) (eq. (2.4), p. 8).

  One simplification is admitted: an enlarged level set would suffice, "but we skip this detail for keeping the presentation simple" (p. 5). The paper gives two ways to satisfy p > p₀. Raise the model generator's probability, or, if that probability is fixed, choose γ₁, γ₂ so that p₀ falls below it (p. 8). For second order: C² with Lipschitz Hessian (Assumption 3.1), (p)-probabilistically fully quadratic models (Definition 3.1), and a Cauchy + eigendecrease step (Assumption 3.2) (p. 12). δ_max enters only the second-order analysis (p. 5). Function values are exact; stochastic f is left as an open question (pp. 16–17).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The S019 skeleton is transplanted, with one repair.
  1. Lemma 2.1: if the model is fully linear and δ_k < κ‖∇f(x_k)‖, the step is taken *and* δ is increased (p. 6). The lemma is stated for the true gradient, "in alignment to what is needed to establish complexity bounds" (p. 5).
  2. **The repair**, Lemma 2.2: Σδ_k² ≤ β for every realization (pp. 6–7). The counting set is K = {ρ_k ≥ η₁ and ‖g_k‖ ≥ η₂δ_k}, because only accepted-*and*-enlarged iterations are guaranteed a decrease of ηδ_k². Between consecutive elements of K the radius decays geometrically. Remark 2.1 explains why K is needed: in direct search every success already yields an α_k²-order decrease, but a trust-region step may be taken while the radius shrinks (p. 6). The authors point out that such summability is not standard for derivative-based trust regions (p. 6), and that it strengthens [2]'s lim δ_k = 0 (p. 7).
  3. Lemma 2.3 counts the fully linear iterations: Σz_l ≤ β/(min{δ₀/γ₂, κ‖∇f(x̃_k)‖})² + p₀k, via z_l ≤ (1−v_l) + v_l y_l. The second half of the proof is "verbatim" from [19] (p. 8).
  4. Lemma 2.4 is a Chernoff lower tail for ΣZ_l, exp(−(p−λ)²k/(2p)). It is where the conditioning on the past enters (p. 9). Setting λ = (p+p₀)/2 turns the event inclusion (2.8) into (2.11) (p. 9).
  5. Results:
     - Theorem 2.1: min‖∇f‖ ≤ O(1/√k) with probability ≥ 1 − exp(−(p−p₀)²k/(8p)) (p. 9);
     - Theorem 2.2: O(ε⁻²) iterations with overwhelmingly high probability (p. 10);
     - Theorem 2.3: E(K) ≤ c₁ε⁻² + 1/(1−exp(−c₂)), obtained from E(K) = Σ_k P(K > k) (pp. 10–11).

     The dominant term, O((p−p₀)⁻¹κ⁻²ε⁻²) in (2.13), matches Cartis–Scheinberg's line-search result with p₀ = 1/2 (p. 11).
  6. Second order: cube summability Σδ_k³ ≤ β₂nd (Lemma 3.3, p. 14); rate 1/∛k (Theorem 3.1, p. 15); O(ε⁻³) iterations (Theorem 3.2, p. 15); a bound in expectation (Theorem 3.3, pp. 15–16).
  7. Function evaluations:
     - with deterministic fully linear models (p = 1), the deterministic O(nε⁻²) iterations and O(n²ε⁻²) evaluations are recovered, up to a factor involving p₀ (p. 11);
     - random linear models with p > p₀ independent of n give the same bounds (p. 11);
     - for tridiagonal Hessians, sparse random quadratic models built from O(n log⁴ n) points give O((n log n)⁴ε⁻³) evaluations, against O(n⁵ε⁻³) deterministically (p. 16, p. 3).
  8. Almost-sure convergence comes out as a corollary: P(inf_k ‖∇f(X_k)‖ = 0) = 1 follows from the complexity theory (§2.5, p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. There is no numerical section.
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**: The results are set against the deterministic trust-region bounds: O(ε⁻²); O(ε⁻¹) convex; O(−log ε) strongly convex; O(ε⁻¹·⁵) (Curtis et al.); O(ε⁻³) second order; and the DFO bound O(κ_d⁻²ε⁻²) (p. 3). §4 criticises stochastic trust-region approaches ([26], [6], [22], [3]) for guarantees that rest on parameter choices depending on unknown constants, or on "unverifiable assumptions" (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: The abstract concedes two things: the results are "essentially the same as" the deterministic ones, and the derivation "follows closely" the direct-search study (p. 1). A remark and a footnote mark exactly where the transplant differs (Remark 2.1, p. 6; footnote 2, p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory: global rates and WCC bounds for trust-region methods based on probabilistic models, first and second order, with high probability and in expectation (pp. 1–3). The limits:
  - the second-order case has no lim-type result; the paper notes "the absence of a rigorous proof" (p. 15);
  - the setting is smooth and unconstrained (p. 1), with exact function values; stochastic f is left open (p. 16).

  Future work: stochastic f with convergence with probability one and rates with overwhelmingly high probability, "without unverifiable assumptions on algorithmic parameters"; better rates for convex and strongly convex f (pp. 16–17).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence:
  - step 3 is followed literally: the threshold p₀ = ln γ₁ / ln(γ₁/γ₂) is derived from the radius update factors, and the practical rule is to raise p or to retune γ₁, γ₂ (p. 5, p. 8);
  - step 4 is followed: convergence with probability one and complexity with overwhelmingly high probability (pp. 9–11).

  Precision for the SKILL.md evidence line: S014 (2014) gave convergence only, and the rates for probabilistic trust regions are in this paper (and sketched in S019 §6).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. Iteration and evaluation bounds come with their n-dependence and are compared with the deterministic ones (p. 3, p. 11, p. 16). The sparse-Hessian case shows where randomization improves the n-order (p. 16).
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (genre). The paper is pure theory: no numerics, no code (whole text).
- Existing candidate *two-layer proof: realization-wise count + conditional Chernoff* ✅. This is the second paper to use it, now for trust regions (pp. 6–11).
- Existing candidate *name where the proof technology breaks* ✅. Remark 2.1 names the break for trust-region success (p. 6), and p. 15 states that no rigorous second-order lim-type proof exists.
- New-pattern candidate: *port a proof to the companion algorithm class via a role dictionary*. δ_k plays the step size and K plays the successful iterations (pp. 7–8). Only the lemma where the dictionary fails is re-proved (Lemma 2.2, pp. 6–7). The abstract names the source ("follows closely", p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Transfer a proof between companion methods with an explicit role dictionary (radius ↔ step size; accepted-and-enlarged ↔ successful). Re-prove only the lemma where the analogy breaks, and flag it in a remark (Remark 2.1, pp. 6–8).
- [D4] Choose the counting set to be the iterations that provably yield order-δ² decrease, not all accepted iterations. Then show the radius decays geometrically between two such iterations; this gives Σδ² < ∞ for every realization (pp. 6–7).
- [D2] Decouple the expansion and contraction factors, so that the probability threshold p₀ = ln γ₁ / ln(γ₁/γ₂) becomes a design knob that can be tuned to a fixed model-generation probability (p. 5, p. 8).
- [D4] Get a bound in expectation from a tail bound: write E(K) = Σ_k P(K > k), split the sum at c₁ε⁻², and sum the exponential tail (pp. 10–11).
- [D8] Turn an iteration bound into an evaluation bound for one concrete random model generator, and compare the power of n with the deterministic one ((n log n)⁴ vs n⁵, p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "Although the road map for our paper was described in [19, Section 6], its actual concretization poses a few delicate issues." (p. 3)
- "these parameters are intimately connected to the minimum probability with which the models are required to be fully linear." (p. 5)

### S036 · On the optimal object orientation in additive manufacturing (The International Journal of Advanced Manufacturing Technology 2018, DOI 10.1007/s00170-018-2218-0)
**Role**: supplement (application) · **Coauthors**: S. Pereira, A. I. F. Vaz (ALGORITMI, Univ. Minho) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: A late-Coimbra application paper from the FIBR3D project on hybrid additive manufacturing of fibre-reinforced composites (Portugal 2020 / ERDF, p. 13). It uses PSwarm, the Vaz–Vicente solver of 2007/2009, as a black box (refs [38, 39], p. 16). **Read**: the whole text, pp. 1–12, and the acknowledgments on p. 13; references skimmed, pp. 13–16. **Extraction caveat**: the fraction layout in eqs. (3)–(5) and in the SEc/SAc definitions is garbled, and Figure 4 (p. 10) is axis-label noise. Formula details come from the prose.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem comes from the application. Additive manufacturing has four planning stages: orientation, supports, slicing and path planning. The paper takes the first three (p. 2). The quality problems are the staircase (stair-stepping) effect and support structures (p. 2, p. 4). Prior work posed orientation as a multi-objective problem:
  - Cheng et al.'s eight criteria (p. 3);
  - Jibin's tri-objective model (staircase effect, supports, building time), solved with a genetic algorithm (pp. 5–6).

  The paper's move: for symmetric objects a single objective suffices, and this "will then allow the application of a state-of-the-art solver from derivative-free global optimization" (p. 6). The contribution sentence names the use of a state-of-the-art solver to optimise surface quality through the staircase effect and the need for supports (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & model**: The model is built from the mesh geometry of STL triangles (p. 2):
  - the cusp height relation cos β = Hc/t, eq. (1) (p. 5);
  - staircase effect SE: a sum over mesh triangles of t-scaled |d·n_j|A_j terms, eq. (3);
  - support area SA = Σ A_j|d·n_j| over down-facing facets, eqs. (4)–(5);
  - building time BT: the object's height along the slicing direction d, eq. (6) (p. 6).

  The modified SEc and SAc drop triangles with |d·n_j| = 1 and triangles on the printing-table base (p. 7). The decision variable is θ = (θx, θy) ∈ [0,180]², two rotation angles, which is equivalent to choosing d (eq. (7), p. 7). The result is a two-variable, bound-constrained, nondifferentiable problem with many local minimizers (p. 3, p. 11). The solver is PSwarm (p. 9). There is no new algorithm.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - constant layer thickness, as in the applications the authors target (p. 6);
  - every down-facing facet needs support, "without loss of generality", although many processes can print overhangs near 45 degrees (p. 6);
  - symmetry (regularity): the facets with d·n_j > 0 match those with d·n_j < 0 in number and area, which makes SE and SA non-conflicting (p. 7);
  - BT is set aside because eq. (6) is "not appropriate for the type of objects of interest to us" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: There is no theorem. The only argument is structural: under the facet symmetry, SE and SA do not conflict (p. 7). The paper checks this numerically by minimising each objective separately (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments (one line)**:
  - Objects: three (Table 1, p. 9; p. 7). Humanoid has 96 triangles; it is a toy and is not symmetric. T-Duct (19,686 triangles) and Duct (28,656) come from aerospace.
  - Runs: PSwarm on SE, SA and BT separately. Reported are the final θ, f, CPU time, iterations (36–88) and evaluations (357–644) (Tables 2–4, p. 9). Landscapes over the whole θ box are plotted (Fig. 4, p. 10).
  - Outcome: SE and SA give the same orientation for Duct and equivalent ones for T-Duct (they differ by 180° about y). They give different orientations for the asymmetric Humanoid (p. 11).
  - Protocol gaps: no baseline solver and no repeated runs of the stochastic solver. The need for global optimization is argued from the landscapes, not from a local-solver comparison (pp. 9–11).
- **D6/D7 (one line)**: The literature is reviewed stage by stage, descriptively (pp. 3–6), and the paper positions itself against multi-objective genetic-algorithm models [12], [29] (p. 6). The conclusion claims "effectiveness and robustness" from three objects (p. 12). BT is declared out of scope (p. 7) but tabulated anyway "for a matter of completeness" (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is application modelling plus the use of an existing solver (p. 2, p. 12). The limits are stated concretely: constant layer thickness and some symmetry are required; the object geometry is not modified; the orientation does not guarantee that the staircase effect or supports disappear (p. 12). Future work:
  - integration into a 5-axis printer;
  - real aerospace parts;
  - optimising process parameters such as layer thickness and the cost of allowable overhangs (p. 12);
  - an accurate building-time measure that uses printer characteristics (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The paper supplies Method 5's step 6, one real application (aerospace parts, p. 7), using the group's released solver (p. 9; refs p. 16). The benchmarking half is missing: no collection, no baseline, no profiles, no repeated runs (pp. 9–11). In this application paper the solver is used, not profiled.
- Method 1 ✅ downstream use only. PSwarm, a Method-1 product with particle swarm in the search step, is the tool chosen for a nonsmooth, multimodal, bound-constrained problem (p. 7, p. 9). No new wrapper is built.
- Method 4 / Research heuristic 4 ⚠ variant. The paper avoids aggregation, as the heuristic asks, but it also avoids computing a front. It argues non-conflict under symmetry and solves each objective separately. For the asymmetric Humanoid, where the objectives do conflict, it reports two separate minimisers and no Pareto front (p. 7, pp. 11–12). DMS is not used, although it was available to the group (S038 p. 13).
- Taste mark 6 (anchored in an application) ✅: aerospace ducts (p. 7, p. 12).
- Existing candidate *reformulate to reuse existing DFO solvers* ✅. This is the second paper after S022: the reduction to one objective is justified by the solver it unlocks (p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Before choosing a multiobjective method, look for a structural property under which the objectives share minimisers (here facet symmetry). Then check it by minimising each objective separately and comparing the minimisers modulo the known symmetries of the decision space (the 180° rotation about y) (p. 7, p. 11).
- [D5] Include one instance that deliberately violates the structural assumption (the asymmetric Humanoid) as a negative control. It shows where the simplification stops holding (pp. 11–12).
