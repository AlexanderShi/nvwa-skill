### S110 · Non-monotone direct-search methods for deterministic and stochastic derivative-free optimization (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2609.11567)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Ding (Lehigh ISE) and Trang H. Tran (Emory; the work was done while Tran was a Lehigh postdoc) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the latest paper in the Lehigh stochastic-DFO line. It combines four earlier pieces:
- probabilistic descent (GRVZ 2015, ref. [20]; card S019);
- the stopping-time framework of Blanchet et al. as generalized by Ding–Rinaldi–Vicente (ref. [12]; card S102);
- the tail-bound language of Rinaldi–Vicente–Zeffiro (ref. [28]; card S067);
- test problems from Giovannelli–Sohab–Vicente (ref. [17]; card S079) and Gratton–Royer–Vicente (ref. [19]).

These are cited on pp. 2, 13, 15, 19 and 23–24. **Text read**: pp. 1–29 in full, including Appendix A (the alternative merit function, pp. 25–27), Appendix B (sample size, p. 28) and Appendix C (problem lists, p. 29).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is unconstrained, L-smooth and bounded below. It is accessed through a zeroth-order oracle f(x) or a stochastic oracle F(x, ξ) (pp. 1–2). The entry is a phenomenon imported from line search: enforcing monotone decrease "may considerably slow" convergence in narrow curved valleys. Grippo–Lampariello–Lucidi's max-over-M line search [21] is the classical remedy (p. 2). The gap is stated at two levels. Non-monotone *derivative-free* methods [13, 14, 15, 22] converge globally, but "their iteration complexity results have yet to be established" (p. 3). And no one has analysed the max-M rule for direct search, or any non-monotone method in stochastic DFO (p. 4). The authors found a concurrent competitor while finishing the paper: Garmanjani's NoMoDS [16], with a summable slack η_k. They separate it from their work on practical grounds: the η_k need tuning and depend on the scale of f (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when a concurrent paper appears late, separate your contribution by a *usability* property of the rule (here, no user-specified sequence whose values depend on the scale of f) as well as by the theorem (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper replaces the sufficient-decrease test f(x_k + δ_k d_k) ≤ f(x_k) − cδ_k² with a test against the max over the current iterate and the M − 1 most recent *successful* iterates, max{f(x^{M−1}_{succ,k}), …, f(x^1_{succ,k}), f(x_k)} − cδ_k² (p. 4). Nothing else changes: the poll, the expansion by γ on success and the contraction by θ on failure are the same (Algorithms 1–3, pp. 6, 10, 14). There are three instantiations:
  - Algorithm 1 polls a complete positive spanning set D with cosine measure 1/√n, e.g. {±e_i} (p. 6).
  - Algorithm 2 polls one direction drawn uniformly from the unit sphere. The "practical version" polls {D_k, −D_k} (p. 10).
  - Algorithm 3 replaces every value in the test by an independent stochastic estimate. This means resampling the M − 1 stored iterates, x_k and the candidate at every iteration (p. 14).

  The memory holds *distinct* (successful) iterates, not all iterations. The experiments found the two choices "broadly comparable" and omit the comparison (p. 20). The real design work is in the analysis object, not the algorithm. §2 is devoted to merit-function design before any algorithm is stated (pp. 4–6).
  - Transferable technique: when you import a known relaxation (here max-M from line search) into a new skeleton, change only the acceptance test and spend the paper on the Lyapunov function that makes the old theory go through (pp. 4–6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Smoothness*: f is bounded below (A3.1) and ∇f is L-Lipschitz (A3.2) (pp. 6–7).
  - *Directions*: p-probabilistically κ-descent directions, conditioned on the σ-algebra F_k of past directions (A3.3, p. 11). The paper cites [20] for κ ≥ 1/(7√n) "for a sufficiently large probability" (p. 11).
  - *Stochastic oracle* (Assumption 4.1, p. 15): for ε_k = G_k − H_k, the error of the estimated max-M decrease (pp. 14–15), P(ε_k ≥ 0 | F_{k+1/2}) ≥ p0 and the one-sided tail P(ε_k ≥ b | F_{k+1/2}) ≤ β∆_k²/b for all b > 0. The paper stresses that the condition is on the estimated *decrease*, not on each function value (p. 15). It also says p0 = 1/2 is attained for symmetric errors and that the analysis "does not require a lower bound on p0" (p. 15).
  - *Growth conditions*: p ln γ + (1 − p) ln θ > 0 is assumed in Theorem 3.9 (p. 13). In Theorem 4.3 it becomes pp0 ln γ + (1 − pp0) ln θ > 0, with β fixed to ½(η − c_{M−1})(1 − θ²) (p. 18).
  - *Sample size*: Appendix B assumes sub-Gaussian averaging and derives N_k ≥ σ²(M+1)²/(β²∆_k⁴), i.e. O(∆_k⁻⁴). The paper calls this order "standard" and cites [28] (pp. 15, 28).
  - Reader's observation (not in the paper), on the growth condition for a single direction. A single uniformly random direction has P(cm(D, −∇f) ≥ κ) < 1/2 for any κ > 0, by symmetry. The condition p ln γ + (1 − p) ln θ > 0 can then hold only if γθ > 1. Card S019 records the matching threshold p0 = ln θ / ln(θ/γ) = 1/2 for γ = 2, θ = 1/2. The experiments use γ = 2, θ = 0.5, so γθ = 1 (p. 19), and poll the ±d variant (p. 19), which is not the analysed Algorithm 2 (p. 10). The paper does not point this out.
  - Reader's observation (not in the paper), on p0. The claim that no lower bound on p0 is needed (p. 15) sits next to the Theorem 4.3 condition (p. 18), which is itself a lower bound: pp0 > ln(1/θ)/ln(γ/θ). With symmetric errors (p0 = 1/2) and one direction (p < 1/2), this requires γ > θ⁻³.
  - Transferable technique: when a new test compares against several stored values, place the probabilistic accuracy requirement on the single scalar the test uses (G_k versus H_k), not on each stored estimate (pp. 14–15).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - *Why the obvious merit function fails* (p. 5). The paper computes the failure before proposing a fix. With Φ̂_k = max{…} − f* + ηδ_k², a successful iteration whose oldest value is not the max makes Φ̂ *increase* by at least η(γ² − 1)δ_k², because the step expands (p. 5).
  - *The fix: ordered corrections* (2.1, p. 5). Φ_k = max{f(x^{M−1}_{succ,k}) − c_{M−1}δ_k², …, f(x^1_{succ,k}) − c_1δ_k², f(x_k)} − f* + ηδ_k², with 0 < c_1 < … < c_{M−1} < η. The oldest stored value receives the largest correction. On success the window shifts. Each gap c_{i+1}γ² − c_i must be positive, and η must dominate on failure (p. 5).
  - *Complete poll* (§3.1). Lemma 3.1 shows that ∆ below δ_ϵ forces success before T_ϵ. The extraction garbles δ_ϵ; reconstructed from the proof, it is 2ϵ/(√n(L+2c)) (p. 7). Lemma 3.2 gives the step-size floor δ_k ≥ θδ_ϵ (p. 7). The merit function φ_k then *freezes* the corrections at c_iθ²δ_ϵ², so it changes only on successes (p. 8). With c_i = (i/M)c, each success decreases φ by (c/M)θ²δ_ϵ² (Lemma 3.4, pp. 8–9). Counting successes and applying Lemma 3.3 (k versus N_succ) gives Theorem 3.5 (p. 9): T_ϵ ≤ ln(δ_0√n(L+2c)/(2θϵ))/ln(1/θ) + [ln(γ/θ)/ln(1/θ)]·nM(L+2c)²(f(x_0) − f*)/(4cθ²ϵ²). This is O(nϵ⁻²) iterations, with a factor M (pp. 9–10, 14).
  - *Probabilistic descent* (§3.2). The constants are c_i = (c/γ²)(1 − γ^{−2i})/(1 − γ^{−2M}) (Lemma 3.7, p. 11). Lemma 3.8 gives the deterministic decrease Φ_k − Φ_{k+1} ≥ ν∆_k² on *every* iteration, with ν ∝ γ^{−2M} (p. 12). The algebra uses max(a, x) − max(a, y) ≥ x − y and a − max(b, c) = min(a − b, a − c) (p. 13).
  - Reader's check (not in the paper). With these c_i, every entry of the min in Lemma 3.7 equals c(1 − γ⁻²)/(1 − γ^{−2M}): c_{i+1}γ² − c_i, c_1γ² and c − c_{M−1} are all this value. Likewise c_i = (i/M)c equalizes the gaps in Lemma 3.4. The constants are chosen to *equalize the gaps* in the min.
  - *Stopping-time step* (p. 13). The paper imports the renewal–reward framework of Blanchet et al. [5] and the general-(p, γ, θ) version of Ding et al. [12]. It needs two conditions: success with probability ≥ p when ∆_k ≤ δ_ϵ, plus the growth condition; and E[Φ_k − Φ_{k+1} | F_k] ≥ ν∆_k². Theorem 3.9 then gives E[T_ϵ − 1] ≤ [ln γ/(p ln γ + (1 − p) ln θ)]·Φ_0(L + 2c)²/(4νθ²κ²ϵ²) (p. 13).
  - *Stochastic case* (§4). Lemma 4.1 multiplies the direction probability p by the oracle probability p0 through the tower property over F_{k+1/2}, the σ-algebra up to D_k (pp. 15–16). Lemma 4.2 splits on the F_{k+1/2}-measurable true decrease H_k (pp. 16–18). If H_k is large, both outcomes decrease Φ. If H_k is small, a false acceptance is possible. Its probability is capped by the tail bound, with β set to half the unsuccessful-iteration decrease, so the expected loss is at most β∆_k², leaving ν = ½(η − c_{M−1})(1 − θ²) (pp. 16–18). Theorem 4.3 is Theorem 3.9 with pp0 in place of p (p. 18).
  - *Rejected alternative* (App. A, pp. 25–27). Ψ_k = Σ_{i=0}^{M−1} max{window i} − Mf* + η∆_k² needs no corrections. It uses a monotonicity lemma for shifted windows (Lemma A.1) and a step-size ratio bound γ^{2M−2} (Lemma A.2). Its decrease, however, depends on the last M acceptance tests, which is why §4 does not use it (pp. 14, 26).
  - *Price stated*: the probabilistic bounds carry a γ^{2M} factor, while the complete poll is linear in M. The authors call the geometric factor an artefact of their coefficients and leave sharper ones "open" (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: build a Lyapunov function for a max-over-memory test by subtracting *ordered* multiples of the squared step from the stored values, and choose the multiples so that all gap terms in the resulting min are equal (pp. 5, 8–12).
  - Transferable technique: first write out the natural merit function and show in one display that it increases on a successful step. That display justifies the more complicated one (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: when the step size has a deterministic floor (complete poll), freeze the merit function's step term at that floor, so that it is constant on failures and you only count successes (pp. 7–9).
  - Transferable technique: prefer a merit function whose decrease depends only on the *current* acceptance test. A function whose decrease depends on M past tests cannot absorb per-iteration stochastic errors (pp. 4, 14).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Problem sets*: two CUTEst sets. Set 1 has 38 problems with n ∈ {5, 10, 100}, "suggested by" [17] (card S079). Set 2 has 53 problems with n from 2 to 50, selected because a second-order method of [2] detected negative curvature on each (pp. 18–19). Both lists are in Appendix C (p. 29).
  - *Protocol*: Moré–Wild performance profiles; a failure is assigned twice the maximum ratio (p. 19). Parameters are γ = 2, θ = 0.5 and c = 1, with M ∈ {2, 5, 10, 20} and a budget of 1000 evaluations. Each solver–problem pair gets 10 seeds, and the average objective value is reported. The poll tries d, then −d (p. 19). M = 2 was best and is used against monotone DS (p. 19).
  - *Deterministic results*: performance deteriorates as M grows, and the authors give a mechanism: historical values are high, so the test accepts too easily (p. 19). On Set 1, monotone DS is more efficient and max-2 more robust. On Set 2, max-2 is competitive in efficiency and more robust (p. 19).
  - *Stochastic setup*: Gaussian noise, with the sample count printed as O(δ_k⁴), with a positive exponent (p. 20). Reader's note: Appendix B gives O(∆_k⁻⁴) (p. 28), so the printed exponent's sign looks inverted. The noise level is not stated in the text.
  - *Two stochastic scenarios* (p. 20): the "theoretical" scenario resamples all M + 1 values; the "practical" one resamples only the candidate. The practical scenario has the same cost per iteration as monotone DS, but its estimates are no longer independent. In the theoretical scenario, monotone DS wins on Set 1 and max-2 wins on Set 2. In the practical scenario, max-2 is better on both sets (pp. 20–21).
  - *Not in the text*: the convergence test that defines success in the profiles, and the performance measure t_{p,s} (p. 19). No code link was found.
  - Transferable technique: build a second test set *selected for the mechanism* your method targets (here, negative curvature detected by a second-order method) and report both the generic set and the targeted set (pp. 18–19).
  - Transferable technique: run the analysed variant and a cheaper practical variant side by side, with equal per-iteration cost for the practical one, and label which one the theory covers (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds the non-monotone lineage in order: Grippo–Lampariello–Lucidi [21], Dai [10], Zhang–Hager [29] and the Cartis–Sampaio–Toint complexity bound [7] (pp. 2–3). Constant-slack noise relaxations (Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal [3]; Cao–Berahas–Scheinberg [6]) are presented as the other use of non-monotonicity (p. 3). In DFO, it credits Diniz-Ehrhardt–Martínez–Raydan [13] as the "first non-monotone DFO algorithm known to us", then García-Palomares [14, 15] and Grippo–Rinaldi [22]. None of these has iteration complexity (p. 3). The difference between line search and direct search is stated, not glossed: direct search keeps the step size adaptive across iterations, so the analysis differs (p. 3). Novelty is claimed twice, with "To the best of our knowledge" (p. 4). The machinery is openly borrowed from the Scheinberg line [5] via the group's own [12] (p. 13). Six of the 29 references are from the group: [9], [12], [17], [19], [20] and [28] (pp. 23–24).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution paragraph (§1.2, p. 4) states the rule as a displayed inequality. It follows with three settings and one bound, the O(ϵ⁻²) that matches monotone DS, and the "main technical challenge" (the merit function). The abstract names the enabling devices, "a new family of merit functions" and "a renewal-reward stopping-time argument" (p. 1). §2 is a design-rationale section: it has no theorems and explains why the natural candidate fails (pp. 4–6). Hedging is moderate. The numerics claim "competitive, sometimes better" performance (p. 4) and "particularly helpful on problems exhibiting negative curvature" (p. 1). The paper is candid about costs: the geometric M factor (p. 14) and resampling that "can offset its benefits" (p. 21). The text has typos ("derterministic" p. 3, "analyis" p. 6, "Let Let" p. 18) and the sample-order exponent noted under D5.
  - Transferable technique: give the analysis its own short section, *before* the algorithms, that states the properties the Lyapunov function must have and shows the naive candidate failing (pp. 4–6).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are theoretical (three complexity results, one merit-function family), algorithmic (a stochastic max-M DS) and empirical (two CUTEst sets) (pp. 4, 21). Stated limits:
  - the geometric dependence on M may not be sharp (p. 14);
  - performance degrades with M (p. 19);
  - resampling can cancel the benefit (p. 21);
  - no problem geometry is known where non-monotone acceptance has a *provable* advantage (p. 22).

  Future work: average-based non-monotone rules, and max-M versions of line search and trust region with probabilistic estimates (p. 22). Limits that are visible but not stated:
  - the experiments run variants the theory does not cover (±d polling; no resampling), with γθ = 1 (see D3);
  - bounds are given in iterations only; there is no evaluation or sample complexity;
  - the only baseline is the group's own monotone DS. There is no NoMoDS, no non-monotone DFO line search and no NOMAD-type solver;
  - the convergence test and noise level are not stated, and there is no code link.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ordered-correction Lyapunov function for max-over-memory acceptance: Φ_k = max_i{f(x^i) − c_i∆_k²} − f* + η∆_k², with increasing c_i chosen so that all gaps in the min are equal (pp. 5, 11–12).
- To prove a merit function decreases on both outcomes, write each case as a min of gap terms using max(a, x) − max(a, y) ≥ x − y and a − max(b, c) = min(a − b, a − c). Then choose η to balance the success and failure cases (pp. 12–13).
- For stochastic acceptance tests, split on whether the *true* decrease H_k exceeds a threshold. Cap the false-acceptance loss with a tail bound whose constant β is set to half the unsuccessful-iteration decrease (pp. 16–18).
- Reuse a stopping-time theorem as a black box by verifying exactly its two conditions and stating them as a numbered list before the theorem (p. 13).
- Derive the sample size behind a tail-bound assumption in an appendix: bound |max_i(a_i + e_i) − max_i a_i| ≤ max_i |e_i|, sum the errors, apply Markov (p. 28).

### S111 · Stochastic block coordinate and function alternation for multi-objective optimization and learning (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2605.12432)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. H. Tran (Lehigh ISE) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh ML-facing multiobjective line. It follows Liu & Vicente's stochastic multi-gradient paper (ref. [34]; card S017 in c06) and their stochastic alternating bi-objective algorithm (ref. [33], JOTA 2023; no card found in this set). The Pareto metrics come from DMS (ref. [12]; card S003) (pp. 2–3, 12–13, 20–21). **Text read**: pp. 1–22 in full, including Appendix A (notation and Lemma A.1, pp. 13–14), Appendix B (non-convex proof, pp. 15–17) and Appendix C (convex proof, pp. 17–19). The first page has no heading for the abstract; the opening paragraph serves as the abstract (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min F(x) = (E[g_1(x, ξ)], …, E[g_q(x, ξ)]), with x split into s blocks, L-smooth objectives and unbiased stochastic gradients (pp. 1–2). The entry is cost. Multi-gradient methods "require computing gradients with respect to all variables at every iteration" (p. 1). The gap is stated as a combination: no prior work alternates *both* functions and variable blocks for stochastic MOO (p. 3). The paper names what is missing in its three nearest neighbours:
  - the group's alternating algorithm [33] alternates only functions, handles only two objectives and covers only convex and strongly convex cases (p. 3);
  - Cheng et al. [9] scalarize and give no theory (p. 3);
  - stochastic BCD [50] is only asymptotic in the non-convex case and assumes vanishing variance (pp. 3, 7).
  - Transferable technique: define the novelty as the *product* of two known alternations (functions × blocks), and name for each nearest neighbour the one dimension it lacks (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Block-SMOO (Algorithm 1, p. 5) has two loops. The outer iteration t draws a permutation σ of the blocks. For each block it runs p = Σ_k m_k plain SGD steps on that block only. Each step uses *one* objective, chosen by an index map π with |π⁻¹(k)| = m_k. The key observation is that step frequencies *are* scalarization weights: the p steps of a cycle target F_m = Σ_k (m_k/p) f_k = (1/p)Σ_j f_{π(j)} (p. 4). The paper's example: m = (5, 15) minimizes 0.25f_1 + 0.75f_2 (p. 4).
  - *Unchanged*: the SGD step and a step size fixed within the outer iteration. There is no multi-gradient QP.
  - *Changed*: which function and which block each step touches.
  - *Special cases*: q = 1 gives stochastic BCD [50], s = 1 gives function alternation [33], and s = q = 1 gives SGD (p. 5).
  - *Front*: the Pareto front is obtained by sweeping m over a grid of the simplex. This is "guaranteed" to find all Pareto points only when the f_k are convex (pp. 4, 8).
  - Transferable technique: turn a weighted objective into a *schedule*. Allocate m_k of every p inner steps to objective k, and state the implied weighted function explicitly (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: list the parameter settings under which your algorithm collapses to known methods, as a sanity check and as positioning in the same paragraph (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Base assumptions*: each f_k is L-smooth and F_m is bounded below (A3.1, p. 5). Stochastic gradients are unbiased with E‖∇g_k(x, ξ)‖² ≤ σ² at every x (A3.2, p. 6). A3.2 is needed only for the partial (block) gradients (p. 6).
  - *Convex case*: every f_k is convex, x* exists and the outer iterates stay within ∆ of x* (A3.3). Projection onto a bounded convex set is offered as the alternative to boundedness (p. 7). Convexity of F_m alone suffices for Theorem 3.2, but tracing the whole front needs every f_k convex (p. 8).
  - *PL case*: F_m satisfies the PL inequality on all of Rⁿ (A3.4, p. 8). The paper notes that no bounded iterates are needed here, unlike [34] (p. 9).
  - Reader's observation (not in the paper), on bounded gradients. A3.2(b) implies ‖∇F_m‖ ≤ σ everywhere (Lemma A.1(a), p. 14). Combined with PL on Rⁿ, this forces F_m − F* ≤ σ²/(2µ) on the whole space, a strong restriction that is not discussed.
  - Reader's observation (not in the paper), on the test problem. f_k(U, V) = ‖Y_k − XUV_k‖² (p. 9) is non-convex in (U, V) and has unbounded gradients. So neither A3.2(b) nor the convexity required for the front guarantee holds globally for the experiments.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Every proof treats one outer iteration as a *perturbed* weighted-sum gradient step.
  - *Non-convex* (App. B, pp. 15–17). A descent lemma is applied at each inner step and telescoped over (i, j). The difference between gradients at the cycle start x^{t,i,0} and at the intermediate points x^{t,i,j} is collected in an error term A_t. It is bounded by 2σL Σ‖x^{t,i,0} − x^{t,i,j}‖ ≤ α_tLσ²sp(p − 1), using the drift bound E‖x^{t,i,j+1} − x^{t,i,j}‖ ≤ α_tσ (Lemma A.1(b), pp. 14–16). Next, −a² ≤ −½b² + (a − b)² moves the gradient from x^{t,i,0} to x^{t,0,0}. The block drift ‖x^{t,i,0} − x^{t,0,0}‖² ≤ i²p²α_t²σ², via ‖Σa_i‖² ≤ nΣ‖a_i‖², produces the constant Lσ²p²(s + Lα_ts³/3) in (3.1) (p. 16).   - *Convex* (App. C, pp. 17–19). A distance recursion, with a drift term B_t ≤ ½α_tσ(∆L + σ)s²p² that uses ‖x^{t,0,0} − x*‖ ≤ ∆. Averaging then gives O(1/√T) for the averaged iterate (Theorem 3.2, p. 7).
  - *PL* (pp. 8–9). Recursion (3.1) with α_t = 2/(µ(t + 1)); multiply by t(t + 1) and telescope. Theorem 3.3 gives 4Lσ²p²(3µs + 2Ls³)/(3Tµ³).

  - *Unit*: all rates are per *outer* iteration T, each of which costs s·p partial stochastic gradients. The p²s³ growth of the constants is not discussed (pp. 6–9).
  - Reader's observation (not in the paper): a slip in the definition of A_t (p. 15).   - updates are sequential Gauss–Seidel only, and Jacobi-style parallel updates are future work (p. 13);
  - the analysis is smooth only, and non-smooth MOO is future work (p. 13).

  - bounded gradients are assumed together with PL;
  - the front guarantee needs convexity, but the test problem is non-convex;
  - a weighted-sum sweep cannot reach non-convex parts of a front;
  - there is no code link.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Drift-bound analysis of alternating or cyclic SGD. Compare each inner gradient with the gradient at the cycle start. Bound the difference by L × (accumulated steps) × σ, and absorb the result as an O(α²) term per cycle (pp. 15–16).
- Use −a² ≤ −½b² + (a − b)² to move a descent term from an intermediate point to the cycle's starting point (p. 16).
- Derive the PL rate from the non-convex one-cycle recursion: substitute ‖∇F‖² ≥ 2µ(F − F*), take α_t = 2/(µ(t + 1)), multiply by t(t + 1) and telescope (pp. 8–9).
- Encode preference weights as integer step allocations m_k with Σm_k = p, and trace the front by enumerating all compositions of p (pp. 4, 11).
