### S021 · Least-squares approach to risk parity in portfolio selection (Quantitative Finance 2016, DOI 10.1080/14697688.2015.1031815)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xi Bai (Lehigh ISE; named among the Lehigh-page students in SKILL.md's Academic Lineage) and Reha Tütüncü (Goldman Sachs Asset Management) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a Lehigh-era applied-optimization paper with an industry co-author. The algorithm is the alternating linearization method (ALM) of Goldfarb–Ma–Scheinberg [7] (card S005), carried from a convex to a nonconvex setting. ALM's details and convergence analysis are left to the Bai–Scheinberg technical report [1] (p. 14, p. 25, p. 27). **Read**: full text, including all tables and the 11-item reference list (pp. 27–28). Figures 3.1 and 6.1–6.6 are graphics; only their captions and the surrounding text were read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a practitioner portfolio-construction problem.
  - Mean–variance portfolios are highly sensitive to their inputs, especially expected returns (Best–Grauer) (p. 2).
  - The simple rules (60/40, 1/n) spread *capital*, not *risk* (p. 2).
  - Risk parity asks for equal risk contributions x_i·∂σ/∂x_i (eq. 2.6, p. 4). The Euler decomposition of a degree-one homogeneous risk measure motivates it (Thm 2.1, p. 4).

  The gap is that existing work is mostly long-only and solves a convex log-barrier problem (p. 2; §2.1, p. 5). Three things break that approach:
  - **Long-short portfolios**: there can be up to 2^{n−1} normalized solutions (Lemma 2.2, p. 6).
  - **General bounds**: a risk-parity solution may not exist, up to 2^{n−1} convex problems may have to be solved before infeasibility is established, and the barrier gives no guidance towards a point "close to risk parity" (p. 7).
  - **Group parity**: it is unclear how to extend the approach (p. 7).

  A small counterexample shows why a naive fix fails. Projecting the long-only parity solution onto the bounds gives F = 0.0143, against 0.0128 from optimizing (2.17). It is also worse on both concentration measures, HRC and Herfindahl (Example 2.1, pp. 8–9). The problem is stated as least-squares deviation from parity, with a free level θ, under bounds and a budget constraint (2.17), p. 8.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: One nonconvex least-squares model replaces an exponential family of convex orthant problems. The model is F(x,θ) = Σ_i (x_i(Σx)_i − θ)² with a_i ≤ x_i ≤ b_i and Σx_i = 1 (2.17), p. 8.
  - Every risk-parity solution is a global minimizer with value 0. When none exists, the minimizer is the portfolio closest to parity in the least-squares sense (pp. 2–3, p. 8).
  - **Changed** relative to Maillard–Roncalli–Teiletche [9] (2.16): their n² pairwise terms become n terms measured against a free θ. The optimal θ is the mean risk contribution (Lemma 2.3, p. 8). The authors call the result "computationally less demanding, is easier to analyze and contains fewer nonlinearities" (p. 8).
  - **Extensions in the same model**:
    - *Minimum-variance parity* (4.1) adds ρ·xᵀΣx. Algorithm 1 is a homotopy (p. 12). It starts with a large ρ, where the problem is convex on a bounded domain and close to minimum variance. It decreases ρ geometrically and warm-starts each subproblem. Finally it drops the term and solves the pure parity problem in the orthant it has identified (p. 12).
    - *Group parity* (4.2) (pp. 13–14).
  - **Solver**:
    - The general class is min Σ_i((A_i x)ᵀ(B_i x) − θ)² (5.1). It is rewritten as Σ_i(xᵀM_i x − θ)², where M_i is neither symmetric nor PSD (5.2), p. 14.
    - Splitting the variable (y = x) makes F(x, y_k) and F(x_k, y) convex. ALM alternates linearized-proximal models Q1 and Q2 (5.6), and each is a strictly convex QP (Alg. 2, p. 15).
    - A backtracking rule on μ with a sufficient-reduction test gives Alg. 3 (pp. 15–16).
  - **Kept**: ALM itself, "initially inspired by" [7], but now "applied to a nonconvex problem" in a substantially different setting (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Σ is symmetric PSD (p. 3). The risk measure is volatility (xᵀΣx)^{1/2} (p. 4). Expected returns are deliberately left out, as in all risk-based strategies (p. 3).
  - Constraints are box bounds plus a budget (p. 8). The group model assumes non-overlapping groups, with investment in every group (p. 14).
  - Cardinality constraints are excluded (p. 13). The 14-asset backtest ignores time-varying covariance (p. 18).
  - **Admitted gaps**:
    - Algorithm 1 has no guarantee because the problem is nonconvex (p. 12).
    - Convergence of ALM to a local optimum is asserted (p. 3, p. 15) but proved only in [1], which was not read here.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There is no convergence or complexity theorem in this paper. What it proves is the *structure of the solution set* and the *landscape*:
  - **Lemma 2.1** (statement p. 5, proof p. 6): the long-only normalized solution exists and is unique. The proof combines strict convexity of the barrier problem (2.8) with a scaling device: √α·x^c solves the barrier problem with constant αc, and α is chosen so that the weights sum to 1.
  - **Lemma 2.2** (p. 6): there are at most 2^{n−1} normalized solutions. Each sign vector s gives one convex orthant problem (2.13), and s and −s give the same normalized point. A diagonal-Σ closed form (2.15) illustrates this (p. 7).
  - **Lemma 3.1** (p. 10): with the constraints dropped, a point with ∇_xF = 0 and ∂F/∂θ = 0 is a global minimum with F = 0. The device is to contract the gradient with x. Homogeneity turns xᵀ∇_xF = 0 into Σ(B_i − θ)B_i = 0, where B_i = x_i(Σx)_i (3.5). Substituting θ = mean(B) gives n·ΣB_i² = (ΣB_i)², which is the equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz, so all B_i are equal (3.6), p. 10.
  - **Counterexamples** locating where spurious points come from:
    - Example 3.1: the budget equality alone creates a third KKT point, and it is a local *maximum*. The authors add: "So far we have not seen an example where the equality constraint alone can cause a local minimum" (p. 11).
    - Example 3.2: bound constraints create a boundary local minimum that is not global, even though a parity solution exists in the same orthant (Fig. 3.1, p. 11).
  - **Reader's note**: the introduction states that "no local optima exist on the interior of the feasible set" (p. 2). Lemma 3.1 proves this only with *all* constraints dropped, the budget equality included. With the budget equality, the claim rests on the examples searched so far (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **§6.1, a 5-asset illustration** (pp. 16–18). Three settings:
    - long-only (Table 6.1, p. 16): 1/n, minimum variance and risk parity, reporting weights, RC_i and volatility;
    - tight bounds [0.05, 0.35], where no parity solution exists, so the "optimal parity" portfolio is reported (Table 6.2, p. 17);
    - long-short bounds [−1, 2] (Table 6.3, p. 18): random starts reach different parity solutions. Algorithm 1 returns the one with the least volatility, compared with the 2⁴ = 16 solutions from enumerating orthants (p. 17).
  - **§6.2, backtests** (pp. 18–21):
    - 14 asset-class indices, monthly data from Nov 2002 to Aug 2012. Four strategies are compared (60/40, 1/n, minimum variance, risk parity) on excess return, volatility, Sharpe ratio, 5% VaR, HRC and Herfindahl (Table 6.4, p. 20).
    - 17 US industry portfolios, monthly data from Oct 1972 to Sep 2012, with rolling covariance windows of 36, 60 and 120 months and annual rebalancing (Tables 6.5–6.7, pp. 20–21; Figs 6.3–6.5, pp. 22–24). Results are mixed and shown as such: with the 5-year window, risk parity's Sharpe ratio of 0.507 is the lowest of the three (Table 6.6, p. 20).
  - **§6.3, group risk parity** (pp. 21, 25): 482 S&P 500 stocks in 10 GICS sectors, with a 220/24 train/test split of daily returns (Table 6.8, p. 25). Reader's note: the Sharpe comparison rests on 24 test returns (p. 21).
  - **§6.4, efficiency** (pp. 25–27):
    - The code is MATLAB, with Mosek 6.0 for the QPs (p. 25).
    - The cost measure is the *number of QPs solved*, plus iterations to a largest KKT violation of 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻⁶ (Table 6.9, p. 26). Test instances include random Σ = AAᵀ with n = 20 and 200.
    - The caption admits "some parameter tuning for a large starting µ" (p. 26).
    - The baseline is MATLAB fmincon on the 14-asset problem only. SQP fails because it never leaves the start point. Interior point is marked "Partially succeed" and takes 5.061 s, against 0.026 s for ALM (Table 6.10, pp. 26–27).
  - Specialised risk-parity algorithms that the paper cites ([5] Chaves et al., [10] Spinu, pp. 27–28) are not run as baselines.
- **D6 Literature positioning**:
  - The closest model is [9], treated as a restatement rather than an attack. The paper calls its model similar to [9], then says "our formulation has a simpler structure" (p. 2).
  - The log-barrier approach is credited to [9], and to [8] and [10] as independent developments (p. 5). It is kept as the exact method for the long-only case, and only then are its limits listed (p. 7).
  - The algorithmic lineage is self-cited: [7] (Goldfarb–Ma–Scheinberg) and [1] (Bai–Scheinberg) (p. 14).
  - The finance references are short and practitioner-facing: Best–Grauer, DeMiguel et al. on 1/N, Chaves et al., Kaya–Lee, Steinbach, and Brito–Vicente on cardinality (pp. 27–28).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Each structural claim gets a 2- or 3-asset worked example with numbers (Examples 2.1, 3.1, 3.2, 4.1; pp. 8–13).
  - The contribution sentence comes early, in the introduction: "we propose a generalized risk parity model which allows for short sales and applies to cases where risk parity solutions may not exist" (p. 2).
  - Hedging is explicit where the guarantee is missing (p. 12; "This paper is not aimed at algoritmic details", p. 25). Elsewhere a strong claim rests on one small example: "our method is very robust with respect to the starting point" (p. 13).
  - Housekeeping slips:
    - the roadmap puts the experiments in Section 5, but they are in Section 6 (p. 3);
    - typos such as "probelms", "concerntration" and "algoritmic" (p. 9, p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**:
  - **Contribution layers**:
    - modelling: one least-squares parity model covering long-short portfolios, bounds, group parity and preference-based selection;
    - structure theory: existence and uniqueness, the 2^{n−1} bound, and no spurious stationary points without constraints;
    - algorithm: ALM for least squares of products of linear forms;
    - empirical: backtests and a solver comparison (pp. 2–3, p. 27).
  - **Stated limits**: local minima caused by bounds (p. 11), no guarantee for Algorithm 1 (p. 12), cardinality left out (p. 13), and the assumptions of the group model (p. 14).
  - **Future work** is one generic sentence: faster and more efficient algorithms are left to future research (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. The objective is recognised as a sum of squared bilinear forms (5.1)–(5.2), and variable splitting turns every half-step into a strictly convex QP (pp. 14–15). This is Method 6 step 3 (structure that makes subproblems closed-form or cheap) in a nonconvex setting.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. The trusted method that is kept is Scheinberg's own ALM [7], with backtracking on μ added (Alg. 3, pp. 15–16). The change is forced by a new *problem class* (convex → nonconvex), not by a weaker oracle.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The algorithm comparison counts QPs, iterations and CPU time, not function evaluations at equal accuracy. It has one instance against generic fmincon, and ALM's starting μ was tuned (pp. 26–27). The strategy comparisons (portfolio metrics across strategies) are equal-data backtests (pp. 16–25).
- Research Taste mark 4 (*a guarantee comes with numbers*) ⚠ variant: numbers without a guarantee in this paper. Convergence is deferred to a technical report (p. 14).
- Research Taste mark 5 (*minimal safeguard, backed by a negative result*) ✅ analogue. The positive landscape lemma is paired with counterexamples that show exactly which constraints create spurious points (Lemma 3.1, Examples 3.1–3.2, pp. 10–11).
- New-pattern candidate: **least-squares residual reformulation with graceful infeasibility**. A target that is a system of equalities becomes a least-squares model: its zero set is the target set, and its minimizer is a principled fallback when constraints make the target infeasible (p. 8, p. 17).
- New-pattern candidate: **landscape audit**. Prove that there are no spurious stationary points in the relaxed problem, then build tiny examples showing which constraint creates them (pp. 10–11). This is related to the existing *counterexample-first assumption audit*.
- New-pattern candidate: **homotopy from a convex anchor**. Add a convex preference term with a large weight, then shrink it with warm starts, to select among many global solutions (Alg. 1, p. 12).
- Existing candidate *carry the home-field solver into the new field* ✅ second instance, after S011: ALM goes from convex splitting to nonconvex portfolio least squares (p. 14).
- Existing candidate *applied side-collaboration with a lighter evidence standard (trajectory note)* ✅ fits: the baseline is generic, tuning was allowed, and there is no theory in the paper (pp. 26–27).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] **Homogeneity contraction.** For an objective built from homogeneous pieces, take xᵀ∇_xF. With first-order optimality in the auxiliary level variable, stationarity reduces to the equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz, which proves that every unconstrained stationary point is a global zero-residual minimizer (Lemma 3.1, p. 10).
- [D2] Replace pairwise-deviation penalties Σ_{i,j}(r_i − r_j)², which have n² terms, by Σ_i(r_i − θ)² with a free θ. The optimal θ is the mean (Lemma 2.3, p. 8).
- [D2] **Variable splitting for squared bilinear forms.** Write F(x,y) = Σ(xᵀM_i y − θ)² with y = x. Each block subproblem is then convex. Alternate linearized-proximal QPs, and backtrack on μ with a sufficient-reduction test (Algs 2–3, pp. 15–16).
- [D2] **Selection homotopy.** When a nonconvex model has many global solutions:
  1. add ρ·(preference) with a large ρ, which gives a convex problem near the preference optimum;
  2. decrease ρ geometrically, warm-starting each solve;
  3. drop the term and polish inside the identified region (Alg. 1, p. 12).
- [D7] Motivate a new formulation with a 3-variable example in which the naive fix (projection) is measurably worse, both on the target and on two independent metrics (Example 2.1, pp. 8–9).
- [D5] When each iteration's cost is a subproblem solve, report the number of subproblems solved and the iterations needed to reach several KKT-violation thresholds, not only time (Table 6.9, p. 26).

**Quotes**:
- "Moreover, no local optima exist on the interior of the feasible set; in other words, all local optima of our proposed formulation occur due to the constraining of the feasible set by bounds on the weights." (p. 2)
- "Note that due to the nonconvexity of the objective function, there is no theoretical guarantee that Algorithm 1 will always converge to the minimum variance risk parity solution." (p. 12)

### S107 · ProxSTORM-A Stochastic Trust Region Method for Nonsmooth Optimization (Sandia National Laboratories report 2024; text read: arXiv:2510.03187v2)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Robert J. Baraldi, Aurya Javeed, Drew P. Kouri (Sandia National Laboratories). Scheinberg's affiliation is Georgia Tech ISyE (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: the first composite (nonsmooth) extension of STORM (Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg 2018 [15]) and of its BCMS19 complexity analysis [11]. It was written with the authors of the deterministic inexact proximal trust region [5] (pp. 1–2). **Skill consistency note**: SKILL.md's Signature Work Anatomy row for STORM calls ProxSTORM a third-party extension whose authors are unverified (⚠️). p. 1 lists Scheinberg as fourth author, so the flag can be resolved when the skill is next edited (not edited here). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–30, including Appendix A (pp. 29–30). The references (pp. 31–33) were skimmed. In the text extraction, the overlines that mark event complements are lost (e.g. the definition of the complement of I_k on p. 6 reads I_k := Ω\I_k), so complements were reconstructed from context. The figures are graphics; only their captions were read. **Cross-check**: this reading agrees with the c08 card of S098 on every point checked. Points that card lacks are marked ➕.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a merger of two frameworks, each missing what the other has (p. 2):
  - The deterministic proximal trust region [5] handles f + ϕ, but its inexactness bounds must hold always and shrink to zero. In practice, inexactness is stochastic: it comes from sampling, or from "sporadic failures of subroutines" in simulations (p. 2).
  - STORM handles random models and estimates, but only for smooth f (p. 2).

  The problem is min f + ϕ (eq. 1): f is L-smooth and possibly nonconvex on dom ϕ, ϕ is proper, closed and convex with an exactly computable prox, and f + ϕ is bounded below (Assumption 1, pp. 1–2). The target applications are data science, operations research and PDE-constrained optimization (p. 1). A second entry is internal: re-analysing STORM shows that function estimates are used only through differences (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (p. 4) is STORM with the gradient replaced by the proximal-gradient map.
  - **Changed**:
    - h_k = (x_k − prox_{rϕ}(x_k − r∇m_k(x_k)))/r replaces ∇m_k in the acceptance test ‖h_k‖ ≥ η2δ_k (pp. 3–4).
    - ϕ is kept exact in the subproblem and in pred/ared (p. 4).
    - Steps must satisfy a generalized fraction-of-Cauchy-decrease condition (S2) with curvature bound b_k (5), p. 3.
    - ➕ If ‖h_k‖ < η2δ_k, the step computation is skipped, because the step would be rejected anyway (p. 4).
  - **Kept**: STORM's two-part acceptance test (6) and its radius update, with a single γ and a cap δ_max = γ^ℓδ0 ((7)–(8), p. 4). The authors call this update a "matter of convenience": a more flexible one "complicates the analysis" (p. 5).
  - **Subproblem solver**: the spectral proximal gradient method of [5], starting from a Cauchy point on the arc p_k(r) (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Assumption 2: uniformly bounded model curvature (p. 6).
  - Assumption 3: P(I_k | F_{k−1}) ≥ α, where I_k = {‖∇M_k(X_k) − ∇f(X_k)‖ ≤ κ_grad Δ_k} (pp. 6–7).
  - Assumption 4: P(J_k | F_{k−1/2}) ≥ β, where J_k = {|A_k − C_k| ≤ ηP_k}, i.e. accuracy of the *reduction*, relative to pred (p. 7).
  - Assumption 5: on successful steps whose reduction estimate is inaccurate, E[−A_k 1(S_k ∩ J̄_k) | F_{k−1/2}] ≤ c1Δ² + c2‖h‖Δ (p. 8). It holds automatically when ϕ ≡ 0, and it is implied by E|C_k − A_k| ≤ c3Δ² (Prop. 2, p. 8).
  - Assumption 6: α and β satisfy (32) and (34) (p. 15), which implies αβ > 1/2 (p. 20).

  The half-step filtration F_{k−1/2} separates the model's randomness from that of the computed reduction ((10), pp. 5–6). ➕ The authors trace Assumption 3 back to BSV [4], which was "inspired by constructing sparse interpolation models in derivative free setting over random interpolation sets" (p. 9). They say the contract tolerates heavy tails and fully corrupted estimates. They also note that in practice sample sizes often need not grow as Δ_k shrinks, because the noise often decreases near the solution (p. 9). Admitted gap: numerical values of α and β are hard to determine (Remark 12, p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The skeleton is STORM's.
  - **Potential**: Ψ_k = ν(f+ϕ)(X_k) + (1−ν)Δ_k², with ν fixed by (20) (p. 11).
  - **Pointwise event bounds**:
    - Lemma 1: on I_k, ared − pred ≤ 2κ_valδ², by the descent lemma applied to f − m_k, because ϕ cancels (p. 7).
    - Lemma 3: large actual reduction on I_k ∩ B_k (p. 10).
    - Lemma 4: I_k ∩ J_k ∩ R_k ⊆ S_k, i.e. such steps are accepted (p. 11).
    - Prop. 5: decrease −c5Δ² on J_k (pp. 11–12).
    - Prop. 6: increase at most c6Δ² on I_k (p. 12).
    - Cor. 7: decrease −νc4‖h‖Δ on I_k ∩ B_k ∩ S_k (p. 12).
    - Prop. 8: when both oracles fail, an *expected* increase bound from Assumption 5. The authors liken it to CVaR (Remark 9, p. 13).
  - **Lemma 10** (pp. 13–15) splits Ω on B_k = {‖h(X_k)‖ ≥ ζΔ_k}. On B_k, the positive ‖h‖Δ term of the worst case is offset by the negative term of the best case, which has weight αβ. α and β are then pushed close to 1, giving E[Ψ_{k+1} − Ψ_k | F_{k−1}] ≤ −ΘΔ_k².
  - **Consequences**:
    - Δ_k → 0 almost surely, since E[ΣΔ²] < ∞ (Cor. 16, p. 17);
    - liminf ‖h‖ = 0 almost surely, by comparing the ±1 submartingale W_k = Σ[2·1(I∩J) − 1] with log_γ Δ_k (Thm 19, pp. 17–19);
    - lim ‖h‖ = 0, via the (2/r + L)-Lipschitz continuity of the prox-gradient map, which follows from nonexpansiveness (App. A, pp. 29–30);
    - E[T_ε] ≤ αβ/(2αβ−1)·Ψ̃0/(Θδ_ε²) + 1 = O(ε⁻²). This is obtained by checking the three conditions of BCMS19's stopping-time theorem [11, Thm 2] (Thm 21, Prop. 22, pp. 19–20).
  - **Sample complexity** (§5): with the same samples at x_k and x_k + s_k (common random numbers), a Taylor expansion gives E|A−C| ≤ σ_gΔ/√N + O(Δ³) + (Hessian-variance)·Δ²/√N (eq. 44, p. 22). Markov's inequality then shows that N = O(Δ⁻²) suffices for Assumptions 4–5, against STORM's O(Δ⁻⁴) (p. 9, p. 23). Total sample complexity is quoted from [23]: O(ε⁻⁴) with common random numbers, O(ε⁻⁶) in general (p. 23).
  - ➕ Remarks that interpret the constants:
    - Remark 11: with β → 1, (32) reduces to α ≥ 1/2, "models being more accurate more often than not" (p. 16).
    - Remark 13: as L → ∞, the restriction on α and β from (32) approaches a finite number, but the right-hand side of (34) grows without bound and drives 1 − β and (1 − α)c2 to zero. With high curvature, the computed reductions must be accurate often, "because otherwise the increase from a poor step could be large" (p. 16).
    - Remark 14: the complexity is proportional to ζ², and 1 − ν (hence Θ) is asymptotically proportional to η1η2, so more relaxed acceptance may mean less progress per step (p. 16).
  - ➕ Reader's notes on the text:
    - η is stated as η ∈ (1, min{η1, 1 − η1}), an empty interval, on p. 6; p. 9 has (0, …). The c08 card noted this too.
    - Prop. 22 writes W̃_k = 1(I_k ∩ J_k) − 1, but Theorem 21 needs values ±1, i.e. 2·1(·) − 1 (p. 20).
    - Theorem 21 conditions on F_k, while Lemma 10 conditions on F_{k−1} (p. 20 vs p. 13). The indexing was not checked against [11].
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **(1) ℓ1-regularized neural network on HIGGS** (pp. 23–25):
    - Data and model: 11M samples with 28 features; a two-layer sigmoid network with h = 10 hidden units; λ = 10⁻² (pp. 23–24).
    - Sampling: a fixed n = 100 samples per iteration, used for the gradient, the Hessian and cred.
    - The authors list how this departs from the theory: (i) the static n is in effect a heuristic choice of α and β; (ii) using the model samples for cred breaks the conditioning on F_{k−1/2} that Assumptions 4–5 require (p. 24).
    - Solver settings: at most two spectral-proximal-gradient inner iterations, matrix-free Hessian products, and the parameters of Table 2 (η1 = 0.5, η2 = 5·10⁻⁵, δ0 = 10, γ = 5) (p. 24).
    - Protocol: 100 realizations. The test error is computed on 10,000 held-out samples, with a different seed per realization (p. 24).
    - Baseline: Adam with the same seeds and samples, and the zero subgradient at 0 for ℓ1 (the PyTorch/JAX convention). Adam gets m = 150 iterations against ProxSTORM's 50, to offset up to 2 Hessian products per iteration (p. 24).
    - A reported loss: as λ → 0, Adam wins (p. 24). The authors argue that the work handicap is unfair when sampling, e.g. a PDE solve, is the bottleneck (p. 24).
  - **(2) Stochastic topology optimization** (pp. 25–28):
    - Problem: compliance under a load angle ξ ~ U[−π/6, π/6], on a 100×200 mesh (d = 20,000), with volume and box constraints handled as the indicator ϕ (pp. 25–26).

  - No high-probability complexity bound is given (pp. 17–20).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. STORM is kept intact, and ProxSTORM reduces to it when ϕ ≡ 0 (p. 1, p. 5, p. 27). The new proof touches only the inequalities where ϕ enters: the descent lemma on f − m_k, the prox nonexpansiveness (4), and (S2) (pp. 2, 7, 18).

- Method 1 ✅ evidence ("sufficiently accurate, sufficiently often", p. 2) and ⚠ variant. The function-value contract becomes a relative accuracy on the reduction difference, scaled by pred_k (p. 7), which lowers per-iteration samples to O(Δ⁻²) with common random numbers (p. 23). Remark 11 ties the threshold back to α ≥ 1/2 (p. 16), which is Method 1 step 5.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a potential of the form ν·objective + (1−ν)Δ², expected decrease, and a stopping-time bound at the deterministic order O(ε⁻²) (pp. 11–20). ⚠ variant: there is no high-probability upgrade; the radius lower bound is delegated to [23] (p. 9, p. 23).
- Research Heuristic 2 ⚠ variant. The function estimates are under the contract, but the contract is on differences, not values (p. 7).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The comparisons are budget-adjusted iteration counts against Adam and a fixed-sample SAA, not equal-accuracy evaluation counts. The baseline trust-region update also differs from ProxSTORM's (pp. 24, 27).
- Existing candidates, **not independent evidence** because this is the same text as S098: *difference-based function-estimate contract* (pp. 3, 7, 21–23), *generalise and recover* (pp. 1, 3; Remark 15, p. 16), *explicit theory-vs-experiment deviation list* (p. 24), and *meta-framework… instantiate* (the three conditions of BCMS19 Thm 2 are checked, pp. 19–20).
- New-pattern candidate: **avoid verify-until-pass loops under probabilistic oracles**. A loop that re-tests a condition which holds only with probability p compounds the chance of a false exit, so design contracts that are checked once per iteration (p. 28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D3] For a composite f + ϕ, put all randomness in f and keep ϕ exact. The pred − ared gap then involves only f − m_k, and the smooth descent-lemma argument carries over line by line (Lemma 1, p. 7).
- [D3/D4] Write the function-estimate contract on the *difference* |ared − cred| ≤ η·pred_k. With common random numbers, a Taylor expansion of the difference gives O(σΔ/√N), so N ∝ Δ⁻² suffices. A contract on each value separately would need N ∝ Δ⁻⁴ (eq. 44, pp. 22–23).
- [D4] Add a half-step σ-algebra F_{k−1/2} between the model draw and the reduction draw. The two success probabilities then multiply cleanly (αβ) under the tower property (pp. 5–6, p. 13).
- [D4] In the expected-decrease lemma, the worst event (both oracles bad) gives a positive ‖h‖Δ term. Cancel it with the negative ‖h‖Δ term of the best event (weight αβ), then choose α and β close to 1 (Lemma 10, pp. 13–15).
- [D4] To upgrade liminf to lim in the prox setting, prove that the prox-gradient map is (2/r + L)-Lipschitz from nonexpansiveness. The smooth crossing-sequence argument then goes through unchanged (App. A, p. 30).
- [D4] Get the complexity by checking the three conditions of a published stopping-time theorem ([11, Thm 2]) instead of redoing the renewal argument (Thm 21, Prop. 22, pp. 19–20).
- [D7] After the key lemma, add a constants table and one remark per constant that translates it into its effect on complexity or on which oracle must be reliable (Table 1, Remarks 11–14, pp. 15–16).
- [D5] Run 100 seeded realizations and compare histograms of the final test objective with a baseline that uses the same seeds and samples. Adjust the baseline's iteration budget for per-iteration work, and state where the baseline wins (p. 24).

### S106 · Novel and Efficient Approximations for Zero-One and Ranking Losses of Linear Classifiers (Vietnam Journal of Mathematics 2025, DOI 10.1007/s10013-025-00767-6; text read: arXiv:1903.00359v1)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Hiva Ghanbari and Minhan Li (both Lehigh ISE; both listed among Scheinberg's students in SKILL.md's Academic Lineage) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the 2019 extension of Ghanbari–Scheinberg 2018 (card S078). The normality assumption moves from the data to the classifier output wᵀX, and the optimizer changes from gradient descent to L-BFGS. It was published in 2025, six years after the preprint. **Read**: the full text of the 2019 preprint, pp. 1–18. Figures 4.1–4.4 are raw axis numbers in the text file, so only their captions and the text describing them were read. The references (p. 18) were read in full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a mismatch between goal and practice. The true goal is the expected error, or the expected ranking loss for imbalanced data (Hanley–McNeil) (p. 1). ERM optimizes surrogates because the sample average of the 0-1 loss is a step function whose gradient is zero or undefined (p. 1, p. 3). The paper's thesis is that "unlike their sample average approximations, the expected error and ranking loss functions of a linear classifier are themselves often smooth" (p. 1). The second entry is cost:
  - the logistic gradient is O(n) per iteration (p. 3);
  - pairwise ranking surrogates cost O(n⁺n⁻), and O(n log n) for pairwise hinge after sorting (pp. 3–4);
  - stochastic-gradient methods for AUC surrogates are "not as well developed" (p. 4).

  The problems are stated as F01(w) = P(Y·f(X;w) < 0) (2.1) and F_rank(w) = P(f(X⁺;w) − f(X⁻;w) < 0) (2.6) (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Lemma 3.1 splits F01 by class into P(wᵀX⁺ ≤ 0) and P(wᵀX⁻ ≤ 0). F01 is therefore smooth wherever the CDF of wᵀX is smooth, except at w = 0 (p. 4).
  - The key move is to assume normality of the *one-dimensional projection* wᵀX, not of X. This gives closed forms:
    - F_n01 = P(+)(1 − φ(μ_Z⁺/σ_Z⁺)) + P(−)φ(μ_Z⁻/σ_Z⁻) (Thm 3.3, p. 5);
    - F_nrank = 1 − φ(μ_Z/σ_Z) for Z = wᵀ(X⁻ − X⁺) (Thm 3.7, p. 7);
    - their gradients (Thms 3.5, 3.8, pp. 6–7).
  - Exact moments are replaced by empirical ones (pp. 1–2, p. 8).
  - Design details:
    - The objective is scale-invariant, so a soft penalty λ(1 − ‖w‖²)² replaces the constraint ‖w‖ = 1 (p. 7).
    - The cross-covariances Σ⁺⁻ are set to zero (p. 8).
    - The start point is w0 ∝ μ⁺ − (μ⁻ᵀμ⁺/‖μ⁻‖²)μ⁻ (p. 10).
    - The optimizer is L-BFGS (memory 20) with a Wolfe line search, used for all four objectives including the nonsmooth pairwise hinge, citing Lewis–Overton and Yu et al. (p. 10).
  - **Changed**: only the objective. **Kept**: an off-the-shelf quasi-Newton method.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The data pairs are i.i.d. (p. 2).
  - wᵀX⁺, wᵀX⁻ and wᵀ(X⁻ − X⁺) are approximately normal for the w met during training. This is justified by CLT variants for dependent variables and by the claim that features "can be grouped into blocks of variables, so that the blocks are (nearly) independent" (p. 5, p. 6).
  - The model is linear classifiers only (p. 4). The objective is nonconvex (p. 2) and undefined at w = 0 (p. 7).
  - **Admitted**: normality is not visible for every data set (Fig. 4.2 discussion, p. 8). The claim that the assumption is milder than Gaussian data is argued and illustrated, not proved (p. 2, p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There is no convergence theory and no bound on the approximation error. The conclusion calls what is given "theoretical motivation" (p. 15). The devices:
  - an event decomposition by class label (Lemma 3.1, p. 4);
  - closure of Gaussians under linear maps, quoted from Tong (Lemma 3.2, Thm 3.6, pp. 5–6);
  - the chain rule for φ(g(w)) with g(w) = wᵀμ/√(wᵀΣw) (Lemma 3.4, pp. 5–6).
  - Per-iteration cost accounting: O(d²) for the new objectives against O(nd) for logistic and O(dn log n) for pairwise hinge. Computing the moments costs O(nd²) once. For sparse high-dimensional data, Σ = XᵀX/(n−1) − n/(n−1)·X̄X̄ᵀ is applied without being formed (p. 10).
  - Reader's note: Thm 3.5 equates the gradients of F01 and F_n01 "Under conditions of Theorem 3.3". Those conditions are stated for a single given w, but equal gradients need normality on a neighbourhood of w (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Checking the assumption**: histograms of wᵀX⁺, wᵀX⁻ and wᵀ(X⁻ − X⁺) at an early and a late iterate, for a9a, whose features are binary encodings (Fig. 4.1, p. 8), and for diabetes, poker, svm3 and segment, where normality is weaker (Fig. 4.2, pp. 8–9).
  - **Synthetic data**:
    - 9 Gaussian sets with d = 500, 1000 and 2500, n = 5000, varied priors and 0/5/10% label-swap outliers; λ = 0; an 80/20 split; 20 runs (Table 4.1, p. 11; p. 10).
    - Each set is solved with *exact* and with *estimated* moments, against logistic regression (Table 4.2, p. 11) and pairwise hinge (Table 4.3, p. 12).
    - The text says estimated moments beat logistic regression except on data8 and data9, where n is small relative to d and the moment estimates are inaccurate (p. 11). In Table 4.2 those two are near-ties (0.9290 vs 0.9263; 0.9249 vs 0.9264), while exact moments reach about 0.99 on both (p. 11).
  - **Real data**:
    - 21 LIBSVM/UCI sets, from fourclass (d = 2) to rcv1 (d = 47,236) and covtype (n = 581,012) (Table 4.4, p. 12). Features are scaled to |x| ≤ 1. Five-fold cross-validation is repeated 4 times, i.e. 20 runs (pp. 11, 13).
    - The tables report test accuracy or AUC ± std, iterations, solve time and a separate "moment time" column (Tables 4.5–4.6, pp. 13–14).
    - Pairwise hinge did not solve real-sim within 24 h (p. 13).
  - **Baselines and tuning are asymmetric**:
    - λ = 0.001 is fixed for the new objectives, against λ = 1/n for logistic and 1/√(n⁺n⁻) for hinge (p. 13);
    - Adam gets "some hand tuning" of batch size and epochs (p. 14);
    - LDA runs from scikit-learn (p. 14).
  - **Surrogate fidelity**: each objective is plotted against the empirical 0-1 or ranking loss at 100 points along the optimization path (p. 14; Figs 4.3–4.4, pp. 16–17).
  - **Visible in the tables but not discussed**:
    - covtype: F_n01 reaches 0.5502 accuracy against 0.7562 for logistic regression (Table 4.5, p. 13);
    - F_n01 hits the 500-iteration cap on sonar, mnist, covtype and real-sim (Table 4.5, p. 13);
    - poker: every method scores 0.9795 accuracy (Table 4.7, p. 15), which is essentially the majority-class rate P⁻ = 0.98 (Table 4.4, p. 12), and the AUC is about 0.505 (Table 4.6, p. 14).
  - **Inconsistencies**:
    - "shuttle" appears in Table 4.6 (p. 14) but not in the data table 4.4 (p. 12);
    - the caption of Fig. 4.2 names only diabetes, but the figure shows five sets (p. 9);
    - the text calls LDA "clearly inferior" (p. 14), yet Table 4.7 has LDA ahead of F_n01 on colon (0.8875 vs 0.7833) and german (0.7675 vs 0.7553) (p. 15);
    - the synthetic Tables 4.2–4.3 match S078's tables digit for digit, run times included (checked by grep: S078 file p. 6 and p. 8). But S078 used gradient descent with backtracking (S078 p. 5), while this paper says L-BFGS was used for all functions (p. 10).
- **D6 Literature positioning**:
  - The paper argues against the surrogate-loss tradition: logistic and hinge losses (pp. 2–3); the sigmoid surrogate of Yan et al., whose β parameter trades approximation quality against oscillating gradients (p. 3); Rudin–Schapire's pairwise surrogates, which cost O(n⁺n⁻) (p. 3); and Steck's pairwise hinge (pp. 3–4).
  - It is set apart from LDA, which is "the method to compute linear classifiers under the Gaussian assumption" on the data itself (p. 2).
  - The reference list has 13 items and **no self-citation**: the 2018 predecessor S078 is not cited, although the method extends it (p. 18).
  - No DFO or stochastic-optimization work of the group is cited.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The contribution sentence opens the introduction: "we propose a novel and efficient approach to empirical risk minimization for binary linear classification" (p. 1).
  - Some claims outrun the evidence. Early on the paper says the approach "achieves better result faster and with a method that requires almost no tuning" (p. 3). The results section says accuracy is "very similar" in almost all cases, with time savings "often" on large instances (p. 13).
  - Hedging is present in places ("We believe that this is due to the fact", p. 7).
  - Theory is labelled modestly: "theoretical justifications" (p. 2), "theoretical motivation" (p. 15).
  - The conclusion is a single paragraph. It lists advantages and gives no limits (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**:
  - **Contributions**:
    - modelling: closed-form smooth approximations of the expected 0-1 and ranking losses;
    - computational: per-iteration cost independent of n;
    - empirical: accuracy and AUC comparable to state-of-the-art methods (p. 15), obtained "in a fraction of the time" (abstract, p. 1). The time gap is largest against pairwise hinge (Table 4.6, p. 14).
  - **Limits**: none are stated in the conclusion (p. 15). Those visible in the body are:
    - normality fails on some data sets (p. 8);
    - estimated moments are poor when n is small relative to d (p. 11);
    - the objective is nonconvex, with dependence on the start point (p. 10);
    - the covtype failure (Table 4.5, p. 13);
    - only linear classifiers are covered (p. 4).
  - No future work is given.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. The structure exploited is distributional: moments are computed once, so each iteration costs O(d²) regardless of n. Moment time is reported separately (p. 2, p. 10; Table 4.5, p. 13). The paper sees "no reason to apply stochastic gradient descent" once n has left the per-iteration cost (p. 13).
- Method 2 ✅ weak evidence. L-BFGS with Wolfe line search is used unchanged. Preconditioned CG and Hessian-vector methods were tried and "did not outperform the L-BFGS in terms of time" (p. 10).
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction in practice. Competitors are compared on test accuracy and CPU time under unequal tuning: the new method's λ is fixed, logistic's λ is set by a rule, and Adam is hand-tuned (pp. 13–14). There is no equal-accuracy target and no evaluation count. The synthetic exact-versus-estimated-moments design (Table 4.2, p. 11) is the one controlled comparison.
- Research Taste mark 4 (*a guarantee comes with numbers*) ⚠ variant: numbers without a guarantee. The only theory is closed-form derivation under a distributional assumption (pp. 4–7, p. 15).
- Existing candidates, **not independent evidence** because this is the same text as S089: *smooth true target instead of convex surrogate* (pp. 1, 5–7) and *assumption on the statistic the objective uses, checked along the path* (p. 5; Figs 4.1–4.2, pp. 8–9).
- Existing candidate *report the variants that did not help* ✅ fits. Sampling-based Σ⁺⁻ estimates did worse than zeros (p. 8), second-order methods did not win on time (p. 10), and random starts were no better than the moment-based w0 (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D3] Put the distributional assumption on the low-dimensional statistic the objective actually uses (here wᵀX), argue it by a CLT for block-dependent features, and show histograms of that statistic at early and late iterates (p. 5; Figs 4.1–4.2, pp. 8–9).
- [D5] Two-tier synthetic test: solve with the *exact* generating moments and with *estimated* ones. This separates modelling error from estimation error, and shows where estimation fails (n small relative to d) (Table 4.2, p. 11).
- [D2] For a scale-invariant objective, use a soft penalty λ(1 − ‖w‖²)² instead of a sphere constraint (p. 7).
- [D2] For a nonconvex objective built from moments, start from a data-informed point, here μ⁺ orthogonalized against μ⁻ and normalized, and report that random starts did no better (p. 10).
- [D5] Plot the proposed objective and the baseline surrogate against the *true* empirical loss along the optimization path (100 points). This shows whether a surrogate tracks the target or only locates its minimizers (p. 14).
- [D4/D5] Report per-iteration complexity symbolically (O(d²) vs O(nd) vs O(dn log n)), and report the one-time preprocessing cost as its own column (p. 10; Table 4.5, p. 13).
- [D2] For large sparse data, apply the empirical covariance as Σv = (Xᵀ(Xv) − n·X̄(X̄ᵀv))/(n−1) without forming Σ (p. 10).
