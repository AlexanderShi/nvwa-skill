### S018 · Convergence of trust-region methods based on probabilistic models (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2014, DOI 10.1137/130915984; arXiv:1304.2808)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. S. Bandeira, L. N. Vicente · **Place in trajectory**: the Lehigh-era pivot from certified (deterministic) fully linear models (refs [14], [15], the 2009 book) to models that are good only with probability p. It thanks J. Blanchet for martingale discussions (p. 27); Blanchet is a later coauthor on the complexity follow-up. · **Read level**: full. Text read is arXiv v1 (9 Apr 2013), pp. 1–29; the journal version was not compared.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a cost/quality trade-off in model-based DFO. Certifying model quality is costly: "A complex mechanism of sample set maintenance is necessary" (p. 2). Random sample sets might give good models from fewer points with no maintenance (p. 2). A second entry: randomized methods that ask only for "some minor level of descent" are cheap, but their practical performance "can be very poor". SGD, by contrast, improves when the sample size grows (Byrd et al.). So the question is how much accuracy to demand of a random model (p. 2). The problem is stated as: a deterministic smooth f with exact function values, random models, and a proof that the classical trust-region (TR) method still converges (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea & algorithm design**: The model must be fully linear (gradient error ≤ κ_eg δ_k, value error ≤ κ_ef δ_k²; Def. 3.1, p. 6), but only with probability p ≥ 1/2 conditioned on the past models (Def. 3.2, p. 7). The algorithm is "a basic trust-region algorithm, with one specific modification". A step is successful, and the radius grows, only if ρ_k ≥ η1 **and** ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2 δ_k. Otherwise the step is rejected and the radius shrinks (Alg. 3.1, p. 8). The paper's rationale: the step length is proportional to ‖g_k‖, so the trust region should be of comparable size (p. 8). Reader's interpretation, not stated in the paper: the test does the job of the deterministic criticality step, which still needs a Λ-poised set (p. 23). Unchanged: Cauchy decrease, the ratio test and the radius factors (pp. 7–8). A three-way radius update closer to practice fits the same proofs (Alg. 4.1, p. 14). A second-order version uses probabilistically fully quadratic models (Def. 5.2, p. 15; Alg. 5.1, pp. 17–18).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f is C¹ with Lipschitz gradient on the enlarged level set L_enl(x0) and bounded below (Assumption 3.3, p. 8).
  - Function values are exact (p. 1). Relaxing this is left to future work (p. 27).
  - Every model realization gives a fraction of Cauchy decrease (Assumption 3.1, p. 7).
  - Model Hessians are uniformly bounded. The authors call this "introduced for convenience" and note that a large Hessian can simply be reset (Assumption 3.2, p. 7).
  - P(S_k | F^M_{k−1}) ≥ 1/2. This conditional, submartingale-like form was chosen because full independence is hard to ensure (p. 7).
  - Second order needs f ∈ C² with a Lipschitz Hessian and bounded ‖∇²f‖ (Assumption 5.1, p. 15), plus a fraction of optimal decrease (Assumption 5.2, p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - (i) Lemma 3.1 (pp. 8–9): δ_k → 0 on *every* realization, whatever the models do. The radius only grows when ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2 δ_k, and that forces a constant decrease of f.
  - (ii) The ±1 walk W_k = Σ(2·1_{S_i} − 1) is a submartingale with bounded increments, so lim sup W_k = ∞ almost surely (Thm 4.1, an extension of Durrett, pp. 9–10; used in Thm 4.2, p. 10).
  - (iii) The log-radius R_k = log_γ(δ_k/b) rises by exactly 1 on good iterations once δ_k < b, and falls by at most 1 otherwise. So r_k − r_{k0} ≥ w_k − w_{k0}, which contradicts δ_k → 0 unless lim inf ‖∇f‖ = 0 (p. 11).
  - (iv) Lemma 4.1 (p. 11): if the Bernoulli signs have conditional probability ≥ 1/2, then {Σ_good Z_i < ∞} ∩ {Σ_bad Z_i = ∞} has probability zero, via the submartingale G_k = G_{k−1} + B_k Z_k. This gives Σ_{k: ‖∇f‖ > ε} Δ_k < ∞ almost surely (Lemma 4.2, p. 12).
  - (v) The lim-type result reuses the deterministic "ascent pair" argument (Thm 4.3, pp. 12–13).
  - (vi) The second-order measure τ(x) includes ‖∇f‖/‖∇²f‖, so bounded model Hessians need not be assumed (p. 16).
  - Guarantee type: almost-sure only (lim inf and lim for first order; lim inf for second order). The paper states no rate or complexity bound anywhere.
- **D5 Experiments**: These are illustrations, not a benchmark.
  - (a) 2-D Rosenbrock. Compass search: 11307 evaluations to f = 1e−6. Random-basis direct search: 5756 to 1e−8. Random search with a 1/k step: 3724 to 1e−8. Quadratic-model TR: 62 to 1e−14 (Figs 1–2, pp. 5–6).
  - (b) Minimum-Frobenius-norm (MFN) models from 5 points on a milder Rosenbrock. A well-poised deterministic coordinate set reaches 1e−4 in 8500 evaluations; random sets reach 1e−6 in 2700. An iteration trace shows the deterministic run cycling between success and failure (p. 24).
  - (c) 10-D problem with 2 active variables. Random-sample sparse models (RSTR): 18 iterations, 494 evaluations, 4.0e−11. Greedy sparse (GSTR): 164, 185, 5.0e−8. MFN: 325, 346, 2.5e−5. RSTR's extra evaluations "can be obtained in parallel" (p. 26).
  - Function evaluations are counted and final f is reported, but at unequal final accuracies. Runs are single, with no seeds and no failure statistics.
- **D6 Positioning**: The stated contrast is with stochastic gradient approaches, where the model is "correct" only in expectation (p. 1). The paper reviews direct search, random search (Matyas, Polyak, Nesterov) and model-based TR (Powell; Conn–Scheinberg–Toint) (pp. 4–5). It criticizes random search directly: its practical usefulness is limited "by the fixed step sizes determined by the complexity analysis" and by the lack of curvature information (p. 4). It relies on Moré–Wild for the practical superiority of model-based methods (p. 5). It builds on the authors' own sparse-recovery model paper [3] (pp. 2, 25) and on Scheinberg–Toint [31]: the criticality step still needs a poised set (p. 23).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The contribution is framed as two differences from stochastic optimization: "Our method and setting differs from many stochastic optimization approaches in two principal ways" (p. 1).
  - A κ-constant glossary comes before the analysis (p. 3).
  - A pictorial method-family comparison precedes any theory (§2, pp. 4–6).
  - §6 turns the abstract condition into constructions: Gaussian sample sets, sparse recovery, nonuniform recovery and asynchronous parallel evaluation (pp. 22–27).
  - Unproved claims are hedged with "We believe" (pp. 7, 25).
  - Conjecture 5.1 comes with a full account of where the proof breaks (pp. 20–21).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical, a framework plus DFO model constructions as applications (pp. 22–27). The limits are concrete. The lim-type second-order result is only a conjecture. The second-order decrease bounds Σδ_j², not Σδ_j, and the radius need not grow at every good iteration. Letting the probability of good models increase would fix this, but "essentially leads to deterministic schemes and hence is of no interest for this paper" (p. 21). Future work: bounds for structured random matrices of quadratic models (p. 25); inexact function values at trial points (p. 27); dependent (reused) sample sets and a controlled relaxation of the submartingale property (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. Accuracy is tied to the radius (κ_eg δ_k, κ_ef δ_k²), with a fixed probability p ≥ 1/2 conditioned on the past (Defs 3.1–3.2, pp. 6–7). Exact f is the other half of the contract (p. 1). This confirms Heuristic 1 from the full text.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The method is "almost identical to the classical method" (p. 1). The single change is the ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2 δ_k condition in the success test (p. 8).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant. The random walk on the step parameter (Method 3 step 2) is already here (W_k and R_k, pp. 10–11), but it is used only for almost-sure convergence. There is no stopping time, expected complexity or tail bound. The paper is a precursor of Method 3, not an instance of it.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. Random sample sets replace deterministic geometry maintenance (pp. 2, 23–26). Gaussian sets are probabilistically fully linear by random-matrix condition-number bounds (p. 24). ℓ1 sparse recovery gives fully quadratic models from O(n (log n)^4) points when a sparse model exists (p. 25).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Function evaluations are counted, but on single illustrative runs that end at different accuracies (pp. 5–6, 24, 26). There is no equal-accuracy protocol.
- New-pattern candidate: **localized-failure conjecture.** State the result you could not prove, identify the exact inequality that fails (Σδ² vs Σδ), and explain why the obvious repair is out of scope (pp. 20–21).
- New-pattern candidate: **realization-wise safety, probability-driven progress.** Prove the safety property (δ_k → 0) for every realization, independent of model quality, and use probability only for progress (Lemma 3.1, pp. 8–9, vs Thm 4.2, pp. 10–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] When the model is only probably accurate, put a gradient-vs-radius test inside the acceptance rule. Accept the step and enlarge the radius only if ρ_k ≥ η1 and ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2 δ_k (Alg. 3.1, p. 8). Then no iteration needs a certified model.
- [D4] Split the convergence proof into two lemmas. The first holds on every sample path (δ_k → 0, pp. 8–9) and needs no probability; it is the contradiction lever. The second is probabilistic: log δ_k dominates a ±1 submartingale walk (p. 11).
- [D4] To show that bad iterations cannot dominate, build G_k = G_{k−1} + B_k Z_k, where B_k = ±1 with conditional probability ≥ 1/2. Then the event {Σ_good Z < ∞} ∩ {Σ_bad Z = ∞} is null (Lemma 4.1, p. 11). This reusable lemma applies to any argument that compares step sizes summed over good and bad iterations.
- [D3] Condition the success probability on the σ-algebra of past oracle draws instead of assuming independence, and justify the choice in one sentence (p. 7).
- [D7] Before any theory, show a 2-D picture of several method families, with evaluation counts and final f in the caption, to motivate why accurate models matter (pp. 5–6).
- [D8] When a natural extension fails, state it as a conjecture, run the proof as far as it goes, and name the inequality that breaks (pp. 20–21).

**Quotes**:
- "As a result of this particular setting, we are able to prove convergence, with probability one, of a trust-region method which is almost identical to the classical method." (p. 1)
- "We believe this condition is more reasonable than assuming complete independence of Mk from the past, which is difficult to ensure given that the current iterate, around which the model is built, and the trust-region radius depend on the algorithm history." (p. 7)

### S029 · Practical inexact proximal quasi-Newton method with global complexity analysis (Mathematical Programming 2016, DOI 10.1007/s10107-016-0997-3; arXiv:1311.6547)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: X. Tang (Lehigh PhD; his thesis is ref. [28], p. 29) · **Place in trajectory**: the Lehigh ML-optimization line, running parallel to the DFO pivot. It continues the sparse inverse covariance work (Scheinberg–Ma–Goldfarb [23]; SINCO [24]; p. 28) and produces the LHAC solver (p. 23). · **Read level**: full. Text read is arXiv v4 (14 Jul 2015), pp. 1–29; the journal version was not compared.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is F = f + g, with f convex and L-smooth and g convex, where g "has some structure that can be exploited", chiefly λ‖x‖₁ (p. 1). Specialized proximal-Newton codes (QUIC, GLMNET) build a quadratic model, run l(k) passes of coordinate descent (CD) on it, then line search (pp. 2–3). They work, but the l(k)-passes heuristic "has not yet been theoretically justified" (p. 3), and the related theory [5, 13] gives no global rates (p. 4). The requirement on g is recast. Instead of a closed-form prox, the scaled-prox subproblem (1.2) only needs to be one that "is computationally inexpensive to solve approximately" for a class of positive definite matrices H (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea & algorithm design**:
  - The line search is replaced by a prox-parameter update. Take H_k = G_k + (1/(2μ_k)) I and shrink μ until a trust-region-like sufficient decrease F(p) − F(x) ≤ ρ(Q(H, p, x) − F(x)) holds (Algs 1–2, pp. 5–6).
  - Subproblems are solved inexactly by *randomized* CD, because cyclic CD "does not have deterministic complexity bounds" (p. 18).
  - The LBFGS compact form G_k = γ_k I − Q R Qᵀ makes one CD step cost O(m) instead of O(n) (pp. 21–23).
  - Unchanged: the quadratic model + CD + sufficient-decrease skeleton of QUIC/GLMNET (pp. 2–3). ISTA is the special case G_k = 0, ρ = 1 (p. 6).
  - A greedy active-set heuristic is added for speed and not analysed (p. 22).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Assumption 1 (p. 7): a nonempty solution set; restriction to the level set X0; g Lipschitz on X0; σI ⪯ H_k ⪯ MI; a bounded distance D_X0 to the solution set. The last item is imposed "for simplicity" and holds if the level set is bounded.
  - No strong convexity and no twice-differentiability (p. 6).
  - Hessian approximations may be arbitrarily inaccurate as long as sufficient decrease holds (p. 6).
  - §3.1 uses an extra Assumption 2 on the sequence H_i/M_i, which the authors call "not verifiable" (p. 8).
  - The random version requires P{φ_k ≤ a²/k²} ≥ 1 − p, conditioned on the past (Thm 15, p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - (i) φ-subdifferential lemma: an φ-optimal prox solution satisfies the optimality condition up to an error η with ½‖η‖²_{H⁻¹} ≤ φ (Lemma 1, p. 6).
  - (ii) A first route extends the Beck–Teboulle and Schmidt et al. analysis but needs Assumption 2 (Thm 3, pp. 8–9).
  - (iii) The main route borrows a smooth-case technique from Nesterov and Cartis–Gould–Toint. Prove ΔF_k − ΔF_{k+1} ≥ c ΔF_k² and telescope 1/ΔF, giving F(x_k) − F* ≤ 2M²(D_X0 M + 2L_g)²/(ρσ³ k) (Thm 6, pp. 11–12).
  - (iv) Inexact case, a two-case dichotomy: each iteration is either error-dominated, ΔF_k ≤ b_k √φ_k, or makes progress, 1/ΔF_{k+1} − 1/ΔF_k ≥ c_k (Lemma 8, pp. 13–15).
  - (v) Start the telescoping from the last error-dominated iteration k₁ (Thm 10 proof, p. 16). This gives O(1/k) whenever φ_k ≤ a²/k², a weaker requirement than the Σ√φ_i < ∞ of [26] (Remark 11, p. 16).
  - (vi) Random inexactness: sum over the index of the last good subproblem with geometric weights. This gives E[ΔF_k] ≤ max{ba, 1/c}(2 − p)/((1 − p)(k − 1)) (Thm 15, pp. 18–19). The proof takes the probability of a pattern of good and bad subproblems to be (1 − p)pⁱ "independently of the other iteration" (p. 19), a stronger reading than the conditional assumption. The authors call the (2 − p) factor an overestimate (p. 19).
  - (vii) The Richtárik–Takáč randomized-CD bound turns the accuracy requirement into an inner-iteration count l(k) = O(n(1 + μ(H)) log(kp/M_Q)) (Lemma 16, pp. 20–21). Totals in inner iterations are given in Thms 12–13 (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - The aim is stated as validation "but not to conduct extensive comparison", in three claims: (a) the exact Hessian is not needed, (b) prox-parameter backtracking works in practice, (c) randomized CD is as good as cyclic (p. 23).
  - Solver: LHAC (C/C++). Test sets: sparse inverse covariance selection on four gene-expression sets with λ = 0.5, against QUIC; sparse logistic regression on a9a, slice, gisette and epsilon, against LIBLINEAR. LHAC-L (the same solver with Armijo line search) is an internal ablation (pp. 23–26).
  - Protocol (p. 24): F* is taken from QUIC/LIBLINEAR at tolerance 1e−7, and runs stop at relative error 1e−8. The baselines were downloaded, compiled on the same machine, and their source was modified only to log f and time. Fixed parameters: μ̄ = 1, β = 1/2, ρ = 0.01, LBFGS memory m = 10. Inner cap: (1 + ⌊k/m⌋)|I_k| randomized-CD steps.
  - Metrics: wall-clock time against log relative objective (Figs 1 and 3, pp. 25–26), plus CD steps per outer iteration (Fig. 2, p. 25).
  - Results: about 2× faster than QUIC on the two largest covariance sets (p. 25); an order of magnitude faster than LIBLINEAR on slice (p. 26); about one third faster on epsilon (pp. 26–27); LIBLINEAR's memory use "more than doubled" compared with LHAC's (p. 27). LHAC and LHAC-L are "fairly similar" (p. 25).
  - The text points to Figs 1(c)–(d) for the two largest sets, but those panels are labelled ER_692 and Arabidopsis (p. 25), so the figure reference is inconsistent.
  - No failure cases are reported. On tuning: "small values of ρ provided much better performance than values close to 1" (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents a framework that covers slightly modified versions of existing algorithms, plus a new one (abstract, p. 1).
  - It answers Byrd et al.'s proposal to call these methods SQA by making the prox term part of the model (p. 3).
  - It credits [5, 13] with superlinear local rates but no global rates (p. 4).
  - It dismisses the accelerated method [12], whose H_k − H_{k+1} ⪰ 0 condition is "very restrictive as is impractical" (p. 4).
  - It claims to improve on the inexact prox-gradient analysis [26]: a weaker accuracy requirement and fewer total inner iterations (p. 3; Remark 11, p. 16; p. 27).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Three numbered main results in the introduction (p. 3).
  - A "first" claim: "the first complete global convergence rate result" for practical inexact proximal Newton-type schemes (p. 3). The conclusion repeats it in narrower form (p. 27).
  - The natural analysis is shown first; its assumption is admitted to be unverifiable; then the alternative analysis follows (pp. 8–10).
  - Remarks interpret the constants. If M_i ≪ L(f), the constant is smaller, the "well known effect of using partial second order information" (pp. 9–10).
  - Remark 14 names the real cost measure: function and gradient evaluations, not inner iterations (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a theory (global sublinear rate, exact, inexact and randomly inexact), an algorithm and a software package, LHAC (pp. 3, 23). The limits are specific:
  - The rate is sublinear only. There is no local superlinear rate, since Hessian accuracy is not used, and no strong convexity (pp. 6, 27).
  - Working-set and active-set strategies are not analysed (pp. 3, 22).
  - The practical rule l(k) = ak + b gives "inferior complexity" in theory (p. 21).
  - Acceleration without restrictive assumptions on H_k is left to future work (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. The LBFGS low-rank structure plus the closed-form one-dimensional ℓ1 step make each CD step O(m) (pp. 22–23). The problem class itself is defined by exploitable structure in g (p. 1). This confirms the Tang–Scheinberg citation under Method 6 from the full text.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. The classical globalization, Armijo line search as used by QUIC and LIBLINEAR, is *replaced* rather than kept, because the rate analysis does not extend to it (pp. 10, 27). Cyclic CD is replaced by randomized CD for the same reason (p. 18). Experiments then show that the replacement costs nothing (LHAC vs LHAC-L, p. 25). The spirit of Method 2, change only what the proof needs, holds; the direction is reversed, since the practical method is moved toward an analysable one.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The accuracy contract is on the inner solver: φ_k ≤ a²/k² with probability ≥ 1 − p, conditioned on the past (Thm 15, p. 18). The accuracy is tied to the iteration count, not to a step size or radius.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant. The expected O(1/k) rate keeps the deterministic order, and randomness costs only the factor (2 − p)/(1 − p) (Thm 15, pp. 18–19). This is Taste 2 (deterministic parity) with the p-dependence explicit, as warning sign 6 requires. The proof, however, is a counting and geometric-series argument, not a supermartingale or stopping-time one.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The comparison metric is wall-clock time with fixed default parameters (p. 24), not evaluation counts. Remark 14 argues that evaluations dominate the cost in big-data problems (p. 18).
- New-pattern candidate: **analysable surrogate for a practical heuristic.** Swap the component that blocks the proof (line search → prox-parameter update; cyclic → randomized CD) for an analysable near-equivalent, then show experimentally that it is no worse (pp. 18, 25, 27).
- New-pattern candidate: **theory-certified inner-loop budget.** A known inner-solver rate turns the outer accuracy requirement into a number of passes l(k). This justifies a heuristic practitioners already used (pp. 3, 17, 20–21, 24).
- New-pattern candidate: **compete on constants when the order is fixed.** The O(1/k) order does not change; the case for second-order information is made through M_i vs L(f) in the constant (pp. 9–10, 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] For an inexact method, prove a per-iteration dichotomy. Either the inexactness dominates (ΔF_k ≤ b√φ_k), or the exact-case progress inequality holds (1/ΔF_{k+1} − 1/ΔF_k ≥ c). Then telescope from the last error-dominated iteration (Lemma 8 and Thm 10, pp. 13–16). This needs only φ_k ≤ a²/k², not summability of √φ_k.
- [D4] For randomly inexact subproblems, condition on the index of the last successful inner solve and sum the deterministic bound with geometric weights (1 − p)p^{i−1}. The result is the deterministic rate times (2 − p)/(1 − p) (Thm 15, pp. 18–19). Check whether your conditioning justifies the product form (p. 19).
- [D2] If the line search in your method has no rate proof, try a trust-region-like acceptance test on a prox or regularization parameter instead. Keep the line-search version as an ablation to show nothing is lost (pp. 5–6, 24–25).
- [D2] Choose the inner solver by its known complexity (randomized over cyclic CD), so that the number of inner passes can be fixed a priori from the outer accuracy requirement (pp. 18, 20–21).
- [D5] State the experiment's aim as 2–3 falsifiable claims before the plots (p. 23). Rebuild baselines on the same machine, change their code only to add logging, and take F* from the strongest baseline at a tight tolerance (p. 24).
- [D7] If the natural analysis needs an unverifiable assumption, show it briefly, say so plainly, then give the analysis that removes it (pp. 8–10).

**Quotes**:
- "The assumption above is not verifiable, hence it does not appear to be very useful." (p. 8)
- "to provide convergence rates we had to depart from some standard techniques, such as line-search, replacing it instead by a prox-parameter updating mechanism with a trust-region-like sufficient decrease condition for acceptance of iterates." (p. 27)

### S002 · SARAH: A novel method for machine learning problems using stochastic recursive gradient (ICML 2017, PMLR 70; arXiv:1703.00102)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: L. M. Nguyen, J. Liu, M. Takáč (all Lehigh ISE; Scheinberg is listed "On leave at The University of Oxford", p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the Lehigh ML-optimization line, on finite-sum variance reduction. It is the most-cited paper in this batch (961 citations per the batch list). · **Read level**: full. Text read is arXiv v2 (3 Jun 2017), pp. 1–8, plus the supplement, pp. 10–14; all proofs were read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is a finite sum, min P(w) = (1/n)Σ f_i(w), with convex, L-smooth f_i (p. 1). SGD is "too slow" and sensitive to gradient variance (p. 1), and its diminishing step sequence "needs to be hand-picked" (p. 2). The known fixes each carry a cost: SAG/SAGA store n gradients, and SVRG's variance does not fall as its inner loop runs (pp. 2, 4). The paper's entry is to combine the accumulated past information of SAGA with the occasional exact gradient of SVRG (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea & algorithm design**: The recursive estimator is v_t = ∇f_{i_t}(w_t) − ∇f_{i_t}(w_{t−1}) + v_{t−1}. SVRG instead anchors at w_0: v_t = ∇f_{i_t}(w_t) − ∇f_{i_t}(w_0) + v_0 (eqs 2–4, p. 2).
  - The estimator is deliberately biased: E[v_t | F_t] ≠ ∇P(w_t), although the total expectation matches, E[v_t] = E[∇P(w_t)] (eq. 5, p. 3).
  - Structure: an outer loop with one full gradient, an inner loop of m recursive steps, and a uniformly random inner iterate as output (Alg. 1, p. 3).
  - Memory is O(d), and m = 1 reduces to gradient descent (p. 3).
  - SARAH+ is the practical variant. It stops the inner loop once ‖v_{t−1}‖² ≤ γ‖v_0‖², caps it at m, and outputs the last iterate (Alg. 2, p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - A1: each f_i is L-smooth.
  - A2a: P is μ-strongly convex; or A2b: each f_i is μ-strongly convex.
  - A3: each f_i is convex (all on p. 3).
  - The accuracy target is E‖∇P(w_T)‖² ≤ ε (eq. 7, p. 3).
  - The learning rate is constant (Table 1, p. 2); any η < 1/L works (Remark 1, p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - (i) Descent lemma. Using aᵀb = ½(‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − ‖a − b‖²) gives Σ E‖∇P(w_t)‖² ≤ (2/η)E[P(w_0) − P*] + Σ E‖∇P(w_t) − v_t‖² − (1 − Lη)Σ E‖v_t‖², with no convexity (Lemma 1, p. 5; proof pp. 10–11).
  - (ii) MSE telescoping identity: E‖∇P(w_t) − v_t‖² = Σ_j E‖v_j − v_{j−1}‖² − Σ_j E‖∇P(w_j) − ∇P(w_{j−1})‖². It holds because the conditional mean of each *increment* v_j − v_{j−1} is the true gradient difference, even though v_j itself is biased (Lemma 2, p. 5; proof p. 11).
  - (iii) Co-coercivity (Nesterov, Thm 2.1.5) gives Σ E‖v_j − v_{j−1}‖² ≤ (ηL/(2 − ηL))(E‖v_0‖² − E‖v_t‖²) (Lemma 3, p. 5; proof pp. 11–12).
  - (iv) E‖v_t‖² contracts linearly within one inner loop, at rate 1 − (2/(ηL) − 1)μ²η² or 1 − 2μLη/(μ + L) (Thms 1a/1b, p. 4; proofs pp. 12–13).
  - (v) A one-outer-loop inequality (Thm 2, p. 5) is recursed across outer loops (Thm 3, p. 5; Thm 4, p. 6).
  - (vi) Parameters are chosen by optimizing the rate expression: the best m for a fixed σ_m (Lemma 6, p. 10). With η = 1/(2L) and m = 4.5κ, σ_m < 7/9 (Cor. 3, p. 6; proof p. 14).
  - Complexities: O((n + κ) log 1/ε) in the strongly convex case (Cor. 3, p. 6); O((n + 1/ε) log 1/ε) in the convex case (Cor. 2, p. 6); O(n + 1/ε²) for a single outer loop (Cor. 1, p. 5). All guarantees are in expectation.
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - A 2-D toy problem (n = 5 quadratics) shows SVRG's inner loop wandering near the optimum while SARAH's converges (Fig. 1, p. 4).
  - Traces of ‖v_t‖² on rcv1 compare SARAH, SVRG, SGD+ and FISTA (Fig. 2, p. 4).
  - The theoretical best learning rates and convergence rates are plotted for n = 10⁶ (Fig. 3, p. 6).
  - Main benchmark: ℓ2-regularized logistic regression with λ = 1/n on covtype, ijcnn1, news20 and rcv1 (Table 3, p. 7). Baselines are SVRG, SAG, SGD+ (step η₀/(k + 1)) and FISTA. The plots show loss residual and test error against effective passes (Fig. 5, p. 8).
  - Every method uses its "best learning rates in hindsight" (p. 7); the best (m*, η*) are tabulated (Table 4, p. 8).
  - Sensitivity: an m*/8 … 8m* sweep for SVRG and SARAH (Fig. 6, p. 8), and a γ sweep for SARAH+ with γ = 1/8 best (Fig. 4, p. 7).
  - SARAH's tuned η stays near its theoretical 1/L, while the other methods' tuned rates exceed their theoretical limits (p. 8).
  - No failure cases are shown, and no seeds or run-to-run variance are reported.
- **D6 Positioning**: SARAH is framed as a new member of the fixed-learning-rate variance-reduction class (SAG/SAGA, SDCA, SVRG, MISO, S2GD; p. 2). Tables place it against GD, SGD, SVRG and SAG/SAGA on complexity, fixed learning rate and storage (Tables 1–2, p. 2). The main contrast is with SVRG. SARAH's inner loop converges linearly, "the property that SVRG does not possess" (abstract, p. 1). SVRG needs η < 1/(4L), SARAH only η < 1/L, and σ_m < α_m (Remark 1, p. 6).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Contributions are listed as five bullets (p. 2).
  - A comparison table uses ✓/✗ columns for fixed learning rate and low storage cost (p. 2).
  - An intuition figure comes before the theorems (Figs 1–2, p. 4).
  - The central qualitative claim, "more stable", recurs throughout (pp. 2, 4, 8).
  - All proofs are in the supplement (pp. 10–14).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are an algorithm, its theory and a practical variant (pp. 2, 7). There is no limitations section. The paper admits that SARAH+'s γ "is another parameter that requires tuning", adding that performance was robust to it (p. 7). The conclusion claims the same complexity as SVRG and SAG with a smaller constant than SVRG (p. 8). There is no future-work statement (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✗ contradiction. SARAH is a new estimator with a constant, tuned learning rate. It is not a classical adaptive method re-analysed (pp. 2–3, 7). The SKILL's Taste 3 and warning sign 1 do not describe this line, so Method 2 should be scoped to the adaptive and DFO line.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The estimator error is controlled in mean square, with no probability-p contract. The error is still tied to step lengths through Lemma 3 (p. 5), which echoes Method 1's idea of accuracy scaled to the step. The paper chooses a *biased* estimator on purpose (eq. 5, p. 3). This is evidence that the stance against assuming unbiased estimates (warning sign 2) predates the 2024 papers the SKILL cites.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant. The bounds are on the expected squared gradient norm of a random output iterate (pp. 3, 5). The deterministic sanity check is explicit: m = 1 is gradient descent (p. 3). There is no stopping-time or high-probability analysis.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Cost is counted in effective passes, that is, oracle calls (Fig. 5, p. 8), which fits Method 5 step 5. But every method is tuned per dataset "in hindsight" (p. 7; Table 4, p. 8), which the Workflow D checkpoint would flag. The paper answers with an argument that SARAH is easier to tune (p. 8) and with sensitivity sweeps (Fig. 6, p. 8).
- Method 6 ✅ weak evidence. Like the other variance-reduction methods it cites, SARAH uses "the specific finite-sum form" to combine deterministic and stochastic aspects (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: **theory-derived stopping rule.** The quantity proved to contract, ‖v_t‖² (Thms 1a/1b, p. 4), becomes SARAH+'s practical inner-loop test (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: **compete on constants and admissible step when the order ties.** The order O((n + κ) log 1/ε) equals SVRG's, but SARAH allows a larger η and has a smaller rate constant (Remarks 1–2, p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] To analyse a biased recursive estimator, do not look for unbiasedness. Write its MSE as a telescoping sum of increment variances, using only that each increment is conditionally unbiased for the true gradient difference (Lemma 2, p. 5; p. 11).
- [D4] Bound the increment variances by co-coercivity so that they telescope against ‖v_t‖² (Lemma 3, pp. 11–12). The error term then cancels against the −(1 − Lη)Σ‖v_t‖² term of the descent lemma.
- [D2] If you prove that some computable quantity contracts (here ‖v_t‖²), turn it into an adaptive loop-termination rule and cap it with a safe maximum (SARAH+, p. 7).
- [D5] If you tune per dataset, publish the best-parameter table for every method (Table 4, p. 8). Sweep the key parameter from ×1/8 to ×8 for your method and the main competitor (Fig. 6, p. 8). Report whether the tuned values sit near the theoretical ones (p. 8).
- [D7] Open with a 2-D toy trajectory (n = 5) that shows the qualitative difference your theorems will prove (Fig. 1, p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "Observe that in SVRG, vt is an unbiased estimator of the gradient, while it is not true for SARAH." (p. 3)
- "We show that the variance of the steps inside the inner loop goes to zero, thus SARAH is theoretically more stable and reliable than SVRG." (p. 2)

### S024 · Stochastic recursive gradient algorithm for nonconvex optimization (arXiv preprint 2017, arXiv:1705.07261)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: L. M. Nguyen, J. Liu, M. Takáč · **Place in trajectory**: a short follow-up to S002, which it cites as "To appear in ICML" (p. 9). It extends SARAH to nonconvex losses with mini-batches and stays in the ML-optimization line. · **Read level**: full. Text read is arXiv v1 (20 May 2017), pp. 1–9, plus the supplement, pp. 11–15; all proofs and extra plots were read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Nonconvex finite sums, such as neural networks, while most variance-reduction methods are "designed and analyzed for the convex/strongly convex cases" (p. 2). The paper seeks stationarity, not w* (p. 1). The entry is through assumptions: the SGD analysis of Ghadimi–Lan needs ‖∇f_i‖ ≤ σ, and σ "can be large", while SARAH needs only L-smoothness (p. 2). Complexity is counted in incremental first-order oracle (IFO) calls (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea & algorithm design**: Mini-batch SARAH: v_t = (1/b)Σ_{i∈I_t}[∇f_i(w_t) − ∇f_i(w_{t−1})] + v_{t−1}, with I_t sampled without replacement (Fig. 2, p. 3). The inner loop alone is analysed for general nonconvex f, and the full method for τ-gradient-dominated f (pp. 4–5). The paper traces SARAH to "momentum SGD, SAG/SAGA, SVRG and L-BFGS" (p. 2). With m = 1, or s = 1 and b = n, SARAH is GD and recovers GD's rate (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Each f_i is L-smooth (Assumption 1, p. 4). The linear rate additionally needs τ-gradient dominance, P(w) − P* ≤ τ‖∇P(w)‖² (Assumption 2, p. 4); this covers a strongly convex P with nonconvex f_i (p. 4). No bounded-gradient assumption is made (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - Lemmas 1–2 are carried over from S002 and extended to b ≥ 1 (p. 4; proofs pp. 11–12).
  - New Lemma 3: E‖∇P(w_t) − v_t‖² ≤ (1/b)((n − b)/(n − 1))L²η² Σ_j E‖v_{j−1}‖². It comes from an exact second-moment computation for sampling without replacement (p. 5; proof pp. 12–13).
  - Step-size device: η = 2/(L(√(1 + 4m(n − b)/(b(n − 1))) + 1)) is the root of the quadratic that makes the error-minus-descent bracket nonpositive (Thm 1, p. 5; p. 13).
  - Inner loop: E‖∇P(w̃)‖² ≤ 2[P(w_0) − P*]/(η(m + 1)), i.e. O(n + L²/ε²) IFO calls (Thm 1 and Cor. 1, p. 5).
  - Gradient dominance chains into a per-outer-loop contraction of 2τ/(η(m + 1)), giving O((n + L²τ²) log 1/ε) (Thm 2 and Cor. 2, p. 5; proof p. 14).
  - Mini-batches: a larger b permits a larger η, but the total IFO count grows (Remark 1, p. 6; Cor. 4 and Remark 2, p. 6).
  - Guarantee type: in expectation, for a randomly chosen output iterate (p. 2).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Networks with one hidden layer: MNIST with n_h = 300 and λ = 1e−4; CIFAR10 with n_h = 100 and λ = 1e−3. Softmax cross-entropy loss, Glorot initialization, batch size b = 10 (p. 7).
  - Baselines: SVRG, plus AdaGrad and SGD-M from TensorFlow (p. 7).
  - Metrics: training loss and test error against effective passes (Fig. 4, p. 8).
  - Tuning: parameters were scanned over "reasonable ranges" for the best training error (Table 2, p. 8). The SARAH+ ratio is held at 0.7 for all runs (p. 8), although the convex study found γ = 1/8 best (S002, p. 7); the change is not discussed.
  - Supplement: b = 20 on MNIST (Fig. 5, p. 14) and m-sensitivity on CIFAR10 (Fig. 6, pp. 14–15). Fig. 6 plots P(w) − P(w*) for a nonconvex network, and the paper does not say how P(w*) was obtained (p. 15).
  - No failure cases or seeds are reported.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper compares against nonconvex SVRG (Reddi et al.; Allen-Zhu–Hazan) and Natasha (p. 2), with a GD/SGD/SVRG/SARAH table (Table 1, p. 2). It is candid about the trade-off: SVRG's ε-dependence is better, its n-dependence worse. SVRG's bound contains an unknown constant ν "whose magnitude is not clear and can be quite small" (p. 2). A footnote notes that AdaGrad and SGD-M have no nonconvex theory "as far as we know" (p. 7).
- **D7 Writing**: A short paper: 9 pages of body, bullet contributions (p. 3), all proofs in the supplement. Hedging is uneven. Page 2 concedes SVRG's better ε-dependence and calls the full-SARAH nonconvex rate open (p. 2). The conclusion then says the inner loop "achieves the best sublinear convergence rate in the literature" (p. 9). The two claims are inconsistent.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an analysis of an existing algorithm, plus neural-network experiments (pp. 2–3). Stated limits: the full SARAH nonconvex rate is open (p. 2), and SARAH+ for nonconvex problems rests on an assumed analogy with the convex case (p. 7). There is no future-work section (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✗ contradiction. As in S002, this is a new estimator with a constant, tuned step (pp. 3, 8). The paper does not re-analyse a classical adaptive method.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant. The bounds are on the expected E‖∇P‖² of a random output (p. 2). Deterministic parity is checked by recovering GD's rate at b = n (pp. 4, 6), in line with Taste 2. There is no high-probability or stopping-time analysis.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Cost is counted in IFO calls, i.e. effective passes (pp. 2, 8), consistent with Method 5 step 5. But the methods are tuned by per-dataset scans (p. 8) rather than run with fixed defaults.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The oracle is the IFO. The estimator's MSE is tied to step lengths through the (n − b)/(n − 1) factor (Lemma 3, p. 5). There is no probabilistic contract.
- New-pattern candidate: **compete on assumptions when the order ties.** The ε-order equals SGD's, but without the bounded-gradient assumption and with better constants (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: **theory-derived stopping rule.** SARAH+ is reused in the nonconvex case, with the analogy flagged as an assumption (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] For mini-batches sampled without replacement, compute the second moment exactly. The factor (1/b)(n − b)/(n − 1) interpolates between SGD (b = 1) and GD (b = n), so the GD special case falls out of the bound (Lemma 3 proof, pp. 12–13).
- [D4] Choose the step size as the positive root of the quadratic that makes the accumulated-error term cancel the −(1 − Lη)Σ‖v‖² descent term. The result is a clean bound with no leftover error term (Thm 1, p. 5; p. 13).
- [D4] Use gradient dominance, P − P* ≤ τ‖∇P‖², to turn a one-epoch sublinear bound into a per-epoch contraction 2τ/(η(m + 1)) < 1 (Thm 2, p. 5; p. 14). It gives linear rates without convexity.
- [D6] In the comparison table, say plainly where the competitor wins (ε-dependence) and where you win (n-dependence, constants, assumptions) (p. 2). Keep the conclusion consistent with that table; p. 9 is the counter-example.
- [D5] Holding an adaptive variant's parameter fixed across datasets (SARAH+ ratio 0.7) while tuning competitors (p. 8) can support a robustness claim, but only if the fixed value is justified.

**Quotes**:
- "Convergence rate of the full SARAH algorithm for general nonconvex functions remains an open question." (p. 2)
- "Under the assumption that similar behavior happens in the nonconvex case, instead of tuning the inner loop size for SARAH, we believe that a proper choice of the ratio γ below, the automatic loop termination can give superior or competitive performances." (p. 7)

## Batch cross-paper notes (for synthesis; not a card)

- **Scope of Method 2.** Keeping the classical method holds for the DFO/TR paper: S018 is "almost identical to the classical method" (p. 1). It is reversed in S029, where the practical line search is replaced by an analysable prox-parameter update (p. 27). It does not hold for the SARAH line, which introduces a new estimator with a constant, tuned step (S002 pp. 2–3, 7; S024 p. 8). Suggest scoping Method 2 and Taste 3 to the adaptive and DFO line.
- **Biased estimators before 2024.** S002 analyses a deliberately biased estimator through an MSE telescoping identity (p. 3, p. 5), and S024 extends this to mini-batches (p. 5). This is earlier evidence for warning sign 2 (assuming unbiased, well-behaved estimates) than the 2024 papers the SKILL cites.
- **Deterministic parity with randomness costing only constants** already appears outside DFO. In S029, randomly inexact subproblems keep O(1/k), at a cost of the factor (2 − p)/(1 − p) (Thm 15, pp. 18–19).
- **No high-probability bound in this batch.** S018 is almost-sure only (pp. 10–13); S029, S002 and S024 are in expectation only (S029 p. 18; S002 p. 3; S024 p. 2). This fits Taste 7 (guarantees strengthen over time).
- **Candidate patterns seen in 3 papers.** Both span 2 independent research lines, since S002 and S024 share the SARAH idea.
  - Theory-derived inner-loop budget or stopping rule: S029 (pp. 17, 20–21, 24), S002 (p. 7), S024 (p. 7).
  - Compete on constants or assumptions when the order ties: S029 (pp. 9–10), S002 (p. 6), S024 (p. 2).
