# Paper cards · batch k01 (core · book material)

Batch intent: The 2009 textbook *Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization* (Conn, Scheinberg, Vicente; S001) has no open full text, so the skill knew it only from its SIAM description (abstract card in `a2-01`). This batch reads the six open items hosted on the authors' book page (http://www.mat.uc.pt/~lnv/idfo/): the authors' table of contents, their two errata lists, their addendum on quadratic-regression error bounds, and the reviews in *Mathematics of Computation* and *SIAM Review*. The same page lists a third review (MAA Reviews, 24 June 2009); its link returned 404 on 2026-09-27 and it was not read. Three questions. (a) What does the authors' own organisation of the field say about what they think matters and in what order: what comes first, what gets a whole chapter, what is left out? This is stated methodology at the level of a syllabus. (b) What do the errata and the addendum show about how the authors correct and complete published theory? (c) How did two peers read the book: strengths, criticisms, position? Every item is checked against the Methods, Heuristics, Research Taste, Inner Tensions and Honest Boundary of `katya-scheinberg/SKILL.md`.

### B001 · Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization — table of contents (SIAM, MPS-SIAM Series on Optimization 8, 2009; book front matter, no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, L. N. Vicente · **Position in trajectory**: IBM era. The book consolidates the 1997–2009 deterministic model-based DFO line [cards S004, S008, S016, S025, S007] five years before the probabilistic-model pivot [card S018, 2014]. Every TOC page carries the build stamp `idfo 2008/11/17` (pp. 1–3), so this is the contents as typeset in November 2008. **Read**: full (all three TOC pages, every line). Only titles and start pages exist here, so anything said about a chapter beyond its title is marked *title only*. The extraction dropped the Λ glyph from three titles (§§3.3, 4.3 and 5.2 print as `-poisedness`, pp. 1–2); reviewer B005 writes Λ-poisedness (B005 p. 2).

- **D1 Problem & entry** (title level): the book makes the case for DFO, and states its limits, before any tool. Chapter 1 has §1.1 Why derivative-free optimization (book p. 1), §1.2 Examples of problems where derivatives are unavailable (book p. 3), §1.3 Limitations of derivative-free optimization (book p. 5), §1.4 How derivative-free algorithms should work (book p. 7) and §1.5 A short summary of the book (book p. 11) (p. 1). A section on the *limitations* of the whole field comes before the first method. Separately, reviewer B006 valued that the book teaches the methods' limitations throughout (B006 p. 1); his remark is about the whole book, not about §1.3. Errata items 1–2 show that §1.4 runs example algorithms, including a Nelder–Mead failure, on book pp. 9–10 (B002 p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: open a monograph or thesis with the case for the field *and* a section on its limitations, before the first tool (p. 1).

- **D2 Architecture** (the core of this card; page counts from the TOC start pages, pp. 1–3):

  | Part | Chapters | Book pages | Length (pp.) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Front | Ch. 1 Introduction | 1–12 | 12 |
  | I Sampling and modeling | Ch. 2–6 | 13–112 | ≈100 |
  | II Frameworks and algorithms | Ch. 7–11 | 113–226 | ≈114 |
  | III Review of other topics | Ch. 12–13 | 227–250 | ≈24 |
  | Appendix: Software for derivative-free optimization | — | 251–254 | 4 |
  | Bibliography | — | 255–270 | 16 |

  - **Theory of models and geometry comes first.** All of Part I, about 100 pages, is about sample sets and models; the only algorithms in it are geometry-improvement algorithms (§§6.2–6.4), and no optimization framework appears before Part II (pp. 1–2). The part titles give the order: *Sampling and modeling*, then *Frameworks and algorithms*.
  - **Part I is ordered by model class, with one section skeleton reused.** Ch. 2 linear models (book pp. 15–34) → Ch. 3 interpolating nonlinear models (35–56) → Ch. 4 regression nonlinear models (57–72) → Ch. 5 underdetermined interpolating models (73–88) → Ch. 6 ensuring well poisedness (89–112) (pp. 1–2). Chapters 3 and 4 run the same four sections: basic concepts → Lagrange polynomials → Λ-poisedness → condition number as a measure of well poisedness (§§3.1–3.4, §§4.1–4.4, p. 1). Ch. 5 repeats Lagrange polynomials and Λ-poisedness (§5.2) and adds minimum Frobenius norm models (§5.3) (p. 2).
  - **Direct search and model-based sampling share Ch. 2.** Positive spanning sets and positive bases (§2.1), gradient estimates used in direct search (§2.2), linear interpolation and regression models (§2.3), their error bounds (§2.4) and simplex gradients (§2.6) sit in one chapter (p. 1). *Title only*: the chapter treats both method families as users of sampling geometry. The errata show how delicate this bridge is: the identity between a simplex gradient and a linear-regression gradient on book p. 33 needed a qualifier (B002 p. 2).
  - **Geometry gets a whole chapter and recurs in titles.** Ch. 6, *Ensuring well poisedness and suitable derivative-free models* (book pp. 89–112, 24 pages), holds §6.1 Fully linear and fully quadratic models (book p. 90), well poisedness via Lagrange polynomials (§6.2) and via pivotal algorithms (§6.3), practical considerations of geometry-improvement algorithms (§6.4) and the regression and minimum-Frobenius-norm cases (§6.5) (p. 2). Eight section titles contain *poisedness* (§§3.3, 3.4, 4.3, 4.4, 5.2, 6.2, 6.3, 6.5), and §2.5 (other geometrical concepts) and §6.4 (geometry improvement) add two more on geometry (pp. 1–2).
  - **Part II runs direct search → simplex-gradient line search → trust region** (pp. 2–3):
    - Ch. 7, directional direct search (book pp. 115–140, 26 pages): coordinate search, a framework, global convergence in the C¹ and the nonsmooth case, simple decrease with integer lattices, the mesh adaptive direct-search method, sufficient decrease.
    - Ch. 8, simplicial direct search (22 pages): Nelder–Mead, its properties, and a globally convergent variant (§8.3, book pp. 149–161, the chapter's longest section).
    - Ch. 9, line search based on simplex derivatives (10 pages), with §9.3 Analysis for noise and §9.4 the implicit-filtering algorithm.
    - Ch. 10, trust-region methods based on derivative-free models (book pp. 173–206, 34 pages, the longest chapter).
    - Ch. 11, trust-region interpolation-based methods (20 pages).
  - **The trust-region part separates the framework from its instances.** Ch. 10 goes framework basics (§10.1) → *Conditions on the trust-region models* (§10.2) → derivative-free trust-region methods, first order (§10.3) → global convergence to first-order points (§10.4) → the same pair for second order (§§10.5–10.6) → model accuracy in larger concentric balls (§10.7) → the trust-region subproblem (§10.8) (p. 3). Only then does Ch. 11 present concrete methods: common features (§11.1), the "DFO" approach (§11.2, book pp. 208–211), Powell's methods (§11.3, book pp. 211–215) and wedge methods (§11.4, book pp. 215–225, the chapter's longest section) (p. 3). "DFO" is the name of the open-source code that the book blurb credits to Scheinberg (`03-process-evidence.md`, DFO row); the TOC itself names no author.
  - Transferable technique: put the shared analytical toolkit first, give each model class the same section skeleton, and state the algorithm as a framework with explicit *conditions on the models* before instantiating it with concrete methods (pp. 1–3).

- **D3 Assumptions & model** (title level): smoothness is the default. Nonsmoothness appears once, for direct search (§7.4 Global convergence in the nonsmooth case, book p. 124) (p. 2). Noise appears in one section title (§9.3 Analysis for noise, book p. 167) (p. 2). Constraints appear only in Part III (Ch. 13, book pp. 241–250) (p. 3). No title mentions randomness, probability or stochastic models (pp. 1–3).

- **D4 Analysis devices named in titles**: positive spanning sets and positive bases (§2.1); Lagrange polynomials (§§3.2, 4.2, 5.2); Λ-poisedness (§§3.3, 4.3, 5.2); the condition number as a measure of well poisedness (§§3.4, 4.4); pivotal algorithms (§6.3); fully linear and fully quadratic models (§6.1); integer lattices for simple decrease (§7.5) against imposed sufficient decrease (§7.7); model accuracy in larger concentric balls (§10.7) (pp. 1–3). Every guarantee named is *global convergence*: to first-order points (§§7.3, 9.2, 10.4), in the nonsmooth case (§7.4), to second-order points (§10.6), and a globally convergent Nelder–Mead variant (§8.3) (pp. 2–3). No title contains complexity, rate or worst case.

- **D5 Experiments**: none at title level. No chapter or section is about numerical comparison or benchmarking, and software gets a 4-page appendix (book pp. 251–254) (p. 3). Reviewer B006 says the introduction holds the book's only comparison between methods (B006 p. 1).

- **D6 Positioning** (title level):
  - Named methods get their own sections: Nelder–Mead (§§8.1–8.2), the mesh adaptive direct-search method (§7.6), implicit filtering (§9.4), Powell's methods (§11.3) and wedge methods (§11.4) (pp. 2–3). Powell's methods sit next to the "DFO" approach in one chapter, as instances of the Ch. 10 framework (p. 3).
  - Direct search (Ch. 7–8, 48 pages) and trust region (Ch. 10–11, 54 pages) get comparable space (pp. 2–3).
  - Surrogates get a chapter of their own, including *Rigorous optimization frameworks to handle surrogate models* (§12.2) (p. 3).

- **D7 Writing & organisation**:
  - Exercises close every chapter except Ch. 1, 11 and 13 (§§2.7, 3.5, 4.6, 5.5, 6.7, 7.9, 8.5, 9.7, 10.10, 12.3; pp. 1–3). Both reviewers judge them mostly extensions of the theory (B005 p. 2; B006 p. 2).
  - Notes-and-references sections close Ch. 4–11 (§§4.5, 5.4, 6.6, 7.8, 8.4, 9.6, 10.9, 11.5), not Ch. 2, 3, 12 or 13 (pp. 1–3).
  - Transferable technique: reuse one section skeleton across variants (here Ch. 3 and Ch. 4) so the reader sees exactly what changes from one model class to the next (p. 1).

- **D8 Contribution & what is left out** (title level):
  - Contribution: a textbook organisation of DFO in which model quality (geometry) organises Part I and trust-region frameworks with explicit model conditions form the longest block of Part II (pp. 1–3).
  - Left out or compressed at title level (pp. 1–3): complexity or rates; probabilistic or stochastic models; noise beyond §9.3; one-dimensional methods (confirmed by reviewer B005, B005 p. 3); problem structure such as least squares (the DFLS paper followed in 2010 [card S023]); machine learning; constraints (Part III only); global optimization and mixed-integer problems (one section, §13.3, book pp. 249–250); numerical benchmarking; software beyond the appendix.

### B002 · Errata to Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization (05/17/2015), first printing (author-maintained errata sheet, 3 pp., no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, L. N. Vicente · **Position in trajectory**: dated 17 May 2015, six years after publication and a year after the probabilistic-model pivot [card S018]. The header names the book and identifies the first printing (p. 1). The sheet is hosted on the book's page (URL in `works.json`). **Read**: full (all 18 items and the acknowledgement).

- **D4/D3 What was corrected** (18 items; section numbers from the B001 TOC):

  | Kind | Items | Where |
  |---|---|---|
  | Theorem statement, proof and constants | 3 (Theorem 2.13 re-proved, with new constants), 11 (the constants of the quadratic-regression bounds) | §2.4, book p. 29; §4.4, book p. 69 |
  | Proof wording and an exercise | 5 (a claim that a step is obvious replaced by a pointer to arguments already seen; Quote 2), 6 (Exercise 10 now asks the reader to show (2.9) first) | §§2.6–2.7, book pp. 33–34 |

  (pp. 1–3)
  - **By part**: 2 items in Ch. 1, 13 in Part I (Ch. 2: 4, Ch. 3: 4, Ch. 4: 1, Ch. 5: 1, Ch. 6: 3), 1 in Part II (Ch. 7) and 2 in Part III (pp. 1–3). No item touches Ch. 8–11, the Nelder–Mead, line-search and trust-region chapters.
  - **The substantive item is 3** (pp. 1–2). Theorem 2.13 (error bounds for linear regression) is re-proved for the model with a free intercept, m = c + gᵀy:
    - with y⁰ = 0, the coefficient error equals M†r, where r is the Taylor residual with |r_i| ≤ (ν/2)Δ² (p. 1);
    - the scaling identity (2.9), M† = diag(1, (1/Δ)I) M̂†, separates the radius from the geometry (p. 1);
    - the result is κ_eg = ν(1 + p^{1/2}‖M̂†‖/2) and κ_ef = 2κ_eg + ν/2 on the ball B(y⁰; Δ) (p. 2).
    - The erratum says exactly when the book's original statement was right: for the model anchored at the centre, m(y) = f(y⁰) + (y − y⁰)ᵀg, whose g coincides with the regression simplex gradient of book p. 33 (p. 1; Quote 1). Items 4 and 6 follow from this (p. 2).
  - **Items 1–2** correct the introduction's illustrative runs (book pp. 9–10). The unique minimizer at (0, 0) belongs to the *underlying smooth function* 10x₁² + 2x₂² + 4x₁x₂, not to the plotted function; and Nelder–Mead did not simply fail: it is trapped at a spurious minimizer for one initial point (p. 1). (Reviewer B005 says the introduction runs key algorithms on Rosenbrock's function and a perturbed quadratic, B005 p. 1.)
  - **The Λ items change together**: the ball in the text for Figs 3.1–3.4 becomes a Euclidean ball with the points in a neighbourhood of it (item 7); the caption values become 294, 5324 and 492624 (items 8–10); and book p. 98 now gives 5324 directly instead of 21296 plus a factor-4 scaling argument (items 13–14) (pp. 2–3). The sheet does not say why the values changed.

- **D7 How the corrections are written**: each item gives the book page, usually with the line (negative numbers count from below), the old text in quotation marks where a sentence changes, and the corrected version (pp. 1–3). The replacement for Theorem 2.13 includes a proof sketch, a footnote on the Moore–Penrose inverse, and the condition under which the old statement holds (pp. 1–2). The sheet ends by thanking all readers and naming the four who reported errors: Griewank, Hare, Le Digabel and Vaz (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when correcting a theorem, state under which extra condition the original statement remains true, give the corrected constants, and say where the reader can check the proof (p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: replace a claim that a step is obvious by a pointer to the argument that proves it (item 5, p. 2; Quote 2).

- **D8 What this says about the practice** (inference, labelled): the authors keep a public, itemised errata sheet per printing (with B003), six years after publication, crediting readers by name (p. 3). The corrections concentrate where the book is most technical, in the model error bounds and the Λ examples of Part I; none is listed for the convergence chapters (pp. 1–3). The sheet does not say whether each correction came from the authors' rereading or from the named readers.

**Method links**:
- **Honest Boundary ✅ evidence** (the neutral lesson *check that tables, text and theorem statements agree before submitting*): the authors' own sheet restates and re-proves a theorem for the model form it is used with, noting that the printed statement holds only for the model anchored at f(y⁰) (item 3; the book's original proof was not read); gives corrected Λ values in three figure captions, without saying why they changed (items 8–10); and replaces a passage that reconciled two numbers by an argument with the corrected number (items 13–14) (pp. 1–3). These are the authors' published corrections, not reading notes, so they can serve as evidence of practice.
- **Method 4 ✅ evidence** (weak): the corrected Theorem 2.13 has Method 4 step 2's form, error ≤ constant × radius (Δ for the gradient, Δ² for the value), with the constant = Lipschitz constant × ‖M̂†‖, the conditioning of the scaled sample matrix (p. 2). 13 of the 18 corrections fall in Part I's geometry and model machinery (pp. 1–3).
- **Heuristic 9 ⚠ variant** (co-authored textbook): the geometry constant Λ is computed on toy sample sets and printed in the captions (items 8–10, 13–15), which made the numbers checkable and correctable (pp. 2–3). These are illustrations of Λ-poisedness, not tests of a theorem's threshold.
- **Method 5 ⚠ variant** (deterministic precursor, weak): item 4's identity (a simplex gradient is the gradient of the linear regression model anchored at f(y⁰)) treats a direct-search estimate and a model gradient as one object. S006 and D003 later turn this unification into a parametrized family of estimators [cards S006, pp. 2–3; D003]. The erratum shows the identity needs the anchoring condition (p. 2).
- **New-pattern candidate**: *public, itemised self-correction that states when the original statement remains valid* (B002 with B003 and B004; one practice of one author team, so it stays a candidate) (pp. 1–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D7] Keep an itemised errata sheet: page and line, old text, corrected text; for a theorem, the corrected proof and the condition under which the old statement holds (pp. 1–2).
- [D4] Before stating a regression error bound, fix the model form: a free intercept (c + gᵀy) and a model anchored at the centre (f(y⁰) + gᵀ(y − y⁰)) are not interchangeable; the erratum keeps the book's statement for the anchored model and gives a new proof and new constants for the free-intercept model (pp. 1–2).
- [D7] When two printed numbers disagree, fix the number at its source rather than adding a sentence that reconciles them (items 13–14, p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "note, however, that the precise statement of Theorem 2.13 as it stands in the book would be valid if the regression model is of the form" (p. 1)
- "Corrected version: It can be proved using arguments already seen" (p. 2)

### B004 · Derivation of Error Bounds for Quadratic Regression (01/27/2011) — addendum to Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization (author-hosted note, 2 pp., no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, L. N. Vicente · **Position in trajectory**: January 2011, between the book (2009) and the dated errata (2015). The header repeats the book's bibliographic block in the same format as the errata sheets (p. 1). Hosted on the book's page (URL in `works.json`). **Read**: full (both pages, every equation).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: the note supplies the derivation of the book's Theorem 4.13, the error bounds for quadratic regression models based on the condition number of a scaled regression matrix (p. 1; Quote 1). It does not say why the derivation was left out of the book or why it was published separately. Context from other cards (inference, labelled):
  - the 2008 journal paper on regression models stated its Λ-based regression bounds without proofs, as straightforward adaptations of the interpolation proofs [card S025, p. 14];
  - B002 item 11 later corrects the constants printed on book p. 69 (§4.4 per the TOC, B001 p. 1) to exactly the constants derived here, with ‖Σ̂⁻¹‖ where the note prints ‖M†‖ (B002 p. 3).
  - The note therefore fills an omitted proof and, at the same time, fixes the constants of a printed theorem.

- **D2 Key idea**: treat the regression coefficients as a least-squares solve and push the Taylor residual through the pseudo-inverse. The model is m(y) = c + gᵀy + ½yᵀHy with a free intercept, and M = M(φ̄, Y) is the regression matrix in the canonical basis φ̄ (p. 1).

- **D3 Assumptions**: ∇²f Lipschitz with constant ν₂ > 0; y⁰ = 0 without loss of generality, by the argument at the end of the proof of the book's Theorem 3.16 (Quote 2); M has full column rank, so M† = (MᵀM)⁻¹Mᵀ (footnote 1); the scaled sample set Ŷ lies in B(0; 1) (p. 1).

- **D4 Proof devices** (pp. 1–2):
  1. **Taylor residual**: the second-order Taylor coefficients at y⁰ fit the samples up to r, M[f(y⁰); ∇f(y⁰); vec(∇²f(y⁰))] − f(Y) = r, with |r_i| ≤ (ν/2)Δ³ (p. 1).
  2. **Pseudo-inverse identity (0.1)**: the coefficient error [f(y⁰) − c; ∇f(y⁰) − g; vec(∇²f(y⁰)) − vec(H)] equals M†r (p. 1).
  3. **Diagonal scaling (0.2)**: M† = diag(1, (1/Δ)I_n, (1/Δ²)I_p̄) M̂†, with p̄ = n(n + 1)/2 and M̂ built on the scaled set Ŷ (p. 1). Each block of the coefficient error inherits its own power of Δ, and the geometry enters only through the norm of the scaled pseudo-inverse.
  4. **Block bounds**: the Hessian coefficients are off by O(Δ) with factor p̄^{1/2}ν₂/2 · ‖M†‖, and ‖∇²f(y⁰) − H‖ is at most √2 times that (0.3); the gradient at y⁰ is off by O(Δ²) with factor n^{1/2}ν₂/2 · ‖M†‖ (0.4) (pp. 1–2).
  5. **From the centre to the whole ball**: a mean-value step, ∇f(y) = ∇f(y⁰) + ∇²f(y⁰ + ty)y with t ∈ (0, 1), gives (p. 2)
     - ‖∇²f(y) − H‖ ≤ (ν₂ + √2 p̄^{1/2}ν₂/2 · ‖M†‖) Δ,
     - ‖∇f(y) − ∇m(y)‖ ≤ (ν₂ + ((n^{1/2} + √2 p̄^{1/2})/2) ν₂‖M†‖) Δ²,
     - |f(y) − m(y)| ≤ (ν₂/2 + (1/2 + n^{1/2}/2 + √2 p̄^{1/2}/4) ν₂‖M†‖) Δ³.
  - The result has the fully quadratic orders Δ, Δ², Δ³ for Hessian, gradient and value, each constant being Lipschitz constant × conditioning of the scaled matrix × an explicit dimension factor (p. 2).
  - The same device, in the linear case, is the rewritten proof of Theorem 2.13 in B002 item 3, and the corrected Exercise 10 asks the reader to prove the scaling identity (2.9) (B002 pp. 1–2).
  - Reading notes (not findings): the residual bound prints ν where the assumption names ν₂ (p. 1); the bounds print ‖M†‖ where, after the scaling (0.2), the scaled M̂† appears to be meant, and the hat may have been lost in extraction (pp. 1–2). How the componentwise residual bound yields the factors p̄^{1/2} and n^{1/2} is not written out and was not checked here.
  - Transferable technique: for any least-squares model, write the coefficient error as pseudo-inverse × Taylor residual, factor the radius out with a diagonal scaling, and bound each block separately; the geometry then appears only as ‖M̂†‖ (pp. 1–2).

- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D6 Positioning**: no citations; the only references are to the book's own Theorems 3.16 and 4.13 and its equation (3.1) (pp. 1–2).
- **D7 Writing**: one sentence of motivation, then equations only, numbered (0.1)–(0.4); a footnote on the Moore–Penrose inverse, word for word the one in B002 item 3, is the only aside (p. 1; B002 p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: completes a printed theorem's proof and makes its constants explicit (pp. 1–2). Limits: deterministic and smooth (Lipschitz Hessian), full-column-rank M; noise is not treated even though regression is the tool the group proposed for noisy data [card S025, p. 1].

**Method links**:
- **Method 4 ✅ evidence**: Method 4 step 2 (*bound the model error by poisedness constant × radius*) in its regression form, with the constant written out as ν₂ × ‖M̂†‖ (the conditioning of the scaled regression matrix) × dimension factors (p. 2).
- **Method 1 ⚠ variant** (deterministic precursor): the orders Δ, Δ², Δ³ are the certified fully quadratic accuracy that the probabilistic contract later asks for only with probability p; the second-order analysis of STORM asks E|F − f| ≤ κ_F δ_k³ [card S013, p. 28] (p. 2).
- **Research Taste warning sign 6 ✅ evidence** (weak): the dependence on n and on p̄ = n(n + 1)/2 is explicit in every constant instead of being absorbed into κ (p. 2). - **Technique catalog P1 ⚠ variant and P3 ✅**: P1 (Taylor subtraction, invert a scaled matrix) with a pseudo-inverse for a least-squares fit; P3 (scale the sample set to the unit ball) (pp. 1–2).

### B005 · Review of Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization (Mathematics of Computation 79(271), July 2010, pp. 1867–1869; S 0025-5718(10)02379-3)
**Role**: core (peer view) · **Coauthors**: none; this is not a Scheinberg work. **Reviewer**: J. L. Nazareth, University of Washington, Seattle (signature, p. 3). Every claim below is the reviewer's. **Position in trajectory**: published electronically on 12 February 2010, within a year of the book (p. 1). **Read**: full (the pages carry only this review and its five references).

- **D1 The problem class, as the reviewer frames it**: benign problems, reasonably smooth, unconstrained and with relatively few variables (up to about a hundred), that are algorithmically hard because derivatives cannot be supplied and evaluations are expensive or noisy, which makes finite differences prohibitive (p. 1). History: Nelder–Mead was the workhorse from 1965, and mid-1990s convergence theory spurred renewed research (p. 1). He separates derivative-free from non-differentiable optimization: DFO algorithms remain gradient-related, because their convergence relies on smoothness (p. 1).
  - Second-hand from the book (reported, not quoted): he quotes the preface's aims (book pp. xi–xii): the state of the art plus the basic theory, to the extent that the reader understands what is needed to ensure convergence, how it affects algorithm design and what success to expect where (p. 1). He also quotes the book's description of DFO as sampling the objective at points sufficiently spaced apart (book p. 57) (p. 1).
- **D2/D6 His reading of the architecture**:
  - Part I is the mathematical machinery or toolkit, nicely organised and well presented, and of interest in its own right (p. 2). He lists positive spanning sets, poisedness as the key notion, Λ-poisedness and condition numbers, three chapters for determined, overdetermined and underdetermined sets, and a closing chapter on improving the set (p. 2).
  - In Part II, direct search (two chapters) and trust region are the two main classes, with implicit filtering sandwiched in between. The trust-region methods based on derivative-free linear and quadratic models are the centerpiece: two frameworks with first- and second-order global convergence proofs, the basis of the "DFO" approach, Powell's methods and wedge methods (p. 2).
  - Part III is brief; the software appendix is useful (p. 2).
- **D5 Experiments** (his view): few numerical illustrations, compared with Kelley's book, and no implementational details (p. 3; Quote 2).
- **D7 Writing** (his view): gracefully written, well organised and timely (p. 3).
- **D8 Criticisms and praise**:
  - Criticisms (pp. 2–3): (1) the exercises are mostly elaborations of the theory and would be better embedded in the text, which reduces the book's use for an introductory course (p. 2); (2) the introduction's examples are, by the book's own admission, atypical of DFO applications (pp. 2–3); (3) few numerics and no implementation (p. 3); (4) the theory of one-dimensional derivative-free methods (Brent) is not covered, though it matters for line searches in the simplex-gradient approach (p. 3); (5) the intersection of DFO with non-differentiable optimization is not adequately addressed; he cites Rademacher's theorem (p. 3; Quote 1).
  - Praise (p. 3): the authors meet their stated aims admirably; the book guides practitioners on the merits and demerits of algorithms, will motivate researchers, and gives theoretical advice to developers of mathematical software.
  - He places the book in his own framework of algorithmic science and engineering, in its theoretical-algorithmic-science mode, citing his own SIAM News article (p. 3).

**Method links** (peer view; not the authors' voice):
- **Method 4 ✅ evidence** (peer): poisedness is the key notion of Part I, and the quality of the interpolation set gets a comprehensive treatment (p. 2).
- **Method 2 ✅ evidence** (peer, weak): two trust-region frameworks with global convergence proofs are the basis of several practical algorithms (p. 2).
