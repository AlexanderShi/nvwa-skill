### S006 · A theoretical and empirical comparison of gradient approximations in derivative-free optimization (Foundations of Computational Mathematics 2022, DOI 10.1007/s10208-021-09513-z; arXiv:1905.01332)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. S. Berahas, L. Cao, K. Choromanski (Google Brain); Scheinberg is corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is a companion to Berahas–Cao–Scheinberg's generic line search with noise ([5] in the paper, arXiv:1910.04055, later SIOPT 2021). That paper states the gradient-accuracy condition a line search needs; this one checks which estimators can meet it, and what they cost (p. 4–6, p. 30). It is the ML↔DFO bridge paper, with an RL co-author (p. 1, p. 31). **Read**: the full main text (pp. 1–32) and Appendix B (p. 42). In Appendix A, A.1–A.3 were read (pp. 36–37) and A.4–A.8 were skimmed (pp. 38–41). The reference list was skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is ML practice. RL and evolution-strategies papers average many directional derivatives along Gaussian or sphere directions. The paper's complaint is that "the number of these directions seems to be chosen to fit the specific method and this choice is somewhat obscure" (p. 2). It names two gaps (p. 3):
  - no analysis compares the accuracy of these estimates;
  - no analysis connects that accuracy to gains in the downstream optimization.

  The research question is explicit: can multiple randomized directional-derivative estimates have a practical or theoretical advantage over finite differences (p. 2)? The Nesterov–Spokoiny two-evaluation estimator sets the stakes. It needs a step n times smaller, so its iteration complexity is n times larger (p. 2). Problem form: minimize a smooth φ when only f(x) = φ(x) + ε(x), with |ε(x)| ≤ ε_f, can be computed (p. 1).
  - Transferable: turn a community's unexplained default (N ∼ n Gaussian directions) into a precise question: how many samples does it take to match the accuracy of the textbook method?
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: One formula covers every estimator: g(x) = Σ_i (f(x+σu_i) − f(x))/σ · ũ_i (1.1), or its central version (1.2). The methods differ only in N, {u_i}, {ũ_i} and σ (p. 2–3):
  - FFD and CFD use u_i = e_i;
  - linear interpolation (LI) uses any n linearly independent directions, with ũ_i the columns of Q_X⁻¹;
  - Gaussian smoothing (GSG) uses Gaussian u_i with ũ_i = u_i/N;
  - sphere smoothing (BSG) uses uniform directions on the sphere with ũ_i = (n/N)u_i.

  All of them get the same accuracy target: the norm condition ‖g(x) − ∇φ(x)‖ ≤ θ‖∇φ(x)‖ (1.3). This is the sufficient condition for the line search of [5], deterministically or with probability 1 − δ (p. 4–6). For each estimator the paper derives N, σ, and the smallest ‖∇φ‖ at which (1.3) can hold (Table 1, p. 5; Table 2, p. 26). **Nothing algorithmic is changed.** The outer method is [5, Algorithm 1], and the paper supplies oracles for it (p. 30). LI is presented as the generalization of FFD that allows sample points to be reused across iterations, at the price of keeping Q_X well conditioned (p. 2, p. 8–9).
  - Transferable: before comparing competitors, write them all as one parametrized formula, then fill in a table of parameters per method.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The assumptions are:
  - Assumption 1.1, bounded noise. Even stochastic noise is replaced by its worst-case bound (p. 1, p. 4). Knowing ε_f is "a key assumption", and the paper concedes that it "may seem a strong" one (p. 1). It is defended by computational noise from internal discretization, by smoothing of nonsmooth f, and by the fact that ε_f can be estimated at the cost of a few evaluations (p. 1). Only the noise at the sample points actually matters (p. 1–2).
  - Assumption 1.2: ∇φ is L-Lipschitz, and an upper bound on L is known (p. 2, p. 4).
  - Assumption 1.3: the Hessian is M-Lipschitz; this is needed for the central variants (p. 4).

  Simplifications: Table 1 assumes n ≥ 4 (p. 5), and the lower bound assumes n ≥ 15 and zero noise (p. 16). Zero-mean unbounded stochastic noise is out of scope; the setting is "more general than [32] but more restrictive than [20]", i.e. between Nesterov–Spokoiny and Duchi et al. (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Short proofs; each one is a single inequality chain.
  - *Choosing σ from a bias–noise trade-off.* The FFD error is at most √n·Lσ/2 + 2√n·ε_f/σ (Thm 2.1, p. 6). Requiring this to be ≤ θ‖∇φ‖ gives a quadratic in σ. Its root interval is nonempty iff θ²‖∇φ‖² ≥ 4nLε_f. The paper picks the minimizer σ = 2√(ε_f/L), which does not depend on the unknown ‖∇φ‖ (p. 6–7). CFD gives a cubic, and σ = ∛(3ε_f/M) (p. 7). The root condition becomes a **gradient-norm floor**: below it no σ can guarantee (1.3). Overestimating ε_f or L makes the algorithm stall earlier (p. 7).
  - *Interpolation.* Bound each row of Q_X(g − ∇φ) with the mean-value theorem, then multiply by ‖Q_X⁻¹‖ (Thm 2.3 and its proof, p. 8). "Well-poised" here means that ‖Q_X⁻¹‖ is bounded (p. 9).
  - *Smoothing.* Split ‖g − ∇φ‖ into the smoothing bias ‖∇F − ∇φ‖ and the sampling error ‖g − ∇F‖ (2.15, p. 10). The variance is bounded in the Loewner order, using (v₁+v₂+v₃)(·)ᵀ ⪯ 3Σv_iv_iᵀ and the Gaussian moment identities (2.18) (Lemma 2.4, p. 11–12). Chebyshev's inequality then finishes the bound (Lemma 2.5, p. 13). Existing bounds [48] assume almost-surely bounded errors, which fails for Gaussian directions (p. 10–11).
  - *Error-budget split.* λθ‖∇φ‖ goes to the bias and (1−λ)θ‖∇φ‖ to the sampling error. The choice λ = 1/(3√n) keeps N linear in n instead of quadratic (p. 14). Once σ is fixed and r is tied to ‖∇φ‖, the required N no longer depends on ‖∇φ‖ (Cor. 2.7, p. 14).
  - *Necessity (lower bound).* Petrov's tail lower bound, P(|X| > b) ≥ (E|X| − b)²/E|X|², is applied to the squared error for a linear φ = aᵀx. The 2nd and 4th moments are computed in closed form (p. 16; App. A.4–A.5). Result: N > (1−2√δ)(n+1)/θ² is *necessary* (p. 17). The authors call this bound weak and probe the true threshold by simulation (10,000 runs, n = 32): θ < 1/2 holds only about half the time at N = 4n = 128 (Fig. 1, p. 17–18).
  - *Sphere vs Gaussian.* Sphere-smoothing summands are bounded deterministically, so matrix Bernstein (Tropp) applies. This improves the δ-dependence from 1/δ to log((n+1)/δ) (Lemma 2.10, p. 20–21; remark p. 23). The 1/δ of GSG is called "an artifact of using Chebychev’s inequality" (p. 15).
  - *Same-sample algebra.* On one Gaussian sample set, g_GSG = (1/n)Q_XᵀQ_X·g_LI. The gap (I − Q_XᵀQ_X/n)·g_LI therefore does not shrink as σ → 0 (§2.5, p. 25).
  - *Result form.* Every method needs N = Θ(n). The constants for smoothing are much larger, and the sampling radii are comparable (p. 5, p. 25).
  - Transferable: when an error bound has the form aσ + b/σ, choose σ at the minimizer. The existence condition of the inequality then gives you the reachable neighbourhood for free.
- **D5 Experiments**: Three layers, each tied to a theoretical claim (p. 25–33; App. B p. 42).
  1. *Estimator accuracy.*
     - A synthetic function (3.2) is built so that n, L, M, σ and N can each be varied on its own (p. 26). Box plots of log₁₀ relative error over 100 realizations, noise-free and with uniform noise ε_f = 10⁻⁴, varying one knob at a time (Fig. 2, p. 26–27). A σ × ε_f grid follows (Fig. 3, p. 28–29).
     - Then 69 Schittkowski functions, evaluated at 5,330 points collected along fixed-step gradient-descent trajectories. The tables report mean log₁₀θ and the percentage of estimates with θ < 1/2, which is the threshold the theory requires (Tables 3–4, p. 29–30). Example, noise-free, σ = 10⁻⁵: FFD 95.10% vs GSG with N = 4n 49.74% (Table 3, p. 29).
     - In the noisy table, LI with random normalized Gaussian directions never exceeds 38.07% (Table 4, p. 30). The text links LI's sensitivity to the conditioning of a randomly drawn Q_X (p. 26).
  2. *End-to-end DFO.*
     - 53 Moré–Wild problems and 17 algorithm variants: steepest descent or L-BFGS directions, adaptive line search or a constant tuned step. The best variant of each estimator is plotted, with DFOTR as the benchmark (p. 30).
     - Performance and data profiles; the data-profile x-axis is function evaluations/(n+1); τ ∈ {10⁻¹, 10⁻³, 10⁻⁵} (Fig. 4, p. 31).
     - "For all the types of gradient approximations, the variants that performed the best used an adaptive step length procedure" (p. 30).
     - §3.2 does not state a noise level for these runs.
  3. *RL.*
     - OpenAI Gym Swimmer, HalfCheetah and Reacher. Compared: FFD, interpolation with a fixed step or a line search, and GSG.
     - Reward is plotted against iterations, as mean and min/max over 3 seeds (Fig. 7, p. 33; App. B p. 42).
     - Settings: σ = 0.1; Adam with α = 0.01 for the fixed-step runs; Armijo c₁ = 0.2 with backtracking factor 0.3 (p. 42).
     - The number of directions used by GSG in these runs is not stated in App. B.
     - A negative result is reported: FD "did not perform well" on some tasks. The authors attribute this to getting stuck, where random orthogonal directions help (p. 31–32).
  - Transferable: test an estimator at points harvested from real optimization trajectories, and report the fraction that meets the threshold your convergence theorem needs, not just the mean error.
- **D6 Positioning**: The critique of the RL/ES literature is direct but factual. Salimans et al. [41] use large N in a fixed-step method "without any analysis or discussion of the choices of N, σ or α" (p. 10). Other positioning (p. 3–4):
  - Choromanski's own orthogonal/structured-ES papers [13, 14, 40] are given the theory their experiments lacked;
  - Nesterov–Spokoiny [32] is extended from N = 1 to N > 1 variance analysis (p. 10);
  - Duchi et al. [20] are placed as a different, stochastic-noise setting (p. 3);
  - the norm condition is credited to Carter [11] and Polyak [37] (p. 4);
  - the step-scaled variant κα_k‖g‖ of Cartis–Scheinberg [12] and Paquette–Scheinberg [34] is acknowledged as the way around the unverifiable (1.3). The paper then argues that (1.3) "turns out to be achievable" for these estimators (p. 4).

  The paper credits Maggiar et al. [29] for bound (2.10) and thanks its first author for guidance on the proof (footnote, p. 10). The interpolation bound is an extension of CSV [17, 18] (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - *Thesis position.* The thesis is stated early and strongly in the introduction: randomized schemes "offer no theoretical or practical advantage" in the line-search setting (p. 2). The abstract, by contrast, is neutral.
  - *Hedging in the results.* Results are hedged: the bounds suggest "deterministic methods may be more efficient, at least in the setting considered in this paper" (p. 5, repeated p. 25).
  - *Uneven strength of claims.* The final remarks open by calling the estimates comparable "under reasonable assumptions" and close with "much more efficient" (p. 32). That strength is uneven against Table 4, where random-direction LI is the weakest deterministic method (p. 30).
  - *Results first.* A summary table of all results (Table 1) comes before any derivation (p. 5), and intuition is kept in its own section (§2.5, p. 25).
  - *Honesty about bounds.* Weak bounds are flagged as weak (p. 15, p. 17).
  - *Typos.* A few typos survive ("fo standard", p. 2; "To the best of our best knowledge", p. 3).
  - Transferable: put the full comparison table in the introduction, and derive it afterwards. Readers who only want the verdict can stop at p. 5.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contributions.* The contribution is theoretical: error bounds, and N and σ requirements, for seven estimators, plus an Ω(n) necessity result for GSG. It is also empirical: estimator accuracy, end-to-end DFO and RL.
  - *Limits stated.*
    - ε_f must be known and the noise bounded (p. 1).
    - The lower bound is weak, and a stronger one "remains an open question" (p. 17).
    - Randomization might help in some noisy setting, but "such setting is yet to be discovered and analyzed" (p. 2).
  - *Extension offered.* The techniques extend to any direction distribution with symmetric i.i.d. unit-variance components (p. 11, p. 32).
  - *Missing.* There is no dedicated future-work section.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Choose the free parameter (sampling radius σ) by minimizing an aσ + b/σ bound. The existence condition of the resulting inequality directly gives the reachable neighbourhood, ‖∇φ‖ ≥ 2√(nLε_f)/θ (p. 6–7).
- [D4] Split a relative-error budget λ/(1−λ) between deterministic bias and sampling error. Tune λ (here 1/(3√n)) so that the sample count keeps the right order in n (p. 14).
- [D4] To show a sample count is *necessary*, apply a second-moment tail lower bound to the simplest instance (a linear function). Then simulate that instance to see how loose the bound is (p. 16–18).
- [D4] Compare two estimators algebraically on the same sample set: g_GSG = (1/n)QᵀQ·g_LI. This exposes an error term that does not vanish as σ → 0 (p. 25).
- [D4] If your random summands are bounded, use matrix Bernstein instead of Chebyshev to get log(1/δ) instead of 1/δ. Say explicitly when the weaker dependence is an artifact of the inequality used (p. 15, p. 20–23).
- [D5] Build a synthetic test function in which every constant of the bound (n, L, M) is a separate knob, and vary one knob at a time (p. 26).
- [D5] Evaluate estimators at points harvested from optimization trajectories. Report the percentage that meet the convergence threshold (θ < 1/2) alongside the mean error (p. 29–30).

### D001 · High Probability Complexity Bounds for Adaptive Step Search Based on Stochastic Oracles (SIAM J. Optim. 34 (2024) 2411–2439, DOI 10.1137/22m1512764; arXiv:2106.06454)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: B. Jin, M. Xie (both Cornell ORIE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the journal extension of the NeurIPS 2021 paper [9]. Relative to that version, it adds the convex and strongly convex (PL) cases, removes the upper bound on the step size, and drops "a strong assumption on the independence of the errors in the function estimates" (p. 4). It takes the algorithm of Berahas–Cao–Scheinberg [3] and generalizes the stochastic step-search line of Cartis–Scheinberg [5] and Paquette–Scheinberg [16] (p. 2–4). The high-probability idea is credited to Gratton–Royer–Vicente–Zhang [7] (p. 3). **Read**: the full text (pp. 1–29), including all proofs. The reference list was skimmed. The proofs of Propositions 7–9 are deferred to [9, Appendix A] and were not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is the set of limitations in the group's own prior step-search analyses (p. 2–3):
  - they give expected complexity only;
  - they need τ = ∞, which forces an explicit upper bound on the step size into the complexity;
  - their zeroth-order oracles are exact ([5]) or deterministically bounded ([3]);
  - [16] needed "a somewhat artificial modification to the method itself" and has an O(L³) dependence (p. 2, p. 17).

  An external competitor is also attacked: Vaswani et al.'s SGD+Armijo [20] needs per-sample Lipschitz gradients, "a very strong assumption that often fails in practice", and severe step-size bounds (p. 3–4). The paper states its five motivations as a bullet list (p. 3). Problem: minimize a smooth, possibly non-convex ϕ, when only probabilistic oracles for ϕ and ∇ϕ are available and L is unknown to the algorithm (p. 1).
  - Transferable: list, as bullets, exactly which assumption of which prior paper (often your own) each part of the new result removes (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The algorithm is Stochastic Adaptive Step Search (SASS, Alg. 2.1, p. 5), **taken unchanged from [3]** (p. 4):
  - draw a fresh gradient estimate g_k at every iteration;
  - test a modified Armijo condition f(x_k⁺) ≤ f(x_k) − α_kθ‖g_k‖² + 2ε′_f, using two zeroth-order estimates;
  - on success, x moves and α_k is multiplied by γ⁻¹; on failure, α_k is multiplied by γ.

  The novelty is in how the oracles are modelled, not in the algorithm. SFO(ε_g, τ, κ, δ) requires ‖g − ∇ϕ‖ ≤ max{ε_g, min{τ, κα}‖g‖} with probability 1 − δ (p. 1). In this condition:
  - ε_g > 0 models the oracle's practical accuracy limit, e.g. the largest affordable mini-batch (p. 2);
  - a finite τ is "a very minor condition which allows us to drop the upper bound on the step size parameter completely" (p. 3).

  SZO(ε_f, ν, b) only needs E|e(x)| ≤ ε_f and a one-sided subexponential tail (p. 1). "Step search" is coined for line searches whose direction changes during backtracking (p. 1, footnotes 1 and 3). The oracle constants are "intrinsic to the oracle" and unknown to the algorithm. Only ε′_f, an upper bound on E[e(x)], is an input (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when a stronger theorem seems to need an algorithmic safeguard (a step-size cap), check whether a mild extra condition on the *oracle* (here τ < ∞) removes the need for it.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - *Standing assumptions.* Assumption 1: ∇ϕ is L-Lipschitz and ϕ is bounded below (p. 4). Assumption 2: ε′_f ≥ ε_f (p. 5).
  - *Zeroth-order oracle.* The light tail is justified by Hoeffding (bounded losses) or by the CLT (averaged samples) (p. 2). An equivalent form SZO′ is shown to be interchangeable; SZO is kept because the results depend explicitly on (ν, b) (p. 3). Only the noise of the *difference* f(x) − f(x⁺) actually needs to be subexponential (p. 5).
  - *Per-class assumptions.* Assumption 4 is strong convexity, and only the PL inequality is used (p. 17–18). Assumption 5, for the convex case, requires iterates to stay within distance D of x* (p. 22).
  - *Admitted conveniences.*
    - The zeroth-order oracle "may appear somewhat convoluted" but "strikes a natural balance between theory and practice" (p. 2).
    - ε′_f is fixed "For simplicity of the analysis" (p. 29).
    - All the theory also works with τ = ∞ (p. 3).
  - *Independence wording is not uniform.* The introduction says the independence assumption on function-estimate errors was removed (p. 4). §3.1 still says the unbounded-noise case needs independent noise across iterations (p. 10), and §4.2 mentions "i.i.d. subexponential noises" (p. 17). The proof of Proposition 1 itself only uses a subexponential bound conditioned on the filtration F′_{k−1} (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The analysis follows an "axioms, then instances" architecture.
  - *Axioms.* Assumption 3 (i)–(v) states abstract properties of the process {I_k (true), Θ_k (successful), Z_k (progress measure), A_k (step)} (p. 7), each with a one-line gloss:
    - (i) a positive progress floor h(ᾱ) at the threshold step;
    - (ii) P(I_k = 1 | F_{k−1}) ≥ p;
    - (iii) true and successful iterations decrease Z_k by h(A_k) − r;
    - (iv) small and true iterations are successful;
    - (v) the damage per iteration is bounded by r(ε′_f, E_k + E_k⁺).
  - *Counting core (a deterministic argument).* A step is *large* if both A_k and A_{k+1} are ≥ ᾱ (U_k = 1), with ᾱ = α₀γ^m WLOG (p. 7). The lemmas then chain:
    - Lemma 3.2: large successful steps ≥ large unsuccessful steps − d, where d counts the down-steps from α₀ to ᾱ (p. 8);
    - Lemma 3.4: small true steps ≤ small false steps (p. 8);
    - key Lemma 3.5: T_ε > t and ΣI_k ≥ p̂t together imply ΣU_kΘ_kI_k ≥ (p̂ − ½)t − d/2 (p. 9).
  - *Probability enters twice.*
    - Azuma–Hoeffding on the submartingale ΣI_k − pt gives P(ΣI_k ≤ p̂t) ≤ exp(−(p−p̂)²t/(2p²)) (Lemma 3.1, p. 7).
    - For unbounded noise, the per-iteration damage bound is replaced by a bound on the *sum* of damages. A Bernstein-type inequality is proved by induction on conditional MGFs, with the current gradient's randomness in the conditioning filtration (Prop. 1, p. 11–12). Jensen's inequality, via the concavity of r in its second argument, connects E[r(E_k + E_k⁺)] to r(2ε′_f) (p. 13).
  - *Supporting algebra.* r of a subexponential variable is subexponential (Lemma 3.7, p. 11). Sums and affine maps of subexponentials stay subexponential (Prop. 2, p. 14). ln W is subexponential for W ≥ 1 (Prop. 5, p. 21).
  - *Instances.* Each function class chooses its own Z_k, h and r (Table 1, p. 6; Props. 3, 4, 6, pp. 15–23):
    - non-convex: Z_k = ϕ − ϕ*;
    - strongly convex: Z_k = ln((ϕ−ϕ*)/ε), which turns linear convergence into additive progress (p. 19–20);
    - convex: Z_k = 1/ε₀ − 1/(ϕ−ϕ*), with a two-part stopping time (ε₀, ε₁). The gradient-norm part is needed because under bias the oracle can return the opposite direction when ‖∇ϕ‖ is small (p. 6).
  - *Two-case oracle argument.* The error is either relative (≤ min{τ, κA_k}‖G_k‖) or absolute (≤ ε_g). The absolute case becomes relative because ε ≥ ε_g/η (p. 16).
  - *Results.* The neighbourhood is ε > max{ε_g/η, (…)·√(4ε′_f/(θ(p−½)))·(…)} (Inequality 1, p. 15). Theorem 4.1: with overwhelming probability the iteration count is of order (1/(p − ½ − (4ε′_f+s)/(Cε²)))·(ϕ(x₀)−ϕ*)/(Cε²). This "essentially recovers the iteration complexity of the deterministic algorithm" when p = 1 and ε′_f = 0 (Remark 2, p. 17). The dependence on L is O(L), versus O(L³) in [16] (Remark 3, p. 17).
  - Transferable: write a short gloss in parentheses after each axiom of an abstract process assumption, e.g. "(Small and true iterations are also successful.)" (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Scope stated up front.* The oracles use fixed mini-batches of 128, and "we do not check or ensure" the theoretical oracle properties. The stated goal is competitiveness with ADAM and SLS, and showing that ε′_f is easy to estimate (p. 26).
  - *Kernel logistic regression.* All 64 PMLB binary datasets with 150–5000 points, RBF kernel (p. 26).
    - ε′_f is re-estimated every epoch as 1/5 of the empirical standard deviation of 30 oracle calls.
    - Sensitivity check with ε′_f ∈ {0, 1/5, 1/2, 1} × std: SASS is robust "as long as it is not chosen to be zero" (Fig. 1, p. 26).
    - Fixed parameters γ = 0.9, θ = 0.2, α₀ = 1. SLS uses its authors' suggested parameters, after other combinations were tried. ADAM is swept over 5 learning rates (p. 26–27).
    - 5 random starts. **Equal budget in inner products**: ADAM 100 epochs vs SASS 50 epochs, because SASS needs 2 inner products per iteration (p. 27).
    - Metric: median best test loss. Results reported plainly: SASS beats SLS on only 32 of 64 datasets, and ADAM with LR 10⁻¹ beats SASS on 47 of 64 (p. 27).
  - *Neural networks.* MNIST (MLP, CNN) and FashionMNIST (ResNet18). The x-axis is forward-plus-backward passes, with SASS charged 3 per iteration (p. 27). Step-size traces are shown. - **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution.* The contribution is theory: high-probability tail bounds for non-convex, convex and strongly convex/PL problems, under biased first-order oracles and subexponential zeroth-order oracles, with no cap on the step size. Section 5 adds oracle constructions for mini-batch ERM and randomized finite differences, with sample-size conditions (Props. 7–9, p. 24–25). The experiments are secondary.
  - *Limits stated.*
    - ε′_f is fixed in the analysis (p. 29).
    - The experiments do not enforce the oracle conditions (p. 26).
    - Other finite-difference schemes are not treated "For brevity" (p. 25).
  - *Future work named.* Practical variants with adaptive mini-batch sizes, and the choice of γ and ε′_f (p. 26).
  - *Practice ahead of theory.* The final remark says the practice is ahead of the theory: ε′_f can be set much smaller "than theory dictates" (p. 29).
  - *Randomized-FD oracle.* ε_g is a fixed bias set by σ, and it is smallest at σ = O(√ε_f). With |U| ∝ n samples, this matches the total cost of one-sample Nesterov–Spokoiny, whose step must be n times smaller (Remark, p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ strong evidence (full text). The contract is written per oracle:
  - accuracy tied to the step, κα‖g‖, probability 1 − δ, and bias allowed (p. 1–2);
  - irreducible parts (ε_g, ε′_f) set the neighbourhood (Inequality 1, p. 15);
  - the probability threshold is p > ½ + r/h(ᾱ) (Assumption 3, p. 7), which is Step 5;
  - the cost of meeting the contract is derived for mini-batch and randomized-FD oracles (Props. 8–9, p. 24–25), which is Step 4.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (refines Step 2):
  - The first-order contract has **three regimes**: an absolute floor ε_g (the practical limit of the oracle), a step-scaled middle κα‖g‖, and a cap τ‖g‖ (p. 1–3).
  - The zeroth-order contract is **not a fixed-probability event**. It is a mean bound plus a subexponential tail, and the "true iteration" probability is *derived* from that tail (p. 5, p. 15).
  - The algorithm never needs the contract constants, only an upper bound ε′_f (p. 4).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The algorithm of [3] is kept as is (p. 4). The new results come from a better oracle model and a better analysis, and the step-size cap is removed through the oracle (τ < ∞), not through the algorithm (p. 3).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: stopping time, expected-to-high-probability upgrade, deterministic-order recovery, and an explicit dependence on p (1/(p − ½ − …)) and on L (p. 3, p. 10, p. 17).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: a **correction to SKILL.md Steps 1–2**. The proof does *not* use a potential combining f and the step size, nor a supermartingale on it. It uses a progress measure Z_k alone, plus a *deterministic counting argument* on large and small steps relative to a threshold ᾱ (Lemmas 3.2–3.5, p. 8–9). Probability enters only through Azuma–Hoeffding on the count of true iterations (Lemma 3.1, p. 7) and Bernstein on the summed damage (Prop. 1, p. 11–12). The "biased random walk" intuition survives as Lemma 3.4: small true steps ≤ small false steps (p. 8).
- Research Heuristic 6 ✅: expected → high probability is an explicit motivation (p. 3). Research Heuristic 12 ✅: the 2ε′_f Armijo slack is essential, since ε′_f = 0 performs badly (Fig. 1, p. 26).
- Taste warning signs 1 ✅ (no pre-specified step sizes, p. 1; ADAM's best LR shifts across problems, p. 28) and 2 ✅ (biased oracles allowed, p. 1–2).
- Method 5 ✅ weak evidence. Methods are compared at an equal budget in the underlying unit of work (inner products or passes), not per iteration (p. 27).
- New-pattern candidate: *axiomatize the process, instantiate per function class*. Assumption 3 (i)–(v) is proved once to give the tail bound (Theorems 3.6 and 3.8). Each function class then only chooses Z_k, h and r (Props. 3, 4, 6; p. 7, p. 14–23).
- New-pattern candidate: *oracle-side fix for an algorithm-side safeguard*. A mild extra oracle condition (τ < ∞) removes a step-size cap that the analysis previously needed (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Prove a high-probability iteration bound in two stages. First, a purely deterministic counting lemma: if not stopped and enough iterations were true, then enough iterations were good. Second, one concentration inequality on the count of true iterations (Lemmas 3.1 and 3.5, p. 7–9).
- [D4] Classify steps as large or small relative to a threshold ᾱ = α₀γ^m. Then count up-moves against down-moves separately in each class (Lemmas 3.2 and 3.4, p. 8).
- [D4] When the noise is unbounded, stop bounding the damage per iteration. Bound the *sum* of damages with a Bernstein inequality proved by induction on conditional MGFs (Prop. 1, p. 11–12).
- [D4] For linear convergence, use Z_k = ln((ϕ−ϕ*)/ε), so that multiplicative contraction becomes the same additive-progress axiom (p. 19–20). For the convex case, use Z_k = 1/ε₀ − 1/(ϕ−ϕ*) (p. 6).
- [D3] Write the oracle's accuracy as max{floor, min{cap, κα}·‖g‖}. The floor models the practical sampling limit, and the cap avoids a step-size bound (p. 1–3).
- [D5] Estimate the noise slack online from repeated oracle calls (here 1/5 of the std of 30 calls, once per epoch), and include the ε′_f = 0 ablation (Fig. 1, p. 26).
- [D5] Give every method the same budget in inner products or network passes, and say how many units each method is charged per iteration (p. 27).
- [D7] Report a split outcome as it is ("32 out of 64 datasets", "47 out of 64", p. 27). Then argue from a different axis, here the absence of tuning (p. 27–28).
