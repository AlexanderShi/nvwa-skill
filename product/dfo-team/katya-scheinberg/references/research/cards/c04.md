### S014 · Global convergence rate analysis of unconstrained optimization methods based on probabilistic models (Mathematical Programming 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10107-017-1137-4)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: C. Cartis · **Position in trajectory**: the first global *rate* result for methods with probabilistic models. It sits between Bandeira–Scheinberg–Vicente 2014, which gives only limit behaviour (p. 3), and the stochastic-function papers S013 and S015, which cite it as [6]. **Text read**: arXiv:1505.06070v2 (5 Jan 2017), pp. 1–34, read in full. There is no appendix.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper enters through a gap between two literatures (p. 2). Deterministic-inexact methods keep traditional line search, trust region or regularization "with little modification" but assume errors bounded "in some decaying manner" at every iteration. Stochastic-gradient methods drop the deterministic bound but are quite different from the traditional methods in step-size selection and iterate averaging. The stated purpose is to keep the traditional algorithms and relax the deterministic error bound, without assuming zero-mean error or bounded variance (p. 2). A second motivation is to explain practical algorithms "that do not always seek to ensure theoretically required model quality" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Generic Algorithm 2.1 (p. 4) builds a random model, takes a step governed by a step parameter α_k, checks sufficient decrease using an *exact* f, and multiplies α by γ⁻¹ on success or by γ on failure. The only change from the deterministic method is that the model is random, "sufficiently accurate" with probability ≥ p conditioned on the past (Def. 2.1, p. 6), and therefore re-drawn after every iteration, successful or not (p. 5). The accuracy is tied to the step the model itself produces: ‖G_k − ∇f(X_k)‖ ≤ κA_k‖G_k‖ for line search (Def. 3.1, p. 14), and gradient and Hessian errors ≤ κ‖S_k‖² along the step for ARC (Def. 4.1, pp. 22–23). §5 contrasts this with fully-linear models, which are tied to an independent radius Δ_k (pp. 28–29). Randomness in the step computation is absorbed into randomness of the model (p. 5). The methods are claimed to be "essentially the exact counterparts" of the deterministic methods, with no averaging (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: ∇f is globally L-Lipschitz and f is bounded below (Assumption 3.2, p. 15). The convex case adds bounded level sets with diameter D (Assumption 3.3, p. 18), and the strongly convex case adds modulus µ (Assumption 3.4, p. 19). ARC needs an L_H-Lipschitz Hessian (Assumption 4.2, p. 23). Function values are exact: "there is no error in the function evaluations" (p. 2). The threshold is p > 1/2 (Assumptions 3.1 and 4.1, pp. 15 and 23). Simplifications the authors flag themselves: the same factor γ is used for increase and decrease, and with γ_inc ≠ γ_dec the 1/2 threshold would move (p. 13); C = γ^c α_0 < γα_max is "a simple technical condition" (p. 9); C ≤ (1+κα_max)²/(2µθ) is assumed "without loss of generality" in the strongly convex case (p. 20); ARC uses a simplified σ update, although the deterministic rule distinguishes "very successful" iterations (Remark 4.1, p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The analysis reduces every algorithm to a process {A_k, F_k}. F_k is a nondecreasing progress measure with a deterministic cap F_ε: f(X⁰) − f(X_k) in the nonconvex case, 1/(f − f*) in the convex case and log(1/(f − f*)) in the strongly convex case (p. 7). N_ε is the hitting time. Assumption 2.1 (p. 8) has three checkable items: (i) a true, successful iteration raises F by h(α_k); (ii) if α_k ≤ C, a true iteration is successful; (iii) F is nondecreasing. Two proof devices follow. Lemma 2.1 (pp. 8–9) says that E[Σ W_k I_k] ≥ p·E[Σ W_k] for any W_k determined by the past. A counting argument partitions iterations into true/false × successful/unsuccessful × A_k above/below C (Lemmas 2.2–2.7, pp. 10–13). For example, at most half of the iterations with A_k ≤ C can be successful (Lemma 2.2, p. 10). Theorem 2.1 gives E(N_ε) ≤ 2p/(2p−1)² · (2F_ε/h(C) + log_γ(C/α_0)) (p. 13). Instantiating it means computing C, h and F_ε: O(ε⁻²) nonconvex (Thm 3.1, p. 16), O(ε⁻¹) convex (Thm 3.2, p. 19), O(log 1/ε) strongly convex (Thm 3.3, p. 20), O(ε⁻³ᐟ²) for ARC (Thm 4.1, p. 26). An exponentially decaying tail P{N_ε > K} is asserted to be "easy" to derive, but it is not proved (p. 13). The acknowledgements credit Zaikun Zhang with significantly simplifying the §2 analysis (p. 33).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The paper is purely theoretical (checked pp. 1–34).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on BSV [1], which analysed only limit behaviour (p. 3), and says of Gratton–Royer–Vicente–Zhang [13]: "The only other work that we are aware of" bounding the number of steps (p. 3). It re-reads Byrd–Chin–Nocedal–Wu [4]: their deterministic norm condition is a special case of Def. 3.1, so Assumption 3.1 "can be viewed as a relaxed version", and the Chebyshev sample-size argument shows sampled gradients qualify (pp. 27–28). The critique of SGD is restrained: SGD analyses bound expected error after a fixed number of iterations, whereas this paper bounds expected iterations to reach accuracy. "Both bounds are useful", but the latter is "somewhat more meaningful" here (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Contributions are three bullets, each phrased as a difference from existing literature (pp. 2–3). A "We believe" hedge frames the practical-relevance claim (p. 3). A generic section comes first "to avoid repetition" (p. 3). Every rate theorem is followed by a remark that reads the ε-order against the deterministic method and the p-dependence (p = 1 recovers the deterministic method, p → 1/2 sends the bound to infinity) (Remarks 3.1–3.3 and 4.3, pp. 16, 19, 20, 26). A rough edge remains in the arXiv text: "Theorem ??" (Remark 4.4, p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a reusable analysis framework and rate theorems, with no software or experiments. Limits stated concretely: f is deterministic and exact (pp. 2, 33); convergence rates for stochastic f are "a topic of future research" (p. 33), and S013 and S015 later deliver them; comparing total gradient-sample complexity with the literature is left open (p. 28); models satisfying Def. 4.1 for ARC under sampling are left open (p. 28); the practical σ-update strategy is untested (Remark 4.1, p. 22). §5.2 only sketches analyses of Algorithms 5.1 and 5.2, which shrink a sampling-radius parameter ξ_k when constants are unknown and claim an extra O(log 1/ε) term (pp. 29–33).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. The contract is a probability ≥ p conditioned on the past, with accuracy scaled to the step (Defs 2.1, 3.1, 4.1, pp. 6, 14, 22). There are no mean or variance assumptions (p. 2).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The methods are "essentially the exact counterparts" of the deterministic methods (pp. 2–3), and Algorithm 4.1 is stated "as close as possible to known/deterministic ARC frameworks" (Remark 4.2, p. 22).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence plus one detail. Full text confirms hitting-time complexity and a deterministic ε-order (pp. 7, 13, 16). The mechanism here, however, is a *counting* argument on {A_k, F_k} (pp. 10–13), not the supermartingale of S013, and the p-constant is 2p/(2p−1)², not p/(2p−1). Step 4, the high-probability tail, is claimed but not done (p. 13).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence for step 4. Randomly sampled fully-linear or fully-quadratic models stand in for certified geometry, with the sampling radius tied to the step (§5.2, pp. 28–31).
- New-pattern candidate: framework-then-instantiation: one abstract process theorem, then per algorithm only the constants C, h and F_ε (pp. 3, 8, 16–26).
- New-pattern candidate: subsume the competitor: Byrd et al.'s condition is shown to be a special case (pp. 27–28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give a three-item assumption about an abstract process (progress on true and successful iterations, a threshold C below which true implies successful, monotone progress). Then each new algorithm or function class only needs its C, h(α) and cap F_ε computed (pp. 8, 16, 19, 20, 26).
- Pick the progress measure per function class so that one hitting-time theorem yields all three rates: f(x⁰) − f(x_k), 1/(f − f*), log(1/(f − f*)), capped by f(x⁰) − f*, 1/ε and log(1/ε) (p. 7).
- Predictable-weight lemma: for W_k measurable with respect to the past, E[Σ_{k<N} W_k I_k] ≥ p·E[Σ_{k<N} W_k]. Use it to turn a probability-≥-p guarantee into bounds on counts of iteration types (Lemma 2.1, pp. 8–9).
- Scale the model-accuracy requirement by the step the model produces (κα_k‖g_k‖, or κ‖s_k‖²). The deterministic descent lemma then shows in one line that a small step with a good model succeeds (Lemma 3.1, pp. 15–16; Lemma 4.2, p. 24).
- If a needed constant (the sampling radius) depends on unknown problem constants, add a parameter that is only ever decreased when a checkable inequality fails (‖g_k‖ ≥ κ_Δ ξ_k), and pay an additive O(log 1/ε) (Algorithm 5.1, pp. 29–30).

**Quotes**:
- "Our purpose in this paper is to derive a class of methods which inherit the best properties of traditional deterministic algorithms, and yet relax the assumption that the derivative/model error is bounded in a deterministic manner." (p. 2)
- "We believe that, amongst other things, our analysis helps to understand the convergence properties of practical algorithms, that do not always seek to ensure theoretically required model quality." (p. 3)

### D003 · Linear interpolation gives better gradients than Gaussian smoothing in derivative-free optimization (arXiv preprint 2019, arXiv:1905.13043)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. S. Berahas, L. Cao, K. Choromanski (Google Brain) · **Position in trajectory**: a short "Under review" companion (p. 1) of the FoCM 2022 comparison paper. That paper is cited here as [2], arXiv:1905.01332 (p. 13), and §2 is "an adaptation" of [1] (Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal) and [2] (p. 3). It is the ML-facing entry point to the estimator-comparison line. **Text read**: arXiv:1905.13043v2, pp. 1–14, read in full. There is no appendix.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper is application-driven. RL policy optimization is posed as DFO with noisy, expensive, derivative-free evaluations (p. 1). Salimans et al. [20] use Gaussian-direction gradient estimates, and follow-ups [5, 6, 19] found orthogonal directions better empirically. The gap named is that these works do not quantify accuracy against parameters and give no convergence result (p. 2). The problem is f = φ + ε with bounded, not stochastic, noise |ε(x)| ≤ ε_f that does not vanish near the solution (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper splits the question in two. (a) A gradient method with a relaxed Armijo test f(x_k − α_k g) ≤ f(x_k) − c_1α_k‖g‖² + 2ε_f converges at the standard rates down to a noise-defined neighbourhood whenever the estimate satisfies the norm condition ‖g − ∇φ‖ ≤ θ‖∇φ‖ with θ < 1/2 (eq. 2.2–2.3, p. 3; Thms 2.5, 2.8 and 2.11, pp. 4–5). (b) The paper then asks what each estimator costs to meet that condition (§3). Linear interpolation on n orthonormal directions meets it *deterministically* when σ lies in the interval (3.10) and ‖∇φ‖ ≥ 2√(Lnε_f)/θ (p. 7). Gaussian smoothing (GSG) needs N ≥ 2n/(δθ²) samples for probability 1 − δ (p. 10). A reframing result: GSG with orthonormal directions *is* linear interpolation, up to scaling (eq. 3.3, pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Noise is bounded (Assumption 2.1, p. 3), and φ has an L-Lipschitz gradient (Assumption 2.2, p. 3). The GSG analysis instead assumes f is L_f-Lipschitz (Assumption 3.3, p. 8), which gives L = √n·L_f/σ and ε_f = σ√n·L_f. The two estimators are therefore analysed under different assumption sets and aligned by imposing θ‖∇φ‖ ≥ 2√(Lnε_f) "for proper comparison" (p. 10). Q_X is assumed orthonormal from Thm 3.1 on (p. 7). The central-difference versions are set aside: limited benefit under noise (Remark 3.2, p. 7) and omitted "for brevity" (p. 8). The proof of Lemma 2.3 uses α_k > τᾱ (p. 4). The algorithm statement only says a failed trial is multiplied by τ (p. 3) and does not state where each backtracking starts, so this lower bound implicitly assumes each search starts at a trial step ≥ ᾱ.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: (i) A descent lemma under the norm condition, with the noise entering as a +4ε_f slack (Lemma 2.3, pp. 3–4). (ii) The convex rate uses a two-regime argument, gap large versus small relative to the noise (pp. 4–5). The strongly convex rate uses a shifted recursion around φ* + 4ε_f/(1 − ρ) (p. 5). The nonconvex rate averages ‖∇φ‖² down to a 4ε_f/η floor (pp. 5–6). (iii) The interpolation error comes from the mean value theorem plus ‖Q_X⁻¹‖ (Thm 3.1, p. 7). The σ-range comes from solving a quadratic in σ (3.9)–(3.11) (p. 7). (iv) The GSG variance comes from Gaussian moment identities (3.17) (p. 9), followed by a multivariate Chebyshev bound (Thm 3.5, pp. 9–10). N ≥ 2n/(δθ²) is a *sufficient* sample size. No matching lower bound is stated (checked pp. 8–10). The sampling radii are also compared: σ = √(ε_f/L) for GSG versus a lower bound of about 2√(ε_f/L) for interpolation (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: (1) Gradient accuracy, noise-free (p. 10). A synthetic function (4.1) with known gradient, boxplots of log₁₀ relative error for N ∈ {n, 4n, 32n} and σ ∈ {1, 1e−3, 1e−6} (Fig. 1, p. 11). The 69 Schittkowski problems × 100 points each (Table 1, p. 11). At σ ≤ 1e−5 the interpolation estimators reach log₁₀θ between −2.1 and −5.4, while GSG and cGSG stay between −0.47 and 0.10 even with N up to 8n or 16n (Table 1, p. 11). (2) RL on OpenAI Gym Swimmer, HalfCheetah and Reacher (pp. 11–12). The policy is an NN with two hidden layers of 41 tanh units and low-displacement-rank weights, with no reward normalization or filtering "to solely focus" on the estimators, and σ = 0.1. Non-line-search methods (FD, LIOD, GSG) use Adam with α = 0.01, and LIOD(LS) uses Armijo with c_1 = 0.2 and τ = 0.3. Each configuration runs with 3 seeds, and the plots show mean and min/max reward against *iterations* (Fig. 2, p. 12). The per-iteration sample counts N used in RL are not stated. The authors note a gap between theory and experiment: FD and LIOD have the same theory, yet random orthonormal directions "helps the algorithm to avoid getting stuck in local maxima" (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper corrects an ML practice by name. It targets [20] (Salimans et al.) and declines to use the term "evolutionary strategies" for it (p. 2). It credits [5, 6, 19] empirically but calls their theory lacking (p. 2). It notes that Nesterov–Spokoiny [16] analysed only N = 1 and that [20] chose N, σ and α "without any analysis" (p. 8). Sphere-direction smoothing is dismissed in two sentences: a similar analysis "reveals that it is inferior to using orthogonal directions" (p. 2). Interpolation is placed in the classical DFO lineage: Powell, Conn–Scheinberg–Toint, CSV and Wild et al. (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The title states the result as a comparative claim. The abstract names the inferior method by citation ("the Gaussian sampling method used in [20] is significantly inferior", p. 1). The contributions list (spelled "Contribitions", p. 2) has four theorem-level bullets and one experiments bullet. Each analysis section ends with a remark that interprets the noise floor, e.g. "the lowest value of the objective that is guaranteed to be achieved in the presence of noise" (Remark 2.9, p. 5; also Remarks 2.6 and 2.12, pp. 4, 6). The final remarks are four sentences long (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are theory (a convergence condition and estimator cost) plus empirical comparison. Limits visible in the text: bounded noise only; the GSG theory needs Lipschitz f; central-difference variants are not analysed (pp. 7–8); the RL evidence is 3 seeds on an iterations axis with Adam for the fixed-step methods (pp. 11–12); the GSG sample bound is a Chebyshev sufficient condition (p. 10). No future directions are stated (Final Remarks, p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. The same accuracy target (norm condition θ‖∇φ‖) is used for every estimator, N and σ are derived per estimator (pp. 7, 10), and estimation error is compared at equal N (Fig. 1 and Table 1, p. 11).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The downstream RL comparison is reported against iterations, not function evaluations, with unstated per-iteration N, only 3 seeds, and Adam for fixed-step baselines (pp. 11–12). This departs from Method 5 step 5 (count evaluations) and Workflow D's checkpoint.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence for step 3. The irreducible noise ε_f fixes the reachable neighbourhood (Remarks 2.6, 2.9 and 2.12, pp. 4–6) and the gradient-norm floor 2√(Lnε_f)/θ (p. 7).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. This is an Armijo line search with only a 2ε_f slack added (p. 3); Heuristic 12 comes from the same group.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The interpolation error bound scales with ‖Q_X⁻¹‖, and the best case is orthonormal Q (p. 7), which is geometry as a conditioning constant.
- Taste mark 6 ✅. The paper has an ML coauthor, RL benchmarks, and ML terminology corrected (pp. 1–2).
- New-pattern candidate: subsume the competitor: the ML estimator with orthonormal directions is rewritten as classical interpolation (pp. 6–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split the paper into two parts: the algorithm converges under condition X, and each estimator A/B/C has a cost of satisfying X. The comparison then reduces to sample counts for one shared condition (pp. 3, 7, 10).
- For bounded noise, add a +2ε_f slack to the Armijo test. Then state every rate as the standard rate down to a noise-determined neighbourhood, and give the neighbourhood explicitly (pp. 3–6).
- Choose the sampling radius by solving the quadratic inequality in σ that combines the truncation error (∝σ) and the noise error (∝1/σ). Its discriminant gives the gradient-norm floor below which the accuracy condition cannot be met (eqs. 3.9–3.11, p. 7).
- Estimator-variance recipe: write Var{g} exactly (3.16), bound it with Gaussian moment identities (3.17), then apply multivariate Chebyshev with r = θ‖∇φ‖ to get N for probability 1 − δ (pp. 8–10).
- Estimation-accuracy protocol: use a synthetic function with a closed-form gradient plus the 69 Schittkowski problems × 100 random points, and report the average log₁₀ relative error for a grid of N and σ with noise switched off (pp. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "None of these recent papers presents any convergence result for the proposed algorithms." (p. 2)
- "We will not use this terminology in this paper, since it encompasses a large and different class of optimization methods than those used in [20] and those considered here." (p. 2)

### S013 · Convergence rate analysis of a stochastic trust-region method via supermartingales (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2019, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2019.0016)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. Blanchet, C. Cartis, M. Menickelly · **Position in trajectory**: extends S014 ([6]) and Gratton et al. ([16]) to *stochastic function values* (pp. 3–4). It supplies the complexity bound that STORM ([8], Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg, a.s. convergence only) lacked. Its renewal-reward framework is reused by S015 ([24], p. 4, p. 33). **Text read**: arXiv:1609.07428v3 (19 Oct 2018), pp. 1–36, read in full, including the Appendix (pp. 35–36, proofs of Lemmas 4.2–4.4).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a critique of what the nonconvex stochastic literature delivers (pp. 2–3). Ghadimi–Lan-type rates [14] need "a carefully chosen step size and a randomized stopping scheme" unlike practice. SVRG, SAGA and SARAH exploit a fixed finite sum, and "the assumption that the data set is fixed (deterministic) contradicts the ultimate goal of learning" (p. 2). Stochastic trust-region methods [7, 26] converge only asymptotically, with no rates. [29] and [27] give bounds that hold only with probability p^T (p. 3). [6] and [16] need exact function values, so "cannot be applied in a stochastic setting" (p. 3). STORM [8] needed the restrictive η₂ ≥ κ_ef (Remark 3.1, p. 12).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper has two layers. (a) An abstract renewal-reward process {Φ_k, Δ_k} with a ±1 walk W_k, where P(W = 1) = p (p. 5). Assumption 2.1 (pp. 5–6) requires Δ_k to be bounded above, Δ_{k+1} ≥ min(Δ_k e^{λW_{k+1}}, Δ_ε) before stopping, and E[Φ_{k+1} | F_k] ≤ Φ_k − Θh(Δ_k). Theorem 2.2 (p. 9) gives E[T_ε] ≤ p/(2p−1) · Φ_0/(Θh(Δ_ε)) + 1. (b) STORM (Algorithm 1, p. 10) is a classical trust region, "essentially a minor modification" (p. 3). It uses random models that are α-probabilistically κ-fully linear (errors κ_eg δ_k and κ_ef δ_k²) and function estimates f_k⁰, f_k^s that are β-probabilistically ε_F-accurate with error ε_F δ_k² (Defs 3.1–3.3, pp. 11–12). A step is accepted if ρ_k ≥ η₁ *and* ‖g_k‖ ≥ η₂δ_k (p. 10). The potential is Φ_k = νf(X_k) + (1−ν)Δ_k² (eq. 20, p. 14). The second-order variant (Algorithm 2, p. 21) uses fully quadratic models, a model criticality measure τ_k^m, estimates accurate to δ_k³, and Φ_k = νf + (1−ν)Δ_k³ (p. 26).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: ∇f is L-Lipschitz and f ≥ 0 without loss of generality (Assumption 3.1, p. 11). The model Hessian is bounded deterministically by κ_bhm (Assumption 3.2(a), p. 12). ε_F must be small relative to η₁η₂κ_fcd (Assumption 3.2(c), p. 12). Unbiasedness is explicitly not assumed (p. 4). Explicit probability conditions appear under particular constants: (αβ − 1/2)/((1−α)(1−β)) ≥ 10 + 30L/(40κ_eg), with a separate lower bound on β (Thm 3.1, pp. 15–16). The constants η₁ = 0.1, γ = 2 and κ_fcd = 0.5 are chosen "merely for convenience" (p. 14). A parameter trade-off is spelled out: a small η₂ allows larger steps but forces smaller ε_F, so the estimates must be "a lot more accurate than the models" (p. 14). The second-order case adds a Lipschitz Hessian, δ_max ≤ 1 "for simplicity", and Assumption 4.3(a), a *first-moment* bound E|F − f| ≤ κ_F δ_k³ (pp. 27–28). Its reason is given explicitly: without it, bad-estimate increases of order δ_k² could not be balanced by good-step decreases of order δ_k³ (p. 28).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: (i) A birth-death process Z_{k+1} = min(Z_k + W_{k+1}, j_ε). Renewals occur when Δ_k ≥ Δ_ε, and the expected inter-arrival time is E[τ] = p/(2p−1), by first-step analysis plus the random-walk hitting time (Lemma 2.1, pp. 6–7). (ii) R_k = Φ_{k∧T} + ΘΣ_{j<k∧T} h(Δ_j) is a nonnegative supermartingale. With monotone convergence this gives E[N(T_ε − 1) + 1] ≤ Φ_0/(Θh(Δ_ε)) (Lemma 2.2, pp. 7–8). (iii) Wald's identity for nonnegative increments is proved *without* assuming T is a.s. finite, so convergence becomes a by-product of the rate (pp. 8–9). (iv) For STORM the case analysis splits on ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≥ ζδ_k versus < ζδ_k, then on (I_k, J_k) ∈ {good, bad}², and chooses ν so that decrease in f dominates radius growth (eq. 25, p. 16). In the small-gradient case, the increase on a bad-estimate iteration is bounded by Taylor with step ≤ δ_k, f(x+s) − f(x) < C_3ζδ_k². β is then chosen so this increase is at most half the unsuccessful-step decrease (pp. 17–18). Parts identical to Thm 4.11 of [8] are omitted (p. 15). Results: E[T_ε] = O(ε⁻²/(2p−1)) first order (p. 4; Thm 3.3, p. 19), with Θ = (1/1800)·min{η₂β, κ_eg⁻¹} (p. 16). Second order is O(ε⁻³/(2p−1)) (Thm 4.2, p. 32). The a.s. liminf results are corollaries (Thm 3.2, p. 19; Cor. 4.1, p. 32). The p-constant is p/(2p−1), unlike S014's 2p/(2p−1)². The paper does not comment on the difference.
- **D5 Experiments**: None (checked pp. 1–36). §3.5 only shows how sample averages would satisfy the assumptions. Sample sizes are O(σ_f²/Δ_k⁴) for f and Õ(σ_g²/(κ_eg²Δ_k²)) for g, with log(1/(1−√α)) factors (pp. 19–20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper claims "the first global complexity bound for a stochastic trust-region method" (p. 1). It is explicitly "an extension of [6] and [16] to the case of stochastic functions" (pp. 3–4). It improves [8] by dropping η₂ ≥ κ_ef (Remark 3.1, p. 12). It compares its order with Ghadimi–Lan and with variance-reduced rates [25, 22], but it "never requires computation of the true gradient" (p. 4). Scheinberg's own SARAH papers are among the variance-reduced methods cited (refs [21, 22]; pp. 2, 4). The critique of SVRG is practical: it is a hybrid that fits neither the sequential-SGD nor the distributed-full-gradient setting (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract labels a "novel framework" and a "first" (p. 1). The contribution paragraph is framed as "twofold" (p. 4). After the assumptions, a prose walk-through of the four (I_k, J_k) outcomes says which can increase f (p. 12), and "Choosing constants" paragraphs explain the roles and sizes of η₂, ε_F, α and β before the proofs (pp. 14, 26). A limitation is stated early and plainly in the introduction (p. 4). The arXiv text has small slips: max{δ_max, γδ_k} written for min (p. 15), and a Case 1 conclusion written with the Case 2 conditioning event (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are theory (a reusable framework) plus the first rates for a stochastic trust-region method. Limits stated: no termination criterion yet; the tail of the complexity distribution "will follow" but is not given (p. 4); the second-order result needs the extra moment Assumption 4.3 (p. 28); the analysis requires progressively more accurate estimates (p. 33). The constants are very large, with Θ carrying a 1/1800 factor (p. 16). The framework "has already been used" for stochastic line search [24] (p. 33).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ strong evidence from full text, confirming the reconstructed steps 1–3 and 5. The potential mixes f and the radius (Φ_k = νf + (1−ν)Δ_k², p. 14). The radius follows an upward-biased ±1 walk when p = αβ > 1/2 (Lemma 3.5, p. 15). The expected stopping time is bounded by a supermartingale plus renewal-reward argument with a p/(2p−1) constant (pp. 5–10).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. There are two contracts: models (α, errors ∝ δ_k and δ_k²) and function estimates (β, error ε_F δ_k²), each conditioned on the past (pp. 11–12), and unbiasedness is not assumed (p. 4).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant on the claim that *occasional arbitrary failures are tolerated*. This holds for first-order STORM, because a wrongly accepted step's true increase is bounded through step ≤ δ_k (p. 17). In the second-order analysis, estimate failures need a first-moment bound (Assumption 4.3, p. 28).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The trust region is kept, with only the ‖g_k‖ ≥ η₂δ_k acceptance guard (p. 10), and the restrictive η₂ assumption is removed by better analysis (p. 12).
- Heuristic 2 ✅ (function values under the same radius-tied contract, p. 11). Taste mark 7 ✅: the paper stops at expected complexity and names tails as the next step (p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate: framework-then-instantiation: the same Theorem 2.2 is instantiated twice here, first and second order (pp. 14–19, 26–32), and "can be applied beyond the algorithms discussed in this paper" (p. 33).
- New-pattern candidate: knowable-quantity accuracy requirements: "all sample sizes are determined by quantities that are generally either chosen or known by the algorithm" (p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A renewal-reward template for adaptive stochastic methods. Show three things: (a) the step parameter is capped; (b) below a threshold Δ_ε it moves like min(Δe^{λW}, Δ_ε) with P(W = +1) ≥ p > 1/2; (c) E[ΔΦ] ≤ −Θh(Δ_k) before stopping. Then E[T] ≤ p/(2p−1) · Φ_0/(Θh(Δ_ε)) + 1 follows without further work (pp. 5–9).
- Prove the stopping-time bound without first proving that T is finite a.s. (Wald for nonnegative increments, pp. 8–9). Almost-sure convergence then follows from the rate.
- Potential Φ_k = νf(X_k) + (1−ν)Δ_k^q, with q matched to the per-step decrease order (2 for first order, 3 for second order) and ν chosen large enough that function decrease beats radius growth (pp. 14, 16, 26, 28).
- Case grid for proofs: {large gradient vs small gradient relative to ζδ_k} × {good/bad model} × {good/bad estimates}. In the small-gradient cell, bound the worst increase via Lipschitz with ‖s‖ ≤ δ_k and choose β to make it at most half the guaranteed decrease (pp. 16–18).
- Order-mismatch diagnosis: if the bad case can raise f by O(δ²) while good steps only guarantee O(δ³), a probability-only contract will not work. Add an expectation bound of the matching order on estimate errors (p. 28).
- Write a "Choosing constants" paragraph that explains which parameter trades against which (small η₂ means larger steps but more accurate estimates) before the heavy proof (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "The key to the analysis lies in the assumption that the accuracy improves in coordination with the perceived progress of the algorithm." (p. 11)
- "It is convenient and useful not to have to prove the convergence result before establishing the convergence rates bounds, since the convergence immediately follows from these bounds." (p. 8)

### S015 · A stochastic line search method with expected complexity analysis (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2020, DOI 10.1137/18m1216250)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: C. Paquette · **Position in trajectory**: the line-search instance of the S013 renewal-reward framework (S013 is its ref. [3], pp. 4, 8, 25), with *random function values* (unlike S014's exact f). **Text read**: arXiv:1807.07994v1 (20 Jul 2018), pp. 1–26, read in full. There is no appendix. This preprint carries the earlier title "A Stochastic Line Search Method with Convergence Rate Analysis" (p. 1). It is the same paper as the listed arXiv id, but the published SIOPT version was not read, so any changes there (e.g., added experiments) are unknown.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper starts from practice (p. 1). SGD directions may not be descent directions, and SGD is "sensitive to the step-size (learning rate)", so the authors "turn to the deterministic optimization approach": GD with Armijo backtracking. The gaps in related work (p. 2) are these. Dynamic-sampling methods cover only convex problems and need a known small step plus pre-set sample growth. Bollapragada et al. [4] choose samples from the unknown true gradient. The line searches in [4, 9] are heuristics on the same batch. Probabilistic Wolfe [14] has no guarantees.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (p. 3) is backtracking Armijo run on estimates, f_k^s ≤ f_k⁰ − α_kθ‖g_k‖². On success α grows by γ (capped at α_max). On failure x stays and α shrinks. The single added device is a second control δ_k. A successful step is "reliable" if α_k‖g_k‖² ≥ δ_k², and δ_k² then grows; otherwise it shrinks. δ_k sets the variance of the function estimates when ‖∇f‖ is unknown ("Unlike classical back-tracking line search, there is an additional control", p. 3; rationale p. 4 and Remark 1, p. 6). The contract has three parts: gradients κ_g-accurate relative to A_k‖G_k‖ with probability p_g (Def. 2.2); function estimates ε_f-accurate relative to A_k²‖G_k‖² with probability p_f (Def. 2.3); and a variance bound max{κ_f²A_k²‖∇f‖⁴, θ²Δ_k⁴} (Assumption 2.4(iii), p. 5). The potential is Φ_k = ν(f − f_min) + (1−ν)A_k‖∇f(X_k)‖²/L² + (1−ν)θΔ_k² (eq. 4.6, p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Iterates stay in a set Ω on which ∇f is L-Lipschitz and f ≥ f_min (Assumption 2.1, p. 3). κ_g ≥ 2 without loss of generality (Remark 1, p. 6). The constants are θ = 1/2 and γ = 2 (Remark 4, p. 16). The explicit probability requirements are p_g ≥ 16/17 and p_g p_f/√(1−p_f) ≥ max{1024κ_f L²(κ_gα_max+1)² + 64, 1024(κ_gα_max+1)²} (Thm 4.7, p. 16). This sits in tension with the claim that p_g "is not close to 1" (p. 7), and the paper concedes that p_f must depend on κ_g (p. 7). If p_f = 1, the variance condition is unnecessary and p_g > 1/2 suffices (p. 6). The convex case assumes bounded Ω and ‖∇f‖ ≤ L_f and admits that iterates need not stay in a level set (Remark 5, p. 19). It also caps Δ_k ≤ δ_max (Assumption 4.13, p. 20) and sets α_max ≤ 1 without loss of generality (p. 20). Gradients need not be unbiased and may come from stochastic finite differences (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The general theorem is borrowed from S013 (Thm 3.3, p. 8; proofs in [3]). The new work is proving the expected decrease of Φ_k (Thm 4.6, pp. 11–16). Three cases: good gradient and good estimates, bad gradient and good estimates, bad estimates, each split into successful-reliable, successful-unreliable and unsuccessful. Table 1 summarizes them (p. 11). The A_k‖∇f‖²/L² term absorbs the growth of ‖∇f‖² after successful steps (Lemma 4.4, p. 10). Bad-estimate expectations are controlled by Hölder: E[1_{J^c}|F − f|] ≤ (1−p_f)^{1/2} · max{…} (Lemma 2.5, p. 6; Case 3, pp. 14–15). The liminf result couples log A_k ≥ Z_k, where Z_k is a capped random walk with upward drift (Thm 4.9, pp. 17–18). Convex and strongly convex rates come from the same decrease, applied to the stopped transforms Ψ_k = 1/(νε) − 1/Φ_{k∧T} and Ψ_k = log Φ_{k∧T} + log(1/(νε)) with Jensen. These mirror the deterministic 1/(f − f*) and log(f − f*) arguments (pp. 19–22). Rates: O(ε⁻²) nonconvex with a p_g p_f/(2p_g p_f − 1) factor (Thm 4.11, p. 19), O(1/ε) convex (Thm 4.15, p. 21), O(log 1/ε) strongly convex (Thm 4.18, p. 22). The general descent-direction extension is in §4.7 (pp. 23–25).
- **D5 Experiments**: None in the v1 text read (pp. 1–26). §2.3 gives only sample-size recipes: |S_k| ≥ Õ(V_g/(κ_g²A_k²‖G_k‖²)) for gradients, a loop over guesses of ‖G_k‖ (p. 7), and |S_k⁰| ≥ V_f/(θ²Δ_k⁴) for the variance condition (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper claims "the first stochastic line search method with convergence rates analysis", and the first rates for adaptive sample sizes "based on knowable quantities" (p. 2). It contrasts itself item by item with [5, 9] (pre-set sample growth), [18] (samples fixed large a priori from ε) and [4] (samples depending on the unknown ‖∇f‖) (p. 7). It positions itself as reusing [3]'s framework (pp. 4, 7, 25) and, when p_f = 1, as "an extension of results in [6]" (S014) (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A "Challenges with randomized line-search" passage lists the two failure modes a proof must defeat: α collapsing to 0, and false acceptance raising f (p. 4). Remark 3 explains the stochastic-process assumptions by analogy with the deterministic fact that α never falls much below 1/L (p. 8). Table 1 compresses a 5-page case proof (p. 11). There are "first" claims in the abstract and introduction (pp. 1–2). As a v1 preprint it has cross-reference slips: (4.42) is used on p. 13 but defined on p. 25; Thm 4.15 refers to "The bound in Theorem 4.18" (p. 21); Thm 4.11 states ‖∇f‖² ≤ ε while T_ε is defined by ‖∇f‖ < ε (pp. 18–19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are an algorithm plus analysis: an implementable stochastic backtracking method with rates matching deterministic GD in all three regimes (p. 25). Limits visible in the text: very high required p_f, and p_g ≥ 16/17 (p. 16); the bounded-iterate assumption for the convex case (Remark 5, p. 19); no numerical evidence in this version; expected complexity only. No future-work paragraph is given (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ strong evidence. The paper keeps "a classical backtracking Armijo line-search" (p. 1) and adds exactly one control (δ_k), justified by the second failure mode that estimates introduce (pp. 3–4).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. Accuracy is relative to the current step, A_k‖G_k‖ and A_k²‖G_k‖² (p. 5). There is no unbiasedness, and finite-difference gradients are allowed (p. 7).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. In the line search, function-estimate failures are *not* arbitrary. They are controlled through a variance condition, "the key reason for Assumption 2.4(iii)" (pp. 5, 14). The required probabilities are also near 1: p_g ≥ 16/17, and p_f depends on κ_g (pp. 7, 16).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. The potential mixes f, the step parameter and the second control (p. 11), with an upward-drift random walk on A_k (pp. 4, 17–18), a hitting-time bound with a p/(2p−1)-type constant (p. 19), and the stated preference for bounding expected iterations over expected error after T (Remark 2, p. 8).
- Taste mark 2 ✅. Deterministic-GD orders hold for nonconvex, convex and strongly convex problems (pp. 19–22).
- New-pattern candidate: framework-then-instantiation: S013's theorem is imported verbatim, and only its assumption (iii) is re-proved (pp. 7–8, 11).
- New-pattern candidate: knowable-quantity accuracy requirements (pp. 2, 7, 25).
- New-pattern candidate: subsume the competitor: with p_f = 1 the result extends [6] (p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a classical adaptive method has two failure modes under noise (parameter collapse, and false acceptance), give each its own control. Here the step parameter handles the first, and a second parameter δ_k, which sets estimate accuracy and is updated by a "reliable step" test, handles the second (pp. 3–4).
- Put a term A_k‖∇f(X_k)‖²/L² in the potential, so that the possible growth of ‖∇f‖ after an accepted step is paid for by the potential itself (Lemma 4.4, p. 10; eq. 4.6, p. 11).
- Hölder trick for rare bad events: E[1_{bad}·|error|] ≤ P(bad)^{1/2}·(second-moment bound)^{1/2}. The bad-case contribution is then small when P(bad) is small, even though the error itself is unbounded (Lemma 2.5, p. 6).
- Obtain convex and strongly convex rates from one nonconvex expected-decrease inequality by applying the same stopping-time theorem to 1/(νε) − 1/Φ_{k∧T} and log Φ_{k∧T} + log(1/(νε)) with Jensen (pp. 19–22).
- Summarize a case-heavy expected-decrease proof in a table of (event × step type) to sign of ΔΦ before writing it out (Table 1, p. 11).
- State every sample-size rule in terms the algorithm knows (α_k, ‖g_k‖, δ_k, variance bounds), and argue against competitors whose sample sizes need ‖∇f(x_k)‖ or ε (p. 7).
