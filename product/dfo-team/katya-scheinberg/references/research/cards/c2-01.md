### S008 · On the convergence of derivative-free methods for unconstrained optimization (Approximation Theory and Optimization: Tributes to M. J. D. Powell, Cambridge University Press 1997, pp. 83–108; no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: Scheinberg's first convergence-theory paper, written as a Columbia IEOR PhD student (affiliation p. 1) with support from an IBM Cooperative Fellowship (p. 22). It was an invited talk at the Powellfest, Cambridge, July 1996 (p. 1), and is dedicated to Powell's 60th birthday (p. 2). It supplies the proofs that the 1997 survey S004 defers to it. The file is Namur Report 96/10 (Dec 1996, p. 1); the book page range is taken from its citation in S019 (p. 11). **Read**: full text. References skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is practitioner demand plus a theory gap. f is expensive and comes from a physical measure or from a simulation whose source code is unavailable (p. 2). Direct search wastes evaluations because it does not exploit smoothness (p. 2); automatic differentiation needs a callable program (p. 3); finite differences with quasi-Newton updates are useful but spend evaluations on derivatives (p. 3). Powell's 1974 secant-vs-Newton argument (rate 1.618 vs ≈1.414 per function value) is quoted at length as the reason to model f itself (p. 3). Powell's interpolation methods already had "encouraging numerical results" (Conn–Toint 1996, Powell 1996, p. 5). The stated purpose is therefore theoretical: to define "a broad class of derivative free trust-region methods and consider their convergence theory" (p. 3). Problem statement: min f(x), f smooth, ∇f cannot be computed (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: A trust-region method on a quadratic interpolation model m_k(x_k+s) = f(x_k) + ⟨g_k, s⟩ + ½⟨s, H_k s⟩ (3.2) (p. 6), stated as a class (p. 14–15):
  1. Step 1: build the model; if ‖g_k‖ ≤ ε_g and Y is inadequate in A_k = {‖y − x_k‖ ≤ μ‖g_k‖}, improve geometry until Y is adequate in a ball of radius ≤ μ‖g_k‖ (a criticality-type inner loop, "close in spirit to a proposal of Carter (1991)", p. 15).
  2. Step 2: minimize the model in B_k and compute ρ_k.
  3. Step 3: if ρ_k ≥ η_1, insert the trial point; if ρ_k < η_1 and Y is inadequate in B_k, improve geometry.
  4. Step 4: enlarge Δ on success; shrink Δ **only if ρ_k < η_1 and Y is adequate in B_k**; otherwise keep Δ.
  5. Step 5: move to the best interpolation point if it gives ρ̂_k ≥ η_0 (p. 15).

  Kept: the ratio test, Cauchy decrease and radius logic (p. 17–18). Changed, as the paper itself lists in "three important aspects" (p. 17): the model gradient need not match ∇f, so coherence must be checked; model validity depends on geometry, so the radius cannot be cut before geometry is satisfactory; and the iterate may move to a geometry point with lower f (Step 5), so progress can happen even with a bad model. The differences are "enough to make the standard convergence theory inapplicable" (p. 18). Geometry is measured with Newton fundamental polynomials rather than Powell's Lagrange polynomials, chosen because of the Sauer–Xu error formula (p. 7). The geometry operations are specified only abstractly: "There is no need to specify exactly how this is done for the purpose of our theory", as long as adequacy is reached after finitely many improvements (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: A.1 f ∈ C² with gradient and Hessian uniformly bounded on all of Rⁿ; A.2 f bounded below; A.3 model Hessians uniformly bounded (p. 6). The authors say the set was chosen "to keep our analysis simple and hopefully intuitive" (p. 6), i.e. for convenience; A.1's global gradient bound is strong. *Adequate in Q_k(δ)* (3.14)–(3.16) means: at least n+1 points ("at least fully linear"), all points within δ of x_k, and Newton-polynomial values bounded by a constant larger than 2^{|Y^[d]|} (p. 11). So the theory needs only fully linear models (p. 11). No noise; unconstrained.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Deterministic, asymptotic, no rates.
  - Sauer–Xu path-sum error bound (3.8): interpolation error ≤ a constant times the sum over paths of products of point distances and Newton-polynomial values (p. 9).
  - Theorem 1 (p. 10): with a pivot threshold θ, all Newton polynomials stay bounded on the ball, by induction over the pivots of procedure CNP (bound M becomes M + M²/θ). The authors note the bound can blow up as θ → 0 (p. 10).
  - Theorem 2 (p. 10–11), constructive existence: choose each point as the argmax of the current Newton polynomial over the ball (3.11). Normalization makes each maximum ≤ 1 and each later update at most doubles it, giving the explicit bound 2^{|Y^[d]|} (3.13).
  - Lemma 3 (p. 11–12): for linear models the Theorem 2 construction has pivots ≥ δ. The authors then say it is "of little theoretical use" because the converse fails: passing the pivot threshold does not certify adequacy, so adequacy cannot be decided from pivots alone (p. 12).
  - Theorem 4 (p. 12–13), the fully-linear bound: adequacy gives |f − m_k| ≤ κ_em max[δ², δ³] and ‖∇f(x_k) − g_k‖ ≤ κ_eg max[δ, δ²]. The gradient bound comes from the function bound by Taylor-expanding f and m_k, subtracting, and choosing the direction h = δ(∇f − g_k)/‖∇f − g_k‖ (p. 13). The constant κ_em = 2n⁶κ_hκ_n (reconstructed; p. 12) hides a steep dimension dependence.
  - Lemma 5 (p. 16–17): (ii) radius reductions happen only with adequate geometry; (iii) the Step-1 loop is finite unless ∇f(x_k) = 0, shown by shrinking the ball by ω and the triangle inequality ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≤ (1/μ + κ_eg)ω^i ε_g → 0.
  - Lemma 7 (p. 18–19): the radius is bounded below while ‖g_k‖ ≥ ε_g. At the first iteration where Δ would drop below the threshold, the geometry is adequate (Lemma 5(ii)), so Theorem 4 forces ρ_k ≥ η_1, a contradiction.
  - Theorems 8–12 (p. 19–22) follow the standard trust-region skeleton: finitely many successes give a stationary limit; liminf ‖g_k‖ = 0 by summing Cauchy decrease over successes against A.2; Lemma 10 transfers g_k → 0 to ∇f → 0 through the criticality loop; Theorem 12 shows every limit point is critical with the usual *sum of radii over a bad stretch ≤ decrease* argument.
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The paper points to numerics in Conn–Toint 1996 and Powell 1996 (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: A whole section surveys Powell's DFO work: conjugate directions (VA04/VA24, PRAXIS), B-conjugate methods, NS01 for nonlinear equations, COBYLA and the 1994 quadratic interpolation method, called "the main source of our inspiration" (p. 4–5). The Newton-vs-Lagrange choice is framed as "At variance with Powell's proposals" but justified by the available error formula, not by a claim of superiority (p. 7). Sauer–Xu is credited as the foundation (p. 2, p. 22), and Sauer and de Boor are thanked (p. 22). Carter 1991 (inexact-gradient trust regions) and Moré 1983 (Cauchy point) are the only trust-region theory cited (p. 15, p. 18).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A modest purpose sentence: "reasonable global convergence results" (abstract, p. 2). Self-limiting hedges: "admittedly simplistic" and "merely to explore a framework" (p. 15). An organization paragraph comes early (p. 3). After Lemma 5, Lemma 7 and before Theorem 9 there is a plain-language paragraph on what each result means (p. 17, p. 19). The paper flags a weakness of its own lemma (Lemma 3, p. 12) and says the geometry constant "only serves a theoretical purpose", with threshold pivoting as the practical rule and the Theorem 2 construction as "a last resort" (p. 11). Theorems and intuition are roughly balanced.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Theory: a global convergence framework (every limit point critical, Theorem 12, p. 21–22) for a class of interpolation trust-region methods. Stated future work (p. 22): find which members of the class perform best numerically, extend the class, and see whether bounds other than Sauer–Xu could give similar results. Unstated limits: no rates or complexity; the global gradient bound in A.1; no algorithmic adequacy test (the Lemma 3 gap); and the geometry-improvement step may need to replace many points, which the 2008 paper S016 later calls "very expensive" (S016 p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ strong evidence (the 1997 origin of the design). Geometry is the certificate that gates radius reduction (Step 4, p. 14; Lemma 5(ii), p. 16), fully linear models are enough for the theory (p. 11), and geometry is enforced at exactly the two places Method 4 step 2 names: unsuccessful iterations about to shrink Δ, and the criticality loop (p. 14–15).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (deterministic prototype). The trust-region skeleton is kept, and the paper lists the three places where the standard proof breaks before re-proving only what they break (p. 17–18). This is the *mark the first inequality that fails* move.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (deterministic ancestor; my interpretation). Lemma 7's *radius bounded below away from criticality* (p. 18–19) is the deterministic lemma that the later stochastic analyses replace by a step-parameter random walk. Here the result is liminf/lim only, with no complexity.
- Method 6 ✅ weak evidence. With the monomial basis, a known zero Hessian entry is imposed by dropping x_i x_j from the basis (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *abstract algorithm class with pluggable quality predicates* (second instance after S004). Geometry insertion and improvement are left unspecified except for the properties the proof needs (p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate: *safeguard test with fallback to the analysable step* (existing pool name). Threshold pivoting is the practical test and the provable argmax construction of Theorem 2 is "a last resort" (p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Turn a uniform function-error bound into a gradient-error bound. Taylor-expand f and the model at x_k, subtract, and choose h = δ(∇f − g)/‖∇f − g‖ (p. 12–13). It takes one paragraph and works for any model class.
- [D2] Gate the radius decrease on a model certificate: shrink Δ only if the model was certified when the step was computed; otherwise repair the model and keep Δ (Step 4, p. 14). *Every radius reduction happens with a good model* then becomes a free lemma (Lemma 5(ii), p. 16).
- [D2/D4] Criticality loop: when the model gradient is small, re-certify the model in a ball of radius ≤ μ‖g_k‖ and shrink by ω until ‖g_k‖ ≥ ε_g. The loop is finite unless x_k is stationary (Step 1, p. 14; Lemma 5(iii), p. 16–17). This ties model accuracy to the size of the model gradient.
- [D4] Prove a well-conditioned set exists by construction: pick each point as the maximizer of the current basis polynomial over the ball, so normalization plus at-most-doubling updates give an explicit bound (Theorem 2, p. 10–11).
- [D7] Before reusing a standard theory, list the ways your algorithm departs from its framework (here "three important aspects", p. 17) and re-prove only what those departures break.

**Quotes**:
- "This implies that the model cannot be judged locally inappropriate (and, consequently, the trust-region radius reduced) before the geometry of the interpolation set is itself satisfactory." (p. 17)
- "We recall here that our purpose is merely to explore a framework in which convergence theory can be discussed, and the above will serve that goal." (p. 15)

### S019 · A derivative free optimization algorithm in practice (7th AIAA/USAF/NASA/ISSMO Symposium on Multidisciplinary Analysis and Optimization 1998, DOI 10.2514/6.1998-4718)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The practice companion of the 1997 pair (S004 survey, S008 theory), which it cites as its refs [5] and [6] (p. 3, p. 11). Scheinberg is now at IBM T. J. Watson (p. 1–2). The file is Namur Report 98/11 (July 1998), accepted for the AIAA conference in St Louis, September 1998 (p. 1). **Read**: full text. Figures 2–3 are axis residue in the text file, but their captions are readable (p. 11). The paper has no conclusion section; it ends with the results (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is engineering applications. Boeing's helicopter rotor-blade design has 11, 31 or about 50 variables (p. 2); one simplified evaluation takes about a minute, so one finite-difference gradient in 31 variables takes more than half an hour (p. 3). An engine-nozzle design has about 10 parameters and hours per evaluation (p. 3). A water-network calibration at the University of Colorado at Denver has up to 100 variables and 20 s per evaluation (p. 3). The goal is to use as few evaluations as possible, "using any function value as soon as it becomes available" (p. 2). The paper says it is mainly concerned with practical issues and numerical performance (p. 3). f is assumed C², "although in the application noise is typically present" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The DFO algorithm is given in prose steps (p. 3–4): quadratic interpolation model; trust-region minimization; ratio test; interpolation-set update (add without dropping if possible, include a good point even if another must go, search for a model-improving point after a bad step, drop points far from x_k); radius increase on a good ratio and decrease **only if the model is at least fully linear**; the new iterate is the best sample point (p. 4). Sample points are of two kinds, those that "hopefully" lower f and those that improve the model, and each kind can serve the other purpose (p. 3, p. 5). Newton fundamental polynomials select the largest poised subset, find points that improve poisedness, and choose which point to replace (p. 5). Design choices:
  - locality over size: a smaller set is preferred to one with distant points (p. 5);
  - start from 2 points and grow the model while decreasing f (p. 5);
  - for underdetermined models, "the best general strategy" is the minimum Frobenius norm of the quadratic coefficient matrix (p. 6);
  - use prior knowledge: quadratic terms only for important variables, zero coefficients for known separability (p. 6).

  Constraints are split into three classes (p. 3, p. 6–8). "Easy" constraints (cheap values and derivatives, e.g. bounds or a linear mass limit) go directly into the trust-region subproblem (0.3), so every evaluated point is feasible (p. 6). Equality constraints reduce the dimension of the interpolation space: in R⁴ with two linear equalities a full quadratic needs 6 points instead of 15 (p. 6–7). *Difficult* constraints (expensive, no derivatives) are out of scope (p. 7). "Virtual" constraints are hidden: one only learns that an evaluation failed, at the cost of a full evaluation (p. 7–8). The heuristic temporarily shrinks the trust region and re-solves, and treats a too-small radius as a local minimum (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f ∈ C², derivatives unavailable, evaluations expensive, noise typically present (p. 2); n usually < 100 (p. 2). All constraints are assumed defined by smooth functions, even when unknown (p. 6). For virtual constraints, the set where f can be evaluated is assumed to be the feasible set of smooth constraints (p. 8). No assumption is flagged as a proof convenience, because there are no proofs.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No proofs. Global convergence is claimed "under reasonable assumptions" by citing [5] (= S008), with the remark that only a fully linear model is needed (p. 5). The arguments are counting and informal:
  - a full quadratic in 31 variables needs 528 evaluations, while a very good solution came after about 180 (p. 5);
  - noise matters only when the trust-region radius is small relative to the noise level, typically near a solution, whereas finite-difference gradients can be very bad if the noise level is not known in advance (p. 5–6);
  - the virtual-constraint heuristic is shown to fail with a picture (Figure 1, p. 7–8): all trial points in shrinking boxes are infeasible, yet x_k is not a local minimum.
- **D5 Experiments**: Three CUTE/Hock–Schittkowski tables plus the Boeing helicopter problem (p. 8–11).
  - Baselines: PDS (direct search), LANCELOT with finite-difference gradients, and Powell's COBYLA (linear interpolation) (p. 8). All are in Fortran 77, double precision except COBYLA (single precision); the DFO subproblem is solved by NPSOL (p. 8).
  - Table 1: 26 unconstrained CUTE problems, n = 2–16. Table 2: 13 inequality-constrained HS problems. Table 3: 16 equality-constrained HS problems, LANCELOT only, because PDS handles no constraints and COBYLA no equalities (p. 9–10).
  - Metrics: function evaluations and final f. CPU time is omitted as "not relevant in our context" (p. 10). DFO's tolerance is 10⁻⁴; the others use "appropriate comparable default values", so accuracy is not matched (p. 10). A "*" marks early termination, with the reason given for each code (p. 10).
  - Outcome (my count from the tables): DFO uses fewer evaluations than PDS on all 26 unconstrained problems, but LANCELOT-FD uses fewer than DFO on 16 of 26, often reaching a better final value (e.g. DQDRTIC 33 vs 403; GROWTHLS final 1.004 vs 12.40) (p. 9). On constrained problems DFO needs fewer evaluations than LANCELOT on 12 of 13 (Table 2) and 15 of 16 (Table 3) (p. 9–10). The text reports the tables without commenting on the unconstrained LANCELOT wins (p. 10).
  - Helicopter: 10 runs each for n = 11 and n = 31, same start, random second initial point; the runs land in different local minima (Figs 2–3, p. 10–11). Evaluation counts include the 15–20% failed evaluations (p. 10). Most solutions beat those "previously obtained by other means", and very good values appear after about 60 evaluations, although runs stop after about 200 (p. 10–11).
- **D6 Positioning**: The algorithm is the one of [5] and [6] (S008, S004), with Conn–Toint 1996 as prototype and Powell 1994/1996 as inspiration (p. 3). The competitors cover direct search, finite-difference quasi-Newton (the authors' own LANCELOT) and Powell's interpolation code (p. 8). Booker et al. 1998 (the Boeing surrogate framework) is cited for comparisons on the helicopter problem (p. 11). Finite-difference methods are described as fragile under unknown noise (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A practice-paper voice: the algorithm is in bullet prose with only the ratio formula (p. 3–4). Contribution sentence: the paper is mainly concerned with practical issues (p. 3). Hedges: the method seems to have a reasonable chance of finding a global minimum (p. 5, paraphrase; the extraction garbles the sentence). The paper names the weakness of its own heuristic, calling its flaw clear (p. 8), and says the heuristic gives no optimality guarantee (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution levels: algorithm-in-practice and software behaviour, a constraint taxonomy (easy / difficult / virtual), and an industrial application. Stated limits: the virtual-constraint heuristic can stop at non-minimizers (p. 8); difficult constraints are left to ongoing research (p. 7); only local stationarity is guaranteed (p. 5); the helicopter problem has many local minima (p. 10). Future work: "More sophisticate heuristics" for virtual constraints (p. 8). Unstated limits: no equal-accuracy protocol across solvers (p. 10) and no discussion of the unconstrained cases where LANCELOT-FD wins (p. 9–10).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (practice side of *pay only the minimum*). Only a fully linear model is needed, and the radius is not reduced until the model is fully linear (p. 5). Good solutions after about 180 evaluations against 528 for one full quadratic (p. 5). Minimum-Frobenius-norm underdetermined models (p. 6).
- Method 6 ✅ strong evidence. Cheap constraints are kept exact in the subproblem (p. 6), exactly Method 6 step 2. Equality constraints are used to lower the interpolation dimension (p. 6–7), and prior knowledge of variable importance or separability shapes the model (p. 6).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Solvers are compared by function evaluations with CPU time explicitly set aside (p. 10), but stopping tolerances are not matched (p. 10). This is the evaluation-count half of Method 5 without the equal-accuracy half.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (deterministic precursor). The noise argument ties the damage from noise to the size of the trust-region radius relative to the noise level (p. 5–6). This is the Δ-versus-noise intuition that Method 1 later turns into an accuracy contract, and Method 4 step 5 into a noise-dependent radius floor.
- New-pattern candidate: *constraint taxonomy by information and cost*. Classify constraints by what the oracle returns and what it costs (cheap with derivatives / expensive black box / hidden pass-fail), then treat each class differently (p. 3, p. 6–8).
- New-pattern candidate: *state your own heuristic's failure with a counter-picture* (Figure 1, p. 7–8; the text calls the flaw clear, p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Motivate with a cost calculation taken from the application: one finite-difference gradient in 31 variables at a minute per evaluation takes more than 30 minutes, and one full quadratic model needs 528 points (p. 3, p. 5).
- [D2] Put constraints that are cheap relative to f into the trust-region subproblem, so no expensive evaluation is spent on an infeasible point (0.3) (p. 6).
- [D2] Use equality constraints to shrink the model space: the number of points needed for a full quadratic depends on the dimension of the feasible affine set, not on n (p. 6–7).
- [D5] Put function evaluations and final f for every solver in one table, mark early terminations with the reason, and say why CPU time is omitted (p. 9–10).
- [D5] On a multimodal real application, run 10 restarts that differ only in one random initial sample, plot all the trajectories, and count failed evaluations in the budget (Figs 2–3, p. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "Our algorithm proves to be very robust in case of noise." (p. 6)
- "We do not list the CPU time, since it is not relevant in our context." (p. 10)

### S003 · Efficient SVM training using low-rank kernel representations (Journal of Machine Learning Research 2 (2001) 243–264, no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shai Fine (IBM Human Language Technologies) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Scheinberg's first ML-optimization paper, from the IBM Mathematical Sciences department (p. 1). It carries her interior-point linear-algebra work for LP (Goldfarb–Scheinberg 1999, product-form Cholesky for dense columns; Scheinberg–Wright 2000) into SVM training (p. 2, p. 7, p. 11). The 2006 paper S022 cites it and uses its low-rank IPM as a warm start. **Read**: full text. Figures 3–7 are axis residue in the text file, but their captions are readable (p. 16–20). The degenerate-case recurrence in Fig. 1 is only partly readable (p. 9–10). ε is lost from the extraction of Theorem 1 and of the introduction's bound (p. 3, p. 15); it is restored below from the theorem's hypothesis. References skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The SVM dual QP scales with the number of training points n, and a general IPM iteration costs O(n³), so large problems are intractable for conventional methods (p. 1). Existing remedies solve sequences of small subproblems: Kernel-Adatron, SMO, chunking, decomposition, shrinking (p. 1–2). The new entry is the rank of the kernel matrix: "three key observations" (p. 2). If Q has low rank, an IPM can run in time linear in n; if not, Q may be approximated by a low-rank PSD matrix; and the resulting change in the optimal value "can be explicitely controlled by the quality of the approximation" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Keep the IPM (Mehrotra predictor-corrector, with the usual 99% step to the boundary, p. 5–6) unchanged and change only the linear algebra of its dominant step, computing (D + Q)⁻¹w with Q = VVᵀ in O(nk²) work and O(nk) storage instead of O(n³) and O(n²) (p. 2, p. 6). The argument for targeting the per-iteration cost is that an IPM "rarely takes more than 50 iterations, regardless of the problem’s size" (p. 6).
  - Two options are compared. Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury (SMW) costs about nk²/2 multiplications and a third of the storage (p. 7). Product-form Cholesky (PFC) costs about k²n multiplications and 3kn storage (p. 11) but is numerically stable (p. 11).
  - PFC is chosen because the IPM itself makes D ill-conditioned: near optimality each D_i tends to 0 or ∞ at the rate of μ, which is the regime where SMW fails (p. 8).
  - For full-rank kernels, an incomplete Cholesky factorization with symmetric pivoting greedily takes the column with the largest remaining diagonal (Fig. 2, p. 13). It costs O(nk²), stores only the diagonal of Q and computes the other columns on demand, so it can be "kernelized" (p. 13–14). It stops when the sum of the remaining diagonal entries, which equals tr(ΔQ), is ≤ tol (p. 14). This is contrasted with Smola–Schölkopf's randomized greedy selection at O(Nnk²) (p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Q is PSD, dense and too large, but of rank k ≪ n (p. 2). A scalar penalty c is used for simplicity; the results extend to a vector c and a general linear term (p. 4). Kernel spectra typically decay fast, though flat-spectrum kernels exist (footnote 2, p. 12). Theorem 1 needs Q − Q̃ PSD with tr(Q − Q̃) ≤ ε (p. 15). The stability proof of PFC exists for LP IPMs; for the SVM QP, "extending it is tedious and beyond interest of this paper" (p. 12). This is an admitted gap, filled by experiments.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Operation counts per solve: SMW nk²/2, PFC k²n, then O(kn) per back-solve (p. 7, p. 11).
  - Instability by a 2×2 example (Example 1, p. 7–8): D = diag(ε², 1), V = (1, −1)ᵀ with ε² below machine precision. SMW returns a solution "not even close" to the correct one, while PFC is correct (p. 8, p. 11). An argument from IPM complementarity then shows the same scaling can occur in SVM problems (data of order 10⁴ at μ = 10⁻⁸) (p. 8).
  - Trace identity: after i pivoted Cholesky steps, the sum of the remaining diagonals equals tr ΔQ_i, so the stopping test costs nothing extra (p. 14).
  - Perturbation sandwich (Theorem 1, p. 14–15). Both problems have the same feasible set. Optimality of x̃* for the perturbed problem and ΔQ ⪰ 0 give f̃(x̃*) ≤ f̃(x*) ≤ f(x*). Optimality of x* gives f(x*) − f̃(x̃*) ≤ f(x̃*) − f̃(x̃*) = ½ x̃*ᵀΔQ x̃*. The box 0 ≤ x ≤ c and the number l of positive components of x̃* then bound this by c²lε/2 (p. 14–15). The bound holds for any approximation with a PSD residual and a bounded trace; a non-PSD residual is handled via a norm bound in a companion paper (p. 15). A shorter proof is credited to R. Gopinath (p. 20).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - vs SMO (§5.1, p. 15–16): 150 speaker-ID binary problems, Fisher-kernel features in 204 dense dimensions, n ≈ 6500–7000, dot-product kernel. The SMO variant is specially designed for dot products, chosen "to obtain results of the SMO at the peek of its performance" (p. 15). CPU time on a log scale favours PFC-IPM; the gain over a traditional IPM is a factor of 1100 in computation and 33 in storage (p. 15–16).
  - vs SVMlight (§5.2, p. 16–17): Abalone (4177 points; all used for training because generalization is not evaluated), dot-product kernel, three values of c, sweeping SVMlight's chunk size. SVMlight is sensitive to chunk size and problem difficulty, while PFC-IPM needs no tuning because the whole QP fits in memory (p. 16–17).
  - SMW failure (§5.3, p. 17): on a constructed degenerate instance scaled by 10⁴, SMW stops at 2 digits and PFC reaches 8. On an Abalone polynomial-kernel approximation, SMW stalls at 5 digits and PFC reaches 12.
  - ICF quality (§5.4, p. 17–20): Abalone with a degree-5 polynomial kernel (3000 train / 1177 test). The optimal value and ‖w‖ (Fig. 6), and the training and testing errors (Fig. 7), are plotted against rank, each next to the Frobenius approximation error.

### S022 · An efficient implementation of an active set method for SVMs (Journal of Machine Learning Research 7 (2006) 2237–2257, no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none. Scheinberg is the sole author; K. P. Bennett and E. Parrado-Hernández are the issue's editors (p. 1), so the batch list's author field is a Scholar metadata error · **Position in trajectory**: IBM period (p. 1). It is the active-set sequel to S003: it cites Fine–Scheinberg 2001 as the IPM route for low-rank Q (p. 2) and uses that IPM as a warm start (§4.3, p. 17). The code, SVM-QP, is open source on COIN-OR (p. 10). **Read**: full text. All six tables are readable. References skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: IPMs suit sparse or low-rank Q (S003; Ferris–Munson 2000), and active-set methods suit dense problems of moderate size. SVM matrices are dense, so for high-rank Q "an active set approach seems to remain the only main alternative" (p. 2). Yet full active-set methods of simplex type for QP (Fletcher 1971; Goldfarb 1972; Goldfarb–Idnani 1983) "were considered impractical for large SVM problems" (p. 1; restated p. 19). Chunking and decomposition converge slowly near the optimum and are sensitive to a chunk size that cannot be predicted (p. 3). The paper states its novelty narrowly: the method is not new, the adaptation and implementation are (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: A dual active-set method (p. 5–6). Indices are split into I_0 (α = 0), I_c (α = c) and I_s (free; the support vectors exactly on the margin). With the bounds fixed, the reduced QP on I_s (with the equality constraint) is attacked; when it is solved, reduced costs s (on I_0) and ξ (on I_c) are priced and the most negative one enters I_s (Step 2, p. 6). The design moves:
  - only **one step** toward the reduced-QP solution per iteration, stopping at the first bound hit (p. 3, p. 6);
  - the active set changes by **one index at a time**, so the Cholesky factor of Q_ss is updated in O(n_s²) instead of refactorized in O(n_s³): a bordered update to add, Givens rotations to delete (p. 3, p. 9–10);
  - a singular Q_ss gets an infinite descent direction read off the singular Cholesky factor (Cases 1–3, following Frangioni 1996 and Kiwiel 1989, p. 7–9);
  - the vector Q_cc·c is maintained incrementally (p. 10);
  - pricing uses the most negative reduced cost rather than any negative one, following simplex practice (Vanderbei 2001), because "any violation" raises the iteration count (p. 13);
  - candidate sets are managed by SVMlight's shrinking or by *sprint* (sifting), an LP-industry strategy (Forrest 1989; Bixby et al. 1992) (p. 13–14); sprint also gives a memory-saving mode (p. 16);
  - warm starts come from a low-rank IPM solution (p. 16–17), and an incremental mode is provided (p. 17–19).

  One-at-a-time updates are defended with a toy case: ten identical, most-violated points cost ten times the work to add together, yet have the same effect as adding one (p. 3–4). Kept: the classical dual active-set QP method (Fletcher; Goldfarb; Nocedal–Wright). Changed: only the linear-algebra and pricing machinery, to fit SVM structure (p. 2–3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The standard soft-margin SVM with any nonnegative penalty vector c (p. 1). Q_ss may be singular even with an RBF kernel, e.g. two identical points with different labels (p. 7). The method assumes the number n_s of margin support vectors is moderate; if n_s is comparable to n it is impractical, and Remark 2 argues such problems probably overfit (p. 10). Q_s is stored within 400 MB in the experiments (p. 13). There are no proofs, so no proof-convenience assumptions.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No new convergence proof. Finite termination is inherited from Fletcher 1971 and Frangioni 1996: "In the worst case this number might be exponential, but it is hardly the case in practice" (p. 4). The paper also says the method can be adapted to Balcazar et al.'s randomized framework, "hence similar average case results apply", with no proof given (p. 4). The derivations are linear-algebraic:
  - Schur complement for the bias β and triangular solves for α_s (p. 6–7);
  - infinite directions for one, two and more zero eigenvalues, each verified by substitution (Q_ss d = 0, yᵀd = 0) (p. 7–9);
  - a structural lemma in prose: more than two zero eigenvalues can arise only from a warm start, because every infinite-direction step removes one index from I_s (p. 8);
  - pricing cost O(|I_s|(|I_0′| + |I_c′|)) per Step 2 (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Data: Letter-G (20,000 × 16), USPS OCR digits 0 and 9 (7291 × 256), Web and Adult (half of each set, 24,692 and 16,100 points), Abalone (4177), Spam (4601) (p. 10–11). RBF kernels with several σ, linear kernels for Letter and Spam, a degree-5 polynomial for Abalone, and several C values chosen to vary n_s and n_c (p. 11).
  - Protocol: CPU seconds on an IBM RS/6000, with the same memory for both codes (p. 10, p. 12). **SVMlight is run twice**, at its default accuracy 10⁻³ and at 10⁻⁶, the accuracy of SVM-QP; both codes apply the tolerance to the same constraints (p. 11).
  - Table 1: SVM-QP is faster than even the low-accuracy SVMlight on all problems, by at least 2× on almost all and 5× or more on a few; SVMlight failures are marked "-" (p. 12).
  - Table 2: runs on the full Adult and Web sets done independently by Hans Mittelmann (spelled "Mittleman" in the text) at Arizona State on another workstation. SVM-QP runs out of memory on one instance (n_s ≈ 10,000), marked "*" (p. 12–13). At the same tolerance the two codes report different support-vector counts (3446 vs 4025), explained by exact vs asymptotic identification of the active set (p. 12).
  - Ablations: sprint vs shrinking, where sprint wins "in most cases, especially on larger, more difficult problems" (Table 3, p. 14–15); memory-saving mode at 20 MB vs SVMlight with a 20 MB cache (Table 4, p. 16), where by my reading SVM-QP loses on several Letter and OCR instances, which the text does not discuss; IPM warm start with k = 50, "not as dramatic as one might hope" and helpful mainly when I_c is large (Table 5, p. 17–18); incremental mode, where lost look-ahead gives "a dramatic increase of CPU time" (Table 6, p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: The method descends from the simplex method and its QP analogues (p. 2). SMO is recast as an active-set method with chunk size fixed at 2 (footnote 2, p. 3). Cauwenberghs–Poggio 2001 and Hastie et al. 2004 are distinguished because they keep primal and dual feasibility and change the problem itself (p. 4). The parametric method of Hastie et al. is "usually slower" and cannot use sprint or shrinking: "It remains to be seen whether a good implementation of the algorithm in Hastie et al. (2004) can match the performance of our algorithm." (p. 17). Kaufman 1998 is credited with some of the efficiency ideas (p. 4). Sprint is credited to J. Forrest and the singular-case treatment to A. Belloni (p. 20).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract opens by conceding that the underlying method "is not new" and puts the novelty in the large-scale application (p. 1). It fences the scope: generalization is identical to SVMlight because the formulation is the same, "and we do not discuss them" (p. 1). An intuition example (ten identical points) justifies a design choice (p. 3–4). A normative remark questions whether some problems "should ever be solved" (p. 10). The conclusion is two sentences and one future direction (p. 19). There are no theorems; the paper is implementation derivations plus tables.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution levels: software (SVM-QP, open source, p. 10), algorithmic adaptation, and a computational study with ablations. Stated limits: memory for the Cholesky factor of Q_ss when n_s is large (p. 4, p. 10, p. 13); extremely large problems may need an iterative linear solver (p. 13); the IPM used for warm starts is "rather crude" (p. 17); only selected C values, not the whole path (p. 17); the incremental mode loses look-ahead (p. 19). Future work: "a more comprehensive theoretical analysis of the behavior and complexity of the method for SVM problems" (p. 19); warm starts over kernel parameters (p. 17); a C++ version (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence, stated and practised (ML side). "The method itself is not new" (p. 3): the classical active-set QP method is kept and only the parts that SVM structure makes expensive (factorization updates, pricing) are re-engineered (p. 3, p. 9–14).

### S016 · Geometry of interpolation sets in derivative free optimization (Mathematical Programming 2008, DOI 10.1007/s10107-006-0073-5)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, L. N. Vicente (Coimbra; most of the paper was written while he visited IBM, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: IBM period (p. 1). It replaces the 1997 Newton-polynomial geometry theory of S008 with Λ-poisedness and the condition number of a scaled Vandermonde matrix, the vocabulary used in the group's later geometry work (cards S030, S088, S104). It announces a companion paper on regression and underdetermined interpolation (p. 2). The file is a 26-page preprint with no journal header. **Read**: full text, including the appendix on the cubic case (p. 24–25). References skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Convergence of DFO sampling methods needs Taylor-like error bounds whose geometry constant Λ_Y stays uniformly bounded over the sets the algorithm uses (p. 2). The existing tools each fall short:
  - Powell's Lagrange-based maintenance "does not provide any bounds on the resulting polynomials" (p. 3, p. 5);
  - the authors' own 1997 Newton-polynomial method (S008) needs "an extra step, where all interpolation points have to be replaced", which is "very expensive" (p. 19), and replacing Y completely is called very unattractive algorithmically (p. 6);
  - Ciarlet–Raviart's split of the constant into a "stretchedness" factor is not algorithmic and can mislead, since six points near a circle are nearly non-poised with small stretchedness (p. 6);
  - the condition number of M(φ, Y) is usually dismissed because it depends on the basis (p. 4). The paper revives it: for the natural basis on the scaled set, it is a good measure (p. 4, p. 11).

  The entry is therefore partly self-criticism of the 1997 framework.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Λ-poisedness: Y is Λ-poised in B(1) if for every x in the ball, φ(x) = Σλ_i(x)φ(yⁱ) with ‖λ(x)‖ ≤ Λ (Definition 3.2, p. 7), where the λ_i are exactly the Lagrange polynomials (Lemma 3.1, p. 6). Geometrically, 1/Λ bounds "the distance to linear dependency" of the vectors φ(yⁱ) (p. 7). This measure is tied to ‖M̂⁻¹‖ and κ(M̂) for the natural monomial basis on Y scaled to the unit ball (Theorem 3.3, p. 8), and the error bounds are derived in those terms (§4, p. 11–18). Two maintenance algorithms (§5, p. 18–23), LU (Gaussian elimination by rows) and QR (Gram–Schmidt), work point by point with a pivot threshold ξ ∈ (0, 1/4). If no existing point gives a pivot ≥ ξ, the point is replaced by the maximizer of the pivot polynomial over the ball, so only the points that "spoil" poisedness are replaced (p. 19–21). Kept: interpolation models and Powell's replace-by-maximization idea (p. 5, p. 18–21). Changed: the quality measure becomes a factorization pivot with a provable bound, and full-set replacement is no longer needed (p. 19). The stated cost: the polynomials may have to be recomputed at each update, though the set does not (p. 19).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Linear case: f ∈ C¹ with ∇f Lipschitz (γ_L) on an open set containing B(Δ) (p. 12). Quadratic case: ∇²f Lipschitz (γ_Q) (p. 14). Cubic case: third derivatives Lipschitz (p. 25). Without loss of generality the smallest enclosing ball is centred at the origin (p. 6), and y⁰ = 0 is an interpolation point, "very natural in a DFO setting" because the centre is the current iterate; shifting otherwise doubles the ball (p. 11). Only complete, determined interpolation (n+1 or (n+1)(n+2)/2 points) is covered; regression and underdetermined models are deferred (p. 2). No noise.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Basis independence: Lagrange polynomials are the coefficients of φ(x) in the vectors φ(yⁱ), so they do not depend on the basis (Lemma 3.1 and corollary, p. 6).
  - Theorem 3.3 (p. 8–9): ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ Λ implies √p₁Λ-poised (direct). Conversely, Λ-poised implies M nonsingular, because a polynomial vanishing on a ball is zero, and ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ θΛ. The proof uses norm equivalence (ψ(v) = max over B(1) of |vᵀφ(x)| is a norm, so ψ(v) ≥ σ‖v‖) and orthogonality of singular vectors (p. 9).
  - Lemma 3.4 (p. 9–10): any quadratic whose coefficient vector has ∞-norm 1 reaches at least 1/4 in absolute value on B(1). The proof restricts to the one or two variables of the largest coefficient and enumerates cases. This gives θ ≤ 1 for d = 1 and θ ≤ 4√p₁ for d = 2 (p. 9–10).
  - Scaling: Lagrange polynomials are invariant under scaling of Y, but κ(M) blows up as Δ → 0, so Y is always scaled to B(1) before measuring (Lemma 3.5, p. 11).
  - Error bounds (Theorems 4.1, 4.2; p. 12–17): write the model errors, subtract the interpolation conditions, Taylor-expand around x, and subtract the y⁰ equation to cancel the unknown function error. What remains is a linear system L e^g(x) = O(Δ²) (linear case) or Q[t; e^H] = O(Δ³) (quadratic case) whose matrix does not depend on x. Bounding the right-hand side with Lipschitz constants and inverting through the diagonally scaled matrix (4.18) gives explicit constants: ‖e^g‖ ≤ (5√n γ_L‖L̂⁻¹‖/2)Δ in the linear case (p. 13), and α_H = 3√2/2, α_g = 3(1+√2)/2, α_f = (6+9√2)/4, β_f = 1/6 in the quadratic case (p. 16). In terms of M̂, their function-value bound has a constant linear in √p₁ where the Ciarlet–Raviart form gives one linear in p₁ (p. 17).
  - Algorithmic bound (5.1): ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ c(p₁)·ε_growth/ξ, with c = √p₁ for LU and c = p₁ for QR (Propositions 5.1–5.2, p. 19–23). Lemma 3.4 guarantees a replacement point with pivot ≥ 1/4 > ξ, and the maximization reduces to "a trivial enumeration" (p. 21). LU costs O(n⁶) flops as presented (p. 21).
  - Appendix: the cubic case follows the same pattern (Theorem 5.3, p. 24–25).
- **D5 Experiments**: One small Matlab experiment (p. 24). From 100 random points in the unit cube, interpolation sets were chosen repeatedly by Powell's Lagrange maximization, by LU and by QR, and ‖M⁻¹‖ was compared: all three gave similar values, mostly between 10 and 100 and never above 1000. The authors add two caveats: the random points may explain the result, and "without a framework of a real derivative free package, it is difficult to reproduce a more practical example" (p. 24). No optimization runs.
- **D6 Positioning**: Powell's strategy is called reasonable (p. 18), but it "seems to work well in practice, however, there is no proof" that it keeps Λ bounded (p. 19). The same criticism is applied to the authors' own 1997 method, plus its expensive full replacement (p. 19). Ciarlet–Raviart provide bounds but no algorithmic constant (p. 6). De Boor's Gaussian elimination by segments works with fixed data, while in DFO the sample sets change (p. 19). The linear case is linked to uniform positive bases from pattern search (p. 20), and the *good set, shifted and scaled* strategy to positive bases (p. 18). Novelty is claimed modestly: the goal is "not so much the novelty of the bounds" as their usefulness to optimizers and the simplicity of the derivation (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract names the contribution as "an alternative, more intuitive, definition", the link to a condition number, a range of algorithms and the bounds (p. 1). Intuition comes first: the geometric reading of Λ (p. 7) precedes the theorem (p. 8). Remarks follow theorems (Remark 3.1 on basis scaling, p. 10–11). The paper hedges on practice: it is possible that the proposed algorithms "will not perform better than Powell’s algorithm" (p. 23). An organization paragraph comes early (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contributions: theory (Λ-poisedness ↔ condition number; explicit Taylor-like bounds for linear, quadratic and cubic models) and algorithms (LU/QR maintenance with a provable bound). Stated limits: determined interpolation only (p. 2); polynomials must be recomputed at updates (p. 19); practical value untested inside a DFO package (p. 23–24); θ for d > 2 "beyond the scope" (p. 10). Stated future work: show that algorithms like Powell's "can also provide guarantees of uniform well-poisedness", "a subject of a separate study" (p. 24). The 2025–26 complexity papers on Powell-style geometry handling (cards S088 and S104 in c08/c09) take this up.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ strong evidence. The geometry constant (Λ or κ(M̂)) is exactly what certifies model quality in the Taylor-like bounds (p. 2–3, p. 13–18), and the maintenance algorithms replace only the points that spoil poisedness (p. 19).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant on *pay only the minimum*. In 2008 the stated practical position is the opposite: maintain well-poisedness throughout, "not just when it is necessary to pass the criterion needed for the convergence proof" (p. 18). The confine-to-the-end stance appears only in Scheinberg–Toint 2010 (S030). SKILL.md's Method 4 evidence should date the minimal-safeguard position from 2009–2010, not treat it as constant since 1997.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. Powell's replace-by-maximization logic is kept, and only the quality measure is swapped for one with a proof (p. 18–21).
- New-pattern candidate: *measure the safeguard with a standard linear-algebra quantity*. Replace a bespoke quality measure (bounds on polynomial maxima) with the condition number or pivots of a normalized matrix, so that factorization algorithms and their analysis can be reused (p. 3, p. 19).
- New-pattern candidate: *revive a dismissed measure under the right normalization*. The condition number of M is basis-dependent in general, but for the natural basis on a set scaled to the unit ball it is a valid poisedness measure (p. 4, p. 8, p. 11).
- New-pattern candidate: *leave certifying the practitioners' heuristic as an explicit open problem* (p. 24). It was later picked up in S104.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] Error bound for any interpolation model: subtract the interpolation equations from Taylor expansions at x, subtract the centre-point equation to cancel the unknown function error, and read off a linear system whose matrix is the scaled interpolation matrix, independent of x. All the geometry then sits in one ‖M̂⁻¹‖ (p. 12–16).
- [D4] Scale the sample set to the unit ball before measuring its conditioning, and state which quantities are scale-invariant (Lagrange polynomials) and which are not (κ(M)) (p. 11).
- [D4] Norm-equivalence trick for uniform constants: ψ(v) = max over the ball of |vᵀφ(x)| is a norm, so a uniform σ exists; for low degree, compute σ explicitly by restricting to one or two variables (Lemma 3.4, p. 9–10).
- [D2] Build a safeguard from a factorization with a pivot threshold. If no current point passes, replace it with the maximizer of the pivot polynomial; a lemma that this maximum is ≥ 1/4 > ξ makes the replacement always succeed (Algorithms 5.1–5.2, p. 20–23).
- [D5] Run a cheap sanity check of the new safeguard against the practitioners' heuristic on the same random point pools, report the range of the controlled quantity, and state why the test is weak (p. 24).
- [D7] Name a competitor's gap precisely (no proof that the strategy keeps Λ uniformly bounded) and apply the same test to your own earlier method (p. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "It is often simpler to design algorithms that operate with the condition number of a matrix rather than directly with a bound on the Lagrange polynomials." (p. 3)
- "In practice, it is more efficient to maintain well-poisedness throughout the algorithm, not just when it is necessary to pass the criterion needed for the convergence proof." (p. 18)
