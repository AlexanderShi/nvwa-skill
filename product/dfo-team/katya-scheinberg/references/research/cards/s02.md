# Paper cards · batch s02 (supplement)

Batch intent: See how Scheinberg's 2018–2022 machine-learning collaborations carry her optimization habits outside model-based DFO. The papers are SGD/Hogwild!, SARAH and shuffling-momentum analyses with Nguyen and Takáč; TRish with Curtis and Shi; direct optimization of 0-1 error and AUC with Ghanbari; and two Lehigh energy-forecasting applications. The questions are whether the oracle-accuracy lens (Method 1) and the keep-the-classical-method move (Method 2) survive in this setting, and where the work departs from the stochastic-process programme (Method 3).
Focus dimensions: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line), D8. D6 and D7 appear only in passing, through the quotes and the "Transferable techniques" lines.
Material roles: all eight papers are **supplement**: S078, S032, S036, S060, S089, S056, S037, S055. None is core, contrast or background in this batch.

Reading note: I read each paper for its abstract, introduction and contributions, the algorithm, the assumptions, the main theorem statements, one key-lemma proof, the experiment setup and the conclusion. I skimmed the appendix proofs of S032, S036, S037 and S055 (see each card). In S060 I did not read §III (forecasting background and metric definitions, pp. 3–4) closely. ### S078 · Directly and efficiently optimizing prediction error and AUC of linear classifiers (arXiv preprint 2018, arXiv:1802.02535)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: H. Ghanbari (PhD student, Lehigh) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh-era ML-optimization work. It is the first version of the idea that S089 extends.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: ML optimizes surrogate losses (logistic, hinge, pairwise) because the empirical 0-1 error and AUC are step functions "whose gradient is either zero or not defined" (abstract p. 1; §1 p. 1; §2 pp. 2–3). Pairwise AUC surrogates must also be computed for every positive–negative pair, which drives up the per-iteration cost (p. 2). The paper states the problem as the *expected* error F_error(w) = P(Y·wᵀX < 0) and the expected AUC of a linear classifier (eqs. 1, 5, pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: if the classes are Gaussian, then wᵀX is Gaussian. The expected error is then P(+)(1−φ(μ_Z+/σ_Z+)) + P(−)φ(μ_Z−/σ_Z−) (Thm 2, p. 4), and 1−AUC = 1−φ(μ_Z/σ_Z) (Thm 6, p. 5). Both have closed-form gradients that depend only on first and second moments (Thms 3, 7, pp. 4–5). The moments are replaced by empirical ones, so the data is touched once (p. 2). **Changed**: the objective (the true metric in smooth closed form instead of a surrogate). **Kept**: plain gradient descent with backtracking Armijo (Alg. 1, p. 5), used "for simplicity" (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Gaussian class-conditional distributions (eq. 8, p. 4) or joint Gaussian (eq. 11, p. 5). The authors argue that wᵀX may be nearly normal even for non-Gaussian data, via a CLT for dependent variables, and state that a theoretical justification is future work (p. 5). The objective is nonconvex, so a moment-based starting point is used (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: there is no convergence theory. The derivations use closure of Gaussians under linear maps (Tong's results restated as Thms 1, 4, 5, pp. 4–5) and the chain rule for φ(g(w)) (Lemma 2, p. 4). Proofs are in Appendix A (p. 10, skimmed).
- **D5 Experiments**: 9 synthetic Gaussian sets (d = 500–2500, n = 5000, with 0/5/10 % label-swap outliers; Table 1, p. 6), run with exact moments, empirical moments and logistic regression. Also 19 LIBSVM/UCI sets under 5-fold CV × 4 (pp. 6–7). On real data the error objective is "comparable" on 13/19 sets, better on 4 and worse on 2 (p. 7). The AUC objective against pairwise hinge is comparable on 14, better on 4 and worse on 2, and far faster (p. 8). Traces of the two losing cases are shown (Figs 1–2, pp. 7–8). LDA is compared in Appendix C (pp. 12–13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: modelling and algorithmic. The contribution is a smooth closed-form expected metric whose per-iteration cost does not depend on n (conclusion, p. 8). Stated limits: the Gaussian assumption is not justified theoretically (p. 5), and in the losing cases the method "stalled" (p. 7). Future work: second-order methods (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (variant): the structure exploited is distributional, i.e. the first and second moments. Precomputing them makes every iteration independent of the data size (p. 2, p. 8).
- Method 2 ✅ weak evidence: the classical Armijo backtracking method is kept unchanged, and the novelty is all in the objective (Alg. 1, p. 5).
- new-pattern candidate: **smooth true target instead of convex surrogate**. Optimize a smooth closed form of the metric you care about (Thms 2, 6, pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Precompute sufficient statistics (moments) once, so that the cost of each optimizer iteration depends only on the dimension d, not on the data size n (p. 2, p. 8).
- Run a two-tier synthetic test: data where the modelling assumption holds exactly, solved with *exact* and with *estimated* parameters. This separates model error from estimation error before you move to real data (Table 2, p. 6).
- Show the traces of the datasets where your method loses and name the likely cause, such as an inaccurate gradient or a local minimum (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "Another key advantage of the proposed method over the classical empirical risk minimization is that the training data is only used once at the beginning of the algorithm to compute the approximate moments." (p. 2)
- "Improving our method for such cases is subject of future work." (p. 7)

### S032 · New convergence aspects of stochastic gradient algorithms (Journal of Machine Learning Research 2019, arXiv:1811.12403)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: L. M. Nguyen, P. H. Nguyen, P. Richtárik, M. Takáč, M. van Dijk · **Position in trajectory**: extends the ICML 2018 paper "SGD and Hogwild! convergence without the bounded gradients assumption" (p. 4). It belongs to the Nguyen–Takáč SGD line, running in parallel to the adaptive-method programme.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: classical SGD analysis assumes E‖∇f(w;ξ)‖² ≤ σ². Combined with strong convexity this gives an upper bound on F that contradicts quadratic growth (derivation, p. 3). A two-function example shows E‖∇f(w_t;ξ)‖² can be arbitrarily large (p. 3). Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal's bound E‖g‖² ≤ M‖∇F‖² + N avoids the contradiction, but M and N are unknown and M sets the learning rate (p. 3). The paper enters through an observation: for expected risk, *each realization* f(·;ξ) is smooth and often convex (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: keep SGD and Hogwild!, and **derive** the second-moment bound instead of assuming it: E‖∇f(w;ξ)‖² ≤ 4L[F(w)−F*] + N with N = 2E‖∇f(w*;ξ)‖² (Lemma 1, p. 6), or 4Lκ[…] without convexity (Lemma 2, p. 7). This gives an explicit M and a larger initial step, 1/(2L) or 1/(2Lκ) against 1/(2L_F κ²) (Remark 1, p. 8). A general recursion with random 0/1 "filter" matrices covers Hogwild! and single-coordinate updates in one proof (eqs. 10–13, pp. 9–10; Alg. 2, p. 10). The admissible step class is widened to diminishing η_t with Ση_t = ∞, without requiring Ση_t² < ∞ (§4, pp. 14–16).
- **D3 Assumptions**: F μ-strongly convex (Assumption 1, p. 2). f(·;ξ) L-smooth for every ξ (Assumption 2, p. 6), and optionally convex for every ξ (Assumption 3, p. 6). The ξ_t are i.i.d. (p. 6). Hogwild! additionally needs consistency with delay τ and atomic per-coordinate reads and writes (Assumption 4, p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: the key lemma combines ‖a‖² ≤ 2‖a−b‖² + 2‖b‖² with the Johnson–Zhang-type bound E‖∇f(w;ξ)−∇f(w*;ξ)‖² ≤ 2L[F(w)−F*] (Lemma 4, p. 21; proof B.1, pp. 21–22). Almost-sure convergence follows from Bertsekas' supermartingale convergence lemma (Lemma 5, p. 21; Thm 1, p. 7). Expected rates are O(1/t) (Thm 2, p. 7), and for Hogwild! the τ-dependent terms are of higher order (Thm 6, p. 11). For general diminishing steps, an ODE-style comparison function C(t) = e^{−M(t)}∫e^{M(x)}n(x)²dx crosses n(t) exactly once (Lemma 3, pp. 15–16), and 1/(K+t) is the fastest of the family 1/(K+t)^q (Thm 11, p. 16). Appendix proofs other than B.1 were skimmed.
- **D5 Experiments**: ℓ2-logistic regression on ijcnn1 and covtype. The runs are single-core simulations of Alg. 2, since the claims concern iteration and update counts (p. 17). Settings: 10 runs, step from Thm 6, support fractions v ∈ {1,…,1/4}, τ ∈ {1, 10, 100}, plotted against coordinate updates and epochs (pp. 17–18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: theoretical. The paper gives SGD and Hogwild! analyses free of bounded gradients, almost-sure convergence, and a larger step class (§1.1, pp. 4–5; conclusion, p. 18). The conclusion states no limitations (p. 18). Strong convexity of F is required throughout (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the second-moment "oracle" bound is *derived* from per-sample structure rather than imposed or controlled by sampling (Lemma 1, p. 6; Remark 1, p. 8).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: SGD and Hogwild! are kept and the novelty moves into the assumptions. The method stays non-adaptive with prescribed schedules, which is the pattern named in Research Taste warning sign 1.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the martingale tool (supermartingale convergence) gives almost-sure convergence under prescribed step sequences. There is no stopping-time analysis of an adaptive step (Thm 1, p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate: **counterexample-first assumption audit**. Open by showing the standard assumption is inconsistent with the problem class (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Test a standard assumption against the rest of your assumptions: combine the bounded-gradient bound with strong convexity and derive a contradiction for large ‖w−w*‖ (p. 3).
- Replace "bounded stochastic gradients" by E‖g‖² ≤ A[F−F*] + B, derived from per-sample smoothness in three lines (proof B.1, pp. 21–22).
- Write one recursion with random coordinate filters (d_ξ E[S_u^ξ|ξ] = D_ξ) so that several algorithms are covered by a single proof (pp. 9–10).
- When the claim is about iteration or update counts, simulate an asynchronous method on one core instead of timing parallel hardware (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "However, this assumption is clearly false if F is strongly convex." (p. 3)
- "By introducing Assumption 2, which holds for many ML problems, we are able to provide the values of M and N." (p. 8)

### S036 · A stochastic trust region algorithm based on careful step normalization (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2019, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2018.0010)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: F. E. Curtis, R. Shi · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh, contemporaneous with STORM (2018). It is a first-order, trust-region-flavoured stochastic method that deliberately avoids random models.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: stochastic gradients have "no natural scaling", so SG theory needs problem-dependent stepsizes of order 1/L, unlike Newton's unit step (p. 2). Always-normalized methods ([12]) need strong assumptions (p. 3). Stochastic trust-region methods ([16], [5]) use random fully linear models with a ratio test (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: TRish takes the trust-region step −α_k g_k/‖g_k‖ (the solution of the linear TR subproblem, eq. 2.3, p. 4) only when ‖g_k‖ ∈ [1/γ₁, 1/γ₂]. Otherwise it takes the SG steps γ₁α_k g_k or γ₂α_k g_k (Algorithm TRish, p. 4), so the step norm is continuous in ‖g_k‖ (Fig. 1, p. 5). Every step is accepted, with no ratio test (p. 3). The design comes from Example 2.1: an unbiased g_k whose normalized direction is an ascent direction with probability 2/3 (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f C¹, bounded below, with an L-smooth upper bound (Assumption 3.1, pp. 5–6). g_k unbiased with E‖g_k‖² ≤ M₁ + M₂‖∇f‖² (Assumption 3.2, p. 6). The PL condition optionally (Assumption 3.3, p. 7). For a sublinear rate with constant γ's, E‖g_k‖² ≤ M₃α_k² + M₄‖∇f‖² (Assumption 3.4, p. 12), which holds with batch |S_k| = α_k⁻² (p. 12). For linear convergence, geometrically decreasing variance (Assumption 3.5, p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Lemma 3.1 (pp. 6–7) splits into the three regimes and the event E_k = {∇fᵀg_k ≥ 0}. All the harm done by normalization is collected into one term, (γ₁−γ₂)α_k P[E_k]E[∇fᵀg_k|E_k]. Lemma 3.2 bounds that term by h₁ + h₂‖∇f‖² (p. 8), with a proof suggested by an anonymous referee (acknowledgment, p. 24). The rest is SG-style recursions: linear convergence to a neighbourhood under PL (Thm 3.1, p. 9); O(1/k) when γ₁−γ₂ ∝ α_k (Thm 3.2, p. 11); average-gradient bounds without PL (Thms 3.5–3.6, p. 16). Proofs of Thms 3.2–3.6 were skimmed.
- **D5 Experiments**: logistic regression on a1a, w1a and rcv1 (1 epoch, and 2 for rcv1; batch 64), and CNNs on mnist and cifar-10 (batch 128). Tuning protocol: G is taken from an independent SG run; TRish gets 60 settings, and SG gets 60 stepsizes spanning the same effective range. The best setting is chosen by test accuracy over the last ten iterations; 10 runs (pp. 18–22). The paper reports the percentage of iterations in each case (pp. 19–23). The authors note the gains on convex problems are "somewhat limited" (pp. 20–21).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an algorithm plus convergence guarantees similar to those of SG (conclusion, p. 23). Stated limits: it only reduces the dependence on problem-dependent stepsizes (p. 2); parameter choice needs a variance estimate M₁ (p. 18); a larger study with stepsize decay is needed (p. 23). Future work: Hessian approximations, acceleration, momentum (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the trust-region step is kept and is safeguarded only where Example 2.1 breaks the descent-in-expectation argument (pp. 4–5).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: Assumption 3.4 ties the gradient second moment to α_k², so accuracy scales with the step, implemented by |S_k| = α_k⁻² (p. 12).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: plain expectation recursions, with no stochastic-process or stopping-time machinery. The paper explicitly separates itself from random-model trust region: "accepts the computed step in every iteration" and needs no fully linear models (p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: **counterexample-first assumption audit**. Example 2.1 motivates the safeguard (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a two-point distribution for which the naive design (always normalize) goes uphill in expectation, and use it to justify each safeguard (Example 2.1, p. 4).
- In the proof, collect everything a non-standard step costs into one term through a case split on the sign of ∇fᵀg. Then tie that term's parameter (γ₁−γ₂) to the stepsize to recover SG rates (Lemma 3.1, pp. 6–7; Thm 3.2, p. 11).
- Tune fairly: give the baseline the same number of settings over the same range of effective stepsizes (pp. 18–19).
- Report how often each algorithmic branch fired in the runs (pp. 19–23).

**Quotes**:
- "We argue in this paper that employing normalized steps in all iterations cannot lead to general convergence guarantees, which perhaps explains the additional assumptions required for convergence by [12]." (p. 3)
- "We attribute this better behavior to the algorithm’s use of normalized steps, which one can argue lessens its dependence on problem-specific quantities." (p. 23)

### S060 · A novel smoothed loss and penalty function for noncrossing composite quantile estimation via deep neural networks (arXiv preprint 2019, arXiv:1909.12122)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: K. Hatalis (ECE PhD student, Lehigh), A. J. Lamadrid, S. Kishore · **Position in trajectory**: a Lehigh application collaboration in energy forecasting. Scheinberg is third author, and the optimization content is a smoothing plus penalty design.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: application-driven, probabilistic wind-power forecasting (abstract, p. 1; §I, p. 1). Estimating multiple quantiles one at a time causes "quantile crossover" (p. 3). Reordering heuristics lack a foundation, and constrained SVQR is expensive (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: SPNN is a feedforward net with M quantile outputs, trained on the logistic smoothing of the pinball loss, S_{τ,α}(u) = τu + α log(1+e^{−u/α}) (eq. 3, p. 5, taken from Zheng [54]). The loss is summed over quantiles, with L2 weight penalties (eq. 5, p. 5), and trained by backprop with Adam (p. 6). Non-crossing is enforced by a squared-hinge penalty c·Σ max(0, ε − (q^{τ_m} − q^{τ_{m−1}}))² (eq. 6, p. 6) instead of monotone-network constraints (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions**: no theoretical assumptions. Hyperparameters were found "through empirical testing on training data": smoothing 0.01, crossing penalty 1000, margin 0 (p. 7). The hidden activation is given as tanh in §IV-B (p. 5) but the case studies use ReLU (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none. Gradients are derived by the chain rule (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: GEFCom2014 wind data, 10 zones, with 2013 as the test year and monthly retraining on a 12-month sliding window (p. 7). Inputs are raw NWP wind components plus 4 time features (p. 7). Baselines: persistence, climatology, uniform, L2 QR, SVQR (p. 7). Metrics: QS, IS, ACE, sharpness, reliability diagrams, QVSS (pp. 7–9). Compared against the competition winners: SPNN2 mean QS 0.042 vs the winner's 0.038 (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: application and modelling (contribution list, pp. 1–2). Future work: solar, wave, prices, load, and very-short-term forecasting from past power alone (p. 10). In §V (pp. 6–9) I found no count of crossing violations with and without the penalty.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ⚠ weak variant: the structured nonsmooth loss is smoothed so that standard gradient methods apply (eq. 3, p. 5), rather than being replaced by a different objective. This is not structure-exploiting DFO.
- new-pattern candidate: **smooth true target instead of convex surrogate**, shared with S078 and S089 (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Enforce an ordering between several outputs with a squared-hinge penalty and a margin ε, added to the loss (eq. 6, p. 6).
- On a public competition dataset, place your score against the leaderboard, and say what inputs the winners used that you did not (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "A possible way to prevent this issue is to utilize simple heuristics of reordering estimated quantiles." (p. 3)
- "We note again that the top teams used a wide range of engineered features while we used raw wind speed data along with time as input to our model." (p. 9)

### S089 · Novel and efficient approximations for zero-one loss of linear classifiers (arXiv preprint 2019, arXiv:1903.00359)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: H. Ghanbari, M. Li · **Position in trajectory**: the 2019 extension of S078. The Gaussian assumption moves from the data to the classifier output, and the optimizer changes to L-BFGS.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: same entry as S078 (abstract, p. 1), stated more sharply. The true goal is the expected error or expected ranking loss (p. 1), and pairwise surrogates cost O(n⁺n⁻), or O(n log n) for pairwise hinge (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: put the normality assumption on the scalar projection wᵀX, and on wᵀ(X⁻−X⁺), instead of on X (Thm 3.3, p. 5; Thm 3.7, p. 7). This is justified by the CLT for sums of weakly dependent features (p. 5). The result is closed-form F_n01 and F_nrank with their gradients (Thms 3.5, 3.8, pp. 6–7). Scale invariance is handled by the penalty λ(1−‖w‖²)² instead of a constraint ‖w‖ = 1 (p. 7). The optimizer is L-BFGS with Wolfe line search (p. 7, p. 10). Hessians are available, but Hessian-vector methods did not beat L-BFGS on time (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions**: wᵀX is approximately normal for the w met during training (p. 2, p. 5). The cross-covariances Σ⁺⁻ are set to zero (method step 1, pp. 8–9). The objective is undefined at w = 0 (p. 7) and nonconvex (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no convergence theory. The derivations follow S078 (Lemma 3.1 proof, p. 4; Lemma 3.4, pp. 5–6). The conclusion calls this "theoretical motivation" (p. 15).
- **D5 Experiments**: histograms of wᵀX⁺, wᵀX⁻ and wᵀ(X⁻−X⁺) at early and late iterates for a9a (Fig. 4.1, p. 8) and four more sets where normality is weaker (Fig. 4.2, pp. 8–9). 9 synthetic sets (Tables 4.2–4.3, pp. 11–12) and 21 LIBSVM/UCI sets up to covtype, rcv1 and real-sim (Table 4.4, p. 12). The tables report accuracy/AUC, iterations, solve time and moment time (Tables 4.5–4.6, pp. 13–14). Pairwise hinge did not solve real-sim within 24 h (p. 13). LDA and ADAM are further baselines (Table 4.7, p. 15). Each objective is plotted against the empirical 0-1 or ranking loss along the path from start to solution (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: new approximations plus a method whose cost per evaluation does not depend on n (conclusion, p. 15). The conclusion states no limits (p. 15). From the tables: covtype accuracy is 0.5502 against 0.7562 for logistic regression (Table 4.5, p. 13), which the text I read does not discuss. The text calls LDA "clearly inferior" (p. 14), but Table 4.7 has LDA at or above F_n01 on several sets, e.g. colon 0.8875 vs 0.7833 and diabetes 0.7703 vs 0.7667 (p. 15). Tables 4.2–4.3 match S078's synthetic tables digit for digit, run times included (S078 p. 6, p. 8, p. 11), yet S089 says L-BFGS was used (p. 10) where S078 used gradient descent (S078 p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: distributional structure (moments) is exploited, and moment time is reported separately from solve time (Table 4.5, p. 13).
- Method 2 ✅ weak: a standard quasi-Newton method is used unchanged (p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: **assumption on the statistic the objective uses, checked along the path**. The assumption is weakened to the 1-D projection the objective depends on and then checked empirically at the iterates (p. 5; Figs 4.1–4.2, pp. 8–9).
- new-pattern candidate: **smooth true target instead of convex surrogate** (with S078 and S060).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Place the distributional assumption on the low-dimensional quantity the objective actually uses (wᵀX), justify it with a CLT argument, and plot its histogram at early and late iterates (p. 5; Figs 4.1–4.2, pp. 8–9).
- Plot your surrogate and the baseline surrogate against the *true* objective along the segment from the starting point to the solution (p. 14).
- Report one-time preprocessing (moment computation) in its own column, next to per-run solve time (Table 4.5, p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "We will argue that this assumption is a lot milder than the assumption that the data itself is nearly Gaussian." (p. 2)
- "We see that Fn01 overall provides a better approximation of the empirical error than logistic regression." (p. 14)

### S056 · Feature engineering and forecasting via derivative-free optimization and ensemble of sequence-to-sequence networks with applications in renewable energy (Energy 196, 117136, 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.energy.2020.117136)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Pirhooshyaran (PhD student, Lehigh), L. V. Snyder · **Position in trajectory**: an application of DFO-TR (Ghanbari–Scheinberg 2017, ref. [25]; IDFO book, ref. [5]) to tune and design ML pipelines. Scheinberg's affiliation is Cornell (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: application-driven, multistep forecasting of wind, solar and wave power. The authors argue that existing ML models (SVR, simple NNs) do not exploit temporal structure or seasonality (p. 1). Seasonality is established by decomposition, an ADF test and ACF/PACF of residuals (§3.1, p. 3). DFO is positioned as a tuner for expensive black boxes (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: an ensemble of seq2seq LSTM networks, of size EF (ensemble factor) (§3.2, p. 3), with "additive resampling" (the whole training set plus one subsection per network) and bagging (pp. 3–4). SDFO-TR is DFO-TR with three changes (p. 8): (1) box constraints handled by an objective upper bound plus a linear penalty on violation distance; (2) variables scaled to [0,1]; (3) integer variables encoded as piecewise-constant sub-intervals of [0,1]. SDFO-TR chooses the hyperparameters and EF, and, with 0/1 thresholds, the input features (§4.3.2, p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions**: the loss is bounded above; MAAPE ≤ π/2 is used as the DFO loss (p. 8). No smoothness is claimed for the piecewise-constant objective. A heuristic argument, citing [25, 33], says that DFO "may escape flat local minima" (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none. Alg. 1 (p. 6) uses a quadratic interpolation model and a ratio test with η₀ < η₁. The radius shrinks only if |X| > P+1, and the farthest point is replaced to keep the set poised (p. 8). As printed, Alg. 1 has a `return` inside the loop (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: NREL wind speed and direction (first 10 days of 2010, 5-min data), GECAD wind power, NSRDB solar GHI and NOAA buoy wave power. Split 60/20/20; horizons of 1–72 steps. Baselines: ELM, ε-SVR, ν-SVR, RF, SARIMA, FOS-ELM and Spearmint BO. Metrics: MAAPE, NRMSE, MAE (pp. 9–19). Feature selection is compared with Spearmint tuned separately on each metric (Table 7, p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: a framework plus applications (contributions, p. 2; conclusion, pp. 19–20). Acknowledged limits: many features may affect DFO performance (p. 14), and the data was cut to the first ten days "due to computational limitations" (p. 15). Future work: DFO "as a decision maker" for designing parallel structures in other ML areas (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the existing DFO-TR is reused and changed only where box and integer variables break it (p. 8).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: geometry is maintained by farthest-point replacement to "keep it poised" (p. 8), but it is applied to a piecewise-constant objective outside the smooth theory.
- Research Taste mark 6 ✅: DFO is used as an ML hyperparameter and architecture tool (p. 2, p. 20).
- new-pattern candidate: **DFO-TR as ML-pipeline design tool (integer encoding)** (p. 8, p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode an integer hyperparameter with t values as t equal sub-intervals of [0,1], plus two linear-penalty sections outside, so a continuous model-based DFO solver can search it (p. 8).
- With a loss bounded above (MAAPE ≤ π/2), return "upper bound + b·distance to box" for infeasible points. The solver then learns to stay inside the box without explicit constraints (p. 8).
- Tune the competing tuner (Spearmint) separately on each reported metric, to show where optimizing one metric degrades the others (Table 7, p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "Theoretically, scaling is not required because the trust region radius would handle different magnitudes for different parameters." (p. 8)
- "This is why we introduced the complexity into the function at the first place and let the DFO decides." (p. 9)

### S037 · Inexact SARAH algorithm for stochastic optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2021, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2020.1818081)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: L. M. Nguyen, M. Takáč · **Position in trajectory**: a follow-up to SARAH (ICML 2017) that carries a finite-sum variance-reduction method to expectation minimization.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: SVRG and SARAH need an exact gradient in every outer iteration, so they apply only to finite sums, not to E[f(w;ξ)] (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). SCSG does handle the expectation, but with worse κ-dependence (pp. 2–3). The paper also notes that the original SARAH non-strongly convex result relied on an assumption "not explicitly stated" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: iSARAH replaces v₀ = ∇F(w₀) with a b-sample mini-batch mean (eq. 3, p. 4) and keeps SARAH's recursive estimator v_t = ∇f(w_t;ξ_t) − ∇f(w_{t−1};ξ_t) + v_{t−1} (p. 5). Both one-loop and multi-loop versions are analysed (Algs 1–2, p. 5). The analysis chooses b so that SARAH's rate is recovered (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f(·;ξ) L-smooth (Assumption 1, p. 6). F μ-strongly convex (Assumption 2) or convex realizations (Assumption 3), as each result needs. E‖∇f(w*;ξ)‖² ≤ σ*² (Assumption 4, p. 7). The multi-loop non-strongly convex case needs F(w̃_k)−F* ≤ M‖∇F(w̃_k)‖² + N at the *outer* iterates only (Assumption 5, p. 17). The paper argues this is weaker than PL and bounded level sets, and illustrates it with a modified logistic function (p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Lemma 7 (p. 10): since E[v_j − v_{j−1}|F_j] = ∇F(w_j) − ∇F(w_{j−1}), the cross terms vanish, and E‖∇F(w_t) − v_t‖² telescopes into E‖∇F(w₀)−v₀‖² + ΣE‖v_j−v_{j−1}‖² − ΣE‖∇F(w_j)−∇F(w_{j−1})‖². The only term SARAH does not have is the initial mini-batch error, which b controls. Multi-loop contraction: E‖∇F(w̃_s)‖² − Δ ≤ α^s(·) (Thm 3, p. 16). With η = 2/(5L), m = 20κ−1 and b = max{20κ−10, 20σ*²/ε}, the complexity is O(max{σ*²/ε, κ} log(1/ε)) (Cor. 4, p. 17). Other appendix-level lemma proofs were skimmed.
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The paper has no numerical section; a search for "numerical" and "experiment" finds nothing.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: theoretical, the total sample complexity of SARAH without full gradients, compared in Tables 1–2 (p. 4; conclusion, p. 19). Stated limits: one-loop iSARAH needs a learning rate of order ε, "practically undesirable" (p. 3); the best non-strongly convex rate holds only under Assumption 5 (p. 3, p. 19). The bounded-variance nonconvex rate is deferred to ProxSARAH (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the sample size b is the accuracy knob, set exactly so that the exact-gradient rate is recovered (p. 4; Cor. 4, p. 17).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the SARAH recursion is unchanged and only the anchor gradient is replaced (pp. 3–5).
- Research Taste mark 2 ✅: parity with the exact-gradient SARAH rate.
- Research Taste mark 4 ✗: a guarantee without numbers, since there are no experiments.
- new-pattern candidate: **complexity table with an explicit additional-assumption column** (Tables 1–2, p. 4).
- new-pattern candidate: **counterexample-first / hidden-assumption audit** of prior work (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a recursive estimator's exact anchor is replaced by a sampled one, prove a telescoping identity for the estimator error. The only new term is then the initial sampling error, and you choose the batch size to make it an ε-fraction (Lemma 7, p. 10; Cor. 4, p. 17).
- Replace "iterates stay bounded" or PL by F − F* ≤ M‖∇F‖² + N, imposed only at outer iterates. Give a concrete function that satisfies it but not PL (pp. 17–18).
- Complexity comparison table with the columns Bound / Problem / Additional assumption, plus footnoted criteria (Tables 1–2, p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "The key idea of the analysis of iSARAH is to establish bounds on b which ensure sufficient accuracy for recovering original SARAH convergence rate." (p. 4)
- "In other words, Assumption 5 is a relaxation of the boundedness assumption and the PL condition." (p. 18)

### S055 · Nesterov accelerated shuffling gradient method for convex optimization (International Conference on Machine Learning 2022, PMLR 162: 21703–21732, arXiv:2202.03525)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: T. H. Tran (PhD student, Cornell), L. M. Nguyen · **Position in trajectory**: Cornell period, continuing the shuffling-SGD line with Nguyen.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: stochastic NAG has no provable acceleration without strong growth or vanishing variance (p. 2). The best convex rate for shuffling SGD is O(1/T^{2/3}) in epochs (pp. 1–3). Per-iteration momentum accumulates gradient errors, citing Devolder et al. and Liu & Belkin (p. 3). The paper states its central question explicitly (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: NASG (Alg. 2, p. 4) runs n incremental gradient steps along any permutation, then applies Nesterov extrapolation ỹ_t = x̃_t + (t−1)/(t+2)(x̃_t − x̃_{t−1}) **once per epoch** (p. 4). It is presented as NAG with the epoch-start full gradient replaced by sequential component gradients (p. 4). A motivating experiment shows the per-iteration variant (NASG-PI) accumulating errors (Fig. 1, p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: components L-smooth (Assumption 3.1, p. 4). Then either convex components, with σ*² = (1/n)Σ‖∇f(x*;i)‖² (Assumption 3.2, p. 5), or convex F with generalized bounded variance Θ‖∇F‖² + σ² (Assumption 3.3, p. 5). No bounded domain or bounded gradient is needed (abstract, p. 1). The step η_t = kα^t/(LT) with α = 1 + 1/T depends on the horizon T (Thm 4.1, p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Nesterov-style auxiliary sequences θ^(t) = 2/(t+2) and v^(t), with x̃_t and ỹ_t written as convex combinations (eqs. 7–8, p. 5), combined with the convexity inequality (p. 5). The key estimate is Lemma A.1, with T(T+2) weights (p. 14). Rate: F(x̃_T)−F* ≤ 4σ*²/(9LT) + c·L‖x̃₀−x*‖²/T for **any** permutation, deterministically, for the last iterate (Thm 4.1, p. 5; Remark 4.4, p. 6). Randomized schemes cut the variance term by a factor n (Thm 4.5, p. 6). Initial-neighbourhood refinements are in Remarks 4.7–4.8 (p. 6). Appendix proofs B–E were skimmed.
- **D5 Experiments**: logistic regression on w8a, ijcnn1 and covtype; linear softmax and a one-hidden-layer network without activation on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10. Baselines: NAG, NASG-PI, SGD, SGD-M and Adam, all with random reshuffling. Constant learning rates tuned on a grid over 20 epochs; 10 seeds with confidence intervals; plotted against effective data passes (pp. 7–9; App. F, p. 29). The code is released (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an algorithm with O(1/T) for shuffling methods in the convex case (conclusion, p. 9). Future work: better dependence on n and a nonconvex analysis (p. 9). The rate is gradient-descent order, not NAG's O(1/T²) (p. 2 vs Thm 4.1, p. 5). So the acceleration is relative to shuffling SGD, not to deterministic NAG.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: NAG's extrapolation and the shuffling inner loop are both kept; the only change is *where* momentum is applied (pp. 3–4).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the error-sensitive step (momentum) is applied only at the level where the accumulated gradient information is closest to the full gradient (pp. 3–4). There is no explicit accuracy contract.
- Research Taste mark 2 ⚠: parity is with GD's O(1/T), not with NAG's O(1/T²).
- Method 5 ✅ weak: comparisons at equal cost, in effective data passes (p. 4, p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate: **complexity table with assumption footnotes** (Table 1, p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: **error-sensitive step only where the estimate is accurate**.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before the theory, show a small experiment in which the naive placement (momentum every iteration) fails and the proposed placement (every epoch) works (Fig. 1, p. 4).
- Rewrite the deterministic method so that your stochastic method becomes a perturbation of it (the full gradient as n sequential component steps, p. 4). The proof can then reuse the estimate-sequence algebra (eqs. 7–8, p. 5).
- State the guarantee type precisely: deterministic for any permutation, for the last iterate rather than an average (Remark 4.4, p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Can we use Nesterov’s momentum technique for Shuffling SGD to improve the convergence rate using only standard assumptions (e.g. without assuming vanishing variance)?" (p. 2)
- "Our convergence bounds in Theorem 4.1 and 4.3 hold in a deterministic sense." (p. 6)
