# Paper cards · batch s01 (supplement)

Batch intent: s01 collects eight supplement papers (2012–2018) that sit beside the main DFO / stochastic-oracle line: sparse-recovery models for DFO (S033, S053), second-order methods for ℓ1-regularized ML (S100, S054), DFO-TR as a black-box optimizer for ML (S052), an explanatory SGD analysis (S063) and two wind-power forecasting applications (S051, S082). ### S033 · Computation of sparse low degree interpolating polynomials and their application to derivative-free optimization (Mathematical Programming 2012, DOI 10.1007/s10107-012-0578-z; arXiv 1306.5729)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. S. Bandeira, L. N. Vicente · **Position in trajectory**: The first paper in this batch whose model is accurate only with high probability. It defers the matching convergence theory to future research (p. 6, p. 22) and points to [15] for relevant results; BSV 2014 (same three authors) is the follow-up in the skill. **Read**: partial — §1–§3, §4 statements, the construction of the basis ψ (pp. 13–15), the proof of Theorem 4.5 (pp. 20–21), §5 and §6. The proof of Lemma 4.1 (pp. 18–19) was not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Interpolation trust-region DFO needs (n+1)(n+2)/2 points for a determined quadratic model; underdetermined minimum-Frobenius models are only provably fully linear, not better (p. 2, pp. 7–8). Certifying a fully quadratic model needs a well-poised set of (n+1)(n+2)/2 points and an O(n⁶) factorization (p. 5). Hessian sparsity could help, but existing DFO approaches need the sparsity pattern in advance (p. 1, p. 2, p. 8). Entry: reverse the usual optimization → compressed sensing direction and use sparse-recovery theory inside DFO (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Choose the underdetermined quadratic model by minimizing the ℓ1-norm of the Hessian coefficients subject to interpolation, problem (4), a linear program (pp. 7–8). What changes relative to the minimum-Frobenius model: only the criterion that fills the free degrees of freedom; the trust-region loop is unchanged (§5, pp. 21–24). Two tracks: (i) theory with random sample points in B∞(x0; Δ) and an orthonormal basis (§4); (ii) a practical trust-region Algorithm 5.1 with deterministic sample sets that reuse past points (pp. 23–24, p. 21). The practical code keeps sample-set quality in its simplest form — enough points near the iterate, far points dropped when Δ is small, and no radius decrease while fewer than n+1 points are available (pp. 22–23).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f twice differentiable with Lipschitz Hessian (Assumption 2.2, p. 4). Approximate Hessian sparsity: for every x0 and Δ there is a fully quadratic model whose quadratic part is h-sparse in the basis ψ (Assumption 4.2, p. 17). Sample points drawn uniformly in the ℓ∞-ball (Corollary 4.1, p. 16). The ℓ∞-ball is chosen because the orthonormal-basis construction does not extend to the ℓ2-ball, which the authors state (Remark 4.1, p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Proof chain: build an orthonormal polynomial basis on the hypercube with a dimension-free bound K = 3 (Definition 4.2, Theorem 4.4, pp. 13–15) → RIP for random samples (Theorem 4.1, p. 11) → noisy partially sparse recovery (Theorem 4.3, pp. 12–13; proof delegated to S053) → treat f minus the sparse fully quadratic model as measurement noise with η = √p·κ'ef·Δ³ (proof of Theorem 4.5, p. 20) → convert coefficient error to value, gradient and Hessian errors with Lemma 4.1 (p. 18, pp. 20–21). Result: a fully quadratic model with probability ≥ 1 − n^(−γ log p) from p = O(n (log n)⁴) points when h = O(n) (Theorem 4.5, pp. 20–21, eq. (21)). The authors flag that the universal constant (c_total < 17190) makes the bound impractical for tractable n (p. 21) and that the card(α) factor in Lemma 4.1 cannot be removed (Remark 4.2, p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments**: MATLAB; minimum-Frobenius vs minimum-ℓ1 variants of Algorithm 5.1 vs NEWUOA on 26 CUTEr problems, performance profiles in function evaluations at accuracies 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻⁶, with a stop-then-retighten rule for fairness (pp. 25–27); then 13 CUTEr problems with known Hessian sparsity, n = 20–22, where the ℓ1 variant wins about 8 of 9 cases (pp. 26–29). Failures are shown: two problems unsolved by both variants (Table 1, p. 26); the 5000-evaluation cap is hit on GENHUMPS, WOODS and (ℓ1 only) POWELLSG (Table 3, p. 29).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Theory (first fully quadratic model from underdetermined interpolation, p. 8, p. 21) plus a practical solver that beats NEWUOA on the test set (p. 26, p. 28). Limits stated concretely: the theorem does not validate the practical algorithm (p. 21); impractical constants (p. 21); LP overhead and no easy warm start for larger n (pp. 28–29); ℓ2-ball basis open (p. 16); convergence theory for randomly sampled models left for future work (p. 6, p. 22). Future directions: sparse gradients or sparse fully linear models (p. 29), use outside optimization (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (p. 5, pp. 22–23): deterministic model-improvement certification (O(n⁶) poisedness check) is replaced by random sample sets that give fully quadratic models with high probability; the practical solver keeps geometry minimal and says openly that this is theoretically weak but works in practice (p. 23).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence, early form (pp. 5–6): model quality is stated as a probability, and the authors say a stochastic trust-region framework must be designed to use it (p. 6) — the seed of BSV 2014.
- Method 6 ⚠ variant (p. 1, p. 2, p. 8): the structure (Hessian sparsity) is exploited without knowing the pattern — ℓ1 recovers it automatically — whereas SKILL.md Method 6 assumes the structure is identified first.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 25–26): comparison by function evaluations against a Powell-family code (NEWUOA, with its best interpolation-set size, p. 26) under a written fairness rule for tolerances.
- new-pattern candidate: reverse field import — take a neighbouring field's recovery theory and put it inside the optimizer (p. 2, p. 28).
- new-pattern candidate: theory as motivation, gap stated — a rigorous theorem for an idealised random-sampling version next to a deterministic practical algorithm, with the gap written out (p. 6, p. 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove that a model built from few samples is accurate, treat (f − best structured model) as bounded measurement noise, set the noise level to the model's Taylor-error bound (√p·κ·Δ³), and apply a noisy-recovery theorem (p. 20).
- Build an orthonormal, uniformly bounded basis on a domain where integrals factorise (the hypercube, via Fubini) so that random-sampling RIP results apply with a dimension-free constant (pp. 13–15).
- Fair DFO comparison: run each solver with its default stopping rule, rerun with tighter tolerances if convergence cannot be declared, and profile function evaluations against a reference optimum at two accuracy levels (pp. 25–26).
- Put both model-selection rules (Frobenius and ℓ1) inside the same trust-region code so the experiment isolates the model choice (pp. 24–25).

**Quotes**:
- "In this paper we propose a reverse relationship between optimization and compressed sensing — instead of using optimization methods to solve compressed sensing problems we use the results of compressed sensing to improve optimization methods by recovering and exploiting possible structures of the black-box objective functions." (p. 2)
- "Theorem 4.5 cannot strictly validate a practical setting in DFO like the one discussed in the next section." (p. 21)

### S053 · On partial sparse recovery (arXiv preprint 2013, arXiv 1304.2809)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. S. Bandeira, L. N. Vicente · **Position in trajectory**: The enabling-lemma paper for S033: S033 leaves the details of partially sparse recovery to a separate paper (S033 p. 3), and S053 names sparse Hessian recovery [5] as an application (p. 1). **Read**: full (6 pages). Page 1 of the text file is split into one token per line but is readable.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Recover x = (x1, x2) where only x1 is expected to be sparse, by minimizing ‖x1‖₁ subject to A1x1 + A2x2 = y, problem (3) (p. 1). Applications: image reconstruction and sparse Hessian recovery (p. 1). Prior sufficient conditions exist (Vaswani–Lu, Friedlander et al., Jacques) but are not simple extensions of NSP and RIP (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Define a partial NSP (Definition 3.1, p. 2) and a partial RIP as the RIP of PA1, with P the projector onto R(A2)^⊥ (Definition 3.2, p. 3). Show both are implied by the ordinary NSP and RIP with the same constant (Theorem 4.1, Theorem 4.2, Corollary 4.1, p. 3), extend noisy/compressible recovery (Theorem 5.2, p. 4), and show Gaussian matrices satisfy partial RIP with fewer measurements (Theorem 6.1, p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: A2 has full column rank (Definitions 3.1–3.2, pp. 2–3); for §VI, i.i.d. Gaussian entries with variance 1/k (p. 5). No optimization assumptions.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Projection reduction — recovering (x̄1, x̄2) is classical recovery for (PA1)x1 = Py followed by a linear solve for x2 (p. 3). NSP ⇒ partial NSP by choosing S = T ∪ (the dense indices) (p. 3). RIP ⇒ partial RIP by choosing x2 = −(A2ᵀA2)⁻¹A2ᵀA1x1 (p. 3). Noisy case: the x1-parts of problems (11) and (14) coincide, then x2 is bounded through C1 = ‖A1‖ and C2 = ‖A2†‖ (p. 4). Gaussian case: union bound over sparsity patterns with the Baraniuk et al. concentration lemma (p. 5). Result: k = O(s + log(N − r)) measurements when s − r = O(1), against the classical k = O(s log(N/s)) (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Pure theory (p. 1, p. 5). The concluding remarks state no limitations or future work (p. 5). Positioning is careful: Jacques' work was found after the results were obtained and is credited as giving the same recovery by a different route (p. 1).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence, weak (p. 5): known partial structure (a dense block with known support) lowers the number of samples needed; this is the sample-count side of the structure exploited in S033.
- Methods 1–5: no link (compressed-sensing theory only).
- new-pattern candidate: spin off the enabling lemma — the technical tool the main DFO paper needs is written up as a short standalone paper for the neighbouring field (S033 p. 3; S053 p. 1, p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To handle an unpenalised block of variables, project it out with P = I − A2(A2ᵀA2)⁻¹A2ᵀ, solve the classical problem for the rest, then recover the dense block by a linear solve (p. 3).
- When introducing a restricted or partial version of a known property, prove at once that the original property implies it with the same constant, so every existing random-matrix result transfers (Theorem 4.2, Corollary 4.1, p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "His conditions guarantee the same recovery as ours but, as far as we can tell, are not the simple extensions of the NSP and RIP properties." (p. 1)
- "Note that the condition (16) can be asymptotically smaller than the one found in the classical case r = 0." (p. 5)

### S100 · Efficiently Using Second Order Information in Large l1 Regularization Problems (arXiv preprint 2013, arXiv 1303.6935)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: X. Tang · **Position in trajectory**: The arXiv precursor of LHAC (Tang–Scheinberg, Math. Program. 160 (2016), listed under Method 6 in SKILL.md). S054 later analyses the rates of the same method. **Read**: full (14 pages; no appendix).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: ℓ1-regularized convex problems (sparse logistic regression, sparse inverse covariance) are large; first-order methods have cheap iterations but slow local convergence, and storing a Hessian is impractical beyond about 10⁴ variables (pp. 1–2). Recent second-order methods (GLMNET, QUIC, Wright, Olsen et al.) rely on active sets that shrink only near the optimum, and the best of them depend on problem-specific Hessian structure (pp. 2–3). Entry: second-order-type convergence at first-order per-iteration cost, with a general scheme (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep the proximal-Newton / SQP outer model of Tseng–Yun and coordinate descent on the Lasso subproblem, as in GLMNET and QUIC (p. 3, p. 4), but (i) replace the true Hessian by the L-BFGS compact form B = γI − QQ̂, so that (Bd)_i costs O(m) instead of O(N) or O(p) (p. 3, p. 5, p. 8); (ii) take the working set as the current nonzeros plus the s_k largest dual-constraint (subgradient) violations, a rule borrowed from LP and from Scheinberg's SVM active-set work (p. 3, p. 6, eq. (21)). Armijo backtracking on the composite objective (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions**: L convex and twice differentiable (p. 1); B_k positive definite, guaranteed when sᵢᵀtᵢ > 0 (strongly convex L), otherwise damped BFGS (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No rate in this preprint. Global convergence is inherited from the Tseng–Yun framework, which needs the Armijo step and positive definite B_k (pp. 6–7). The working-set rule is argued, not proved, to let every coordinate enter or leave, which the authors say is needed for convergence (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: Sparse logistic regression on 8 training sets from RCV1 and GISETTE vs FISTA and GLMNET, all re-implemented in MATLAB, CPU time at tolerances 10⁻² and 10⁻⁵ (pp. 8–9, Table 1 p. 11); sparse inverse covariance on 5 gene-expression sets vs QUIC, compared by counted flops in the coordinate-descent step because QUIC is C++ and LHAC is MATLAB, at 10⁻² and 10⁻⁶ (pp. 10–12, Table 2 p. 13). Table 1 shows LHAC not fastest in two of 16 runs (gisette500 at 10⁻⁵ vs GLMNET; gisette5000 at 10⁻² vs FISTA), while the text speaks of all but one experiment (p. 9, p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Algorithm plus empirical evidence (p. 3, p. 12). The conclusion names the two sources of efficiency (low-rank Hessian approximation, greedy active set) and states no limitations or future work (pp. 12–13).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (p. 3, p. 8): the low-rank form of the quasi-Newton matrix — not of the true Hessian — is the structure that makes each coordinate step O(m).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (p. 4, pp. 6–7): the outer framework (Tseng–Yun model, Armijo, L-BFGS compact representation) is kept; novelty is confined to the Hessian approximation and the working-set rule.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 9–11): equal footing is enforced by re-implementing competitors in one language, or, when that is impossible, by counting flops of the dominant step instead of CPU time; the unit is not function evaluations.
- new-pattern candidate: reuse own working-set rule across problem classes — the largest-violation working set from LP and SVM (Scheinberg 2006) is moved to ℓ1 problems (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If a quasi-Newton matrix is diagonal plus low rank (γI − QQ̂), cache d̂ = Q̂d and update it after each coordinate move so (Bd)_i costs O(m) (p. 8).
- When competitor codes are in different languages, count the flops of the step that dominates runtime (here 95–99% of time is spent in subproblems) and report that as the cost measure (pp. 10–11).
- Solve every instance twice, at a loose tolerance (quick useful solution) and a tight one (accurate solution), to expose the first-order vs second-order trade-off (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "The key observation here is that the Hessian approximation obtained by LBFGS is low rank unlike the true Hessian" (p. 3)
- "CPU time, as often used, will not be a reasonable complexity measure in this case, because QUIC is implemented in C++ and LHAC - in MATLAB." (p. 10)

### S051 · Smooth pinball neural network for probabilistic forecasting of wind power (arXiv preprint 2017, arXiv 1710.01720)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: K. Hatalis (first author), A. J. Lamadrid, S. Kishore · **Position in trajectory**: An applied ML collaboration at Lehigh (ECE and Economics), outside the DFO line; follow-up of S082 by the same group. Scheinberg's contribution is not stated in the text. **Read**: full (8 pages; reference list skimmed).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Probabilistic wind-power forecasting by quantile regression; estimating quantiles one by one causes quantile crossing, reordering heuristics lack a theoretical basis, and the constrained SVQR fix is slow to train (p. 2). The pinball loss is not differentiable at 0, which blocks gradient-based neural-network training (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace the pinball loss by Zheng's smooth approximation S_{τ,α}(u) = τu + α log(1 + exp(−u/α)) and train a one-hidden-layer network with one output per quantile by plain gradient-descent backpropagation (pp. 4–5). Replace hard non-crossing constraints by an initialization: random input weights, and output weights set by least squares (H†Q) to equally spaced quantile targets, in the style of an extreme learning machine (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: No distributional assumption on wind power (nonparametric, p. 3). The limit α → 0 recovering the pinball loss is cited from Zheng (p. 4). No convergence theory for training.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Only the backpropagation gradients of the smoothed loss (pp. 4–5). No theorem.
- **D5 Experiments**: GEFCom2014 wind data, 10 zones, all of 2013 as test data (87,600 hourly observations), 2-month sliding training window, 18 quantiles; baselines uniform, persistence, climatology, MQR and SVQR; metrics quantile score, interval score, ACE and crossing counts; SPNN with and without the initialization (pp. 5–7, Table I p. 7, Fig. 2 p. 6). Hyperparameters were chosen by empirical testing on training data (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Four listed contributions (smooth loss for multi-quantile NN, initialization scheme, sliding-window wind application, benchmark design; p. 2). The initialization does not guarantee non-crossing (p. 5). Future work: other renewables, prices and load, very-short-term forecasting from lagged power with recurrent architectures (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (p. 4): keep the standard trainer (gradient-descent backprop) and change only the piece that breaks it — the nondifferentiable loss is smoothed. The same minimal-change move, applied to a loss rather than to an oracle.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 5–7): head-to-head comparison against standard and advanced baselines on public competition data with a proper scoring rule, but without equal tuning budgets; hyperparameters are set by hand on training data (p. 6).
- new-pattern candidate: initialization as a soft constraint — impose a hard structural constraint (non-crossing) approximately through a symmetric initialization instead of a constrained solver, then count the remaining violations (p. 5, Fig. 2 p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Smooth a kink loss with τu + α log(1 + exp(−u/α)) to use off-the-shelf gradient training, and report α with the other hyperparameters (p. 4, p. 6).
- Ablate the heuristic (SPNN-wo vs SPNN-w) and count the violations it is meant to prevent, not only the accuracy score (Table I p. 7, Fig. 2 p. 6).
- Use a public competition data set and its official score (the quantile score) so results are comparable with the competition literature (p. 3, p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "This scheme does not guarantee there are no cross-overs, but it does greatly reduce them as that it is seen in section IV." (p. 5)
- "The goal of our study is not custom feature engineering, which could result in better scores, but to showcase the feasibility of our method as a forecasting model given standard features and compare it with common and advanced benchmarks." (p. 6)

### S052 · Black-box optimization in machine learning with trust region based derivative free algorithm (arXiv preprint 2017, arXiv 1703.06925)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: H. Ghanbari · **Position in trajectory**: Brings the practical DFO-TR code (the same radius-freeze rule as S033's Algorithm 5.1) and the STORM theory of Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg into ML black-box tasks (AUC maximization, hyperparameter tuning), against Bayesian optimization. **Read**: full (13 pages, including appendix tables and figures).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Black-box optimization has two nearly independent communities, Bayesian optimization in ML and DFO in optimization (p. 1). BO is the default for hyperparameter optimization but its per-iteration cost grows and it scales poorly with dimension, and random search has been shown to compete (p. 1). AUC is piecewise constant in the classifier weights, so gradient methods cannot optimize it directly (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Apply model-based DFO-TR (Algorithm 1, p. 4): a quadratic interpolation model from points near the iterate, one new function value per iteration, replacement of the farthest point, the radius shrunk only when more than d+1 points are available, no explicit geometry-improvement step (pp. 3–4). Stochastic variant S-DFO-TR: evaluate AUC on a random subsample whose size grows with k, and after an unsuccessful step re-sample f(w_k) and average it with the old value (p. 7), motivated by STORM (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions**: If positive and negative classes are jointly normal, E[AUC(w)] = Φ(μ_Z/σ_Z) is smooth in w (Theorems 1–3, pp. 4–5); the authors call this assumption too strong for most practical data and conjecture that smoothness holds more generally (p. 5). There are no convergence results for DFO-TR or STORM on deterministic nonsmooth noisy functions (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Only the Gaussian computation of E[AUC] (pp. 4–5). The displayed formula for φ is the normal density although the text calls φ the cumulative function (p. 5), a slip that does not affect the smoothness claim. Convergence is argued by reference to STORM and Blanchet et al. (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**: Branin, Camelback, Hartmann vs TPE, SMAC, SPEARMINT (Tables 1–3, p. 6); AUC of linear classifiers on 12 LIBSVM/UCI sets with equal function-evaluation budgets, 5-fold CV, optimizer time reported without evaluation time (Table 5, p. 7); random search also given twice the budget (p. 6, Table 6 p. 8); S-DFO-TR vs DFO-TR by accessed data points on shuttle and letter, 20 runs (p. 7, Fig. 1 p. 8); SVM hyperparameter tuning in 3 continuous parameters (Fig. 2, p. 8); pairwise-hinge baseline in the appendix (Table 7, p. 11). Losses are visible in Table 5: SPEARMINT gives higher AUC on ijcnn1, german and satimage, SMAC on segment and svmguide3 (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A computational comparison, borrowed theory, and a direct AUC optimizer (p. 3). Limits stated: tuning tested only with three continuous hyperparameters (p. 2); convergence difficulties when the dimension of w is very large (p. 7); the jointly normal assumption (p. 5); language differences in timing (pp. 6–7). Future: smoothness of E[AUC] for other distributions (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 3–4): minimal geometry — no model-improvement steps, points beyond θΔ_k discarded, radius frozen when the set is too small — justified by Scheinberg–Toint self-correcting geometry (p. 3). Same radius-freeze rule as S033 Algorithm 5.1 (S033 pp. 23–24).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (p. 7): the STORM contract (estimates need not be accurate far from the solution) becomes a growing sample-size schedule for AUC evaluations.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (p. 7): after an unsuccessful step the value at the current iterate is re-estimated and averaged so that a lucky high AUC cannot block progress — the re-estimate-f repair of Method 2 step 3.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence with caveat (pp. 6–8): equal evaluation budgets, optimizer time separated from evaluation time, random search given 2× budget; the competitors run in Python, Java and MATLAB, which the authors acknowledge (pp. 6–7).
- Taste mark 6 (DFO and ML zeroth-order optimization are one field) ✅ evidence (p. 1).
- new-pattern candidate: smooth in expectation — justify a smooth-model method on a discontinuous empirical objective by showing its expectation is smooth under a stated data model (pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before applying a smooth-optimization method to a discontinuous empirical metric, write the metric as a probability, compute its expectation under a tractable data model, and state that the model is illustrative only (pp. 4–5).
- For DFO on a subsampled objective, let the sample size grow with k and re-estimate and average f(w_k) after every unsuccessful step (p. 7).
- Give random search twice the evaluation budget, as in the hyperparameter-optimization literature, to pre-empt the random-search objection (p. 6, Table 6 p. 8).
- When budgets are equal, report optimizer overhead time separately from function-evaluation time (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "We can see that on all three problems DFO-TR reaches the global value accurately and quickly, outperforming BO methods." (p. 6)
- "noisy function evaluations do not need to be accurate far away from the optimal solution." (p. 7)

### S082 · An Empirical Analysis of Constrained Support Vector Quantile Regression for Nonparametric Probabilistic Forecasting of Wind Power (AAAI Workshops 2017, arXiv 1803.10888)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: K. Hatalis (first author), S. Kishore, A. J. Lamadrid · **Position in trajectory**: The earlier of the two wind-forecasting papers; the follow-up S051 describes CSVQR as slow to train (S051 p. 2). Scheinberg's contribution is not stated in the text. **Read**: full (7 pages).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Probabilistic wind forecasting; the authors say SVMs had not been examined for wind uncertainty forecasting, and quantile crossing requires joint estimation with non-crossing constraints (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Re-purpose the non-crossing constrained SVQR of Takeuchi et al. (2006): a primal over M quantiles with constraints w_mᵀφ(x_i) ≤ w_{m+1}ᵀφ(x_i), its Lagrangian and dual QP (pp. 3–4), RBF kernel, solved by SMO (p. 4); sliding 3-month training window (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Kernel quantile regression model; the density is described nonparametrically by quantiles (p. 2). No theory.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Only the dual derivation (pp. 3–4).
- **D5 Experiments**: GEFCom2014, Zone 1 only, test months June–November 2013, 13 engineered features; baselines persistence (last 12 h), climatology and uniform; metrics PICP/ACE (Table 1, p. 6) and Q-score (Table 2, p. 7); other kernels were tried and dropped (p. 4). Climatology has lower ACE in June and October (p. 5, Table 1 p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: First use of CSVQR with a sliding window for wind (p. 2). The text claims a margin of several magnitudes over all three benchmarks in the other months (p. 5, second quote); Table 1 shows ACE ratios against climatology from about 1 (September, 60%: 0.69 vs 0.70) to about 24 (November, 80%: 0.42 vs 10.14) (p. 6). No limitations stated; future work: electricity prices and load (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (pp. 4–6): only naive baselines, one zone, six months, no advanced competitor and no cost comparison, and the verbal claim goes beyond the table (p. 5 vs p. 6). Advanced baselines (MQR, SVQR) appear only in the follow-up S051 (S051 p. 6). This is evidence that the student-led application papers of this collaboration do not follow the comparison standard of the core group papers.
- Methods 1–4, 6: no link.
- new-pattern candidate (trajectory note, not a method): applied side-collaboration with a lighter evidence standard (S082 pp. 4–6; S051 p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Evaluate interval forecasts with both coverage (PICP/ACE) and a proper scoring rule (quantile score): the paper itself attributes climatology's June/October ACE wins to wide intervals (p. 5).
- Use sliding-window training (train on the previous k months, forecast the next) to mimic operational forecasting (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "This formulation is re-purposed here for probabilistic forecasting." (p. 1)
- "However in all other months CSVQR outperformed all three benchmarks by several magnitudes." (p. 5)

### S054 · Proximal quasi-Newton methods for regularized convex optimization with linear and accelerated sublinear convergence rates (Computational Optimization and Applications 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10589-017-9964-z; arXiv 1607.03081)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: H. Ghanbari · **Position in trajectory**: Theory follow-up to LHAC (Scheinberg–Tang, Math. Program. 160 (2016), ref. [19]; p. 1) that reuses the full-backtracking FISTA of Scheinberg–Goldfarb–Bai (ref. [17], FoCM 2014; p. 4, p. 6, p. 31). **Read**: partial — §1–§3 in full, §4.1, statements of Lemmas 1–6 and Theorems 8–10 with the proofs of Lemma 2, Theorem 8 and Lemma 5, §5 and §6; the algebra of Lemma 4 (pp. 19–20) was skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The inexact PQNA of Scheinberg–Tang has only an O(1/k) rate, and with L-BFGS Hessians proximal quasi-Newton methods are essentially first-order in theory (p. 3). The accelerated PQNA of Jiang–Sun–Toh needs 0 ≺ H_k ⪯ H_{k−1} while H_k must also over-approximate f; the two can conflict, and for H_k = I/μ_k the condition forces nondecreasing step sizes, the opposite of FISTA's rule (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: (i) Analyse the inexact PQNA (Algorithm 3, p. 8) under strong convexity, with ε_k-accurate subproblems and a relaxed, trust-region-like decrease test F(p) − F(v) ≤ η(Q_H(p, v) − F(v)) (p. 7). (ii) Give an inner-iteration count rule r(k) ≥ k log_{1/α}(k/ℓ) that delivers the needed ε_k without knowing ρ (Remark 4, p. 12; Theorem 7 for randomized coordinate descent, p. 14). (iii) APQNA (Algorithm 5, p. 16) replaces monotone H_k by σ_{k+1}H_{k+1} ⪯ σ_kH_k, with θ_k = σ_k/σ_{k+1} entering t_{k+1}, following the full-backtracking FISTA of [17] (pp. 15–16); Algorithm 6 fixes H and scales it by 1/σ_k (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f convex and L-smooth, g proper lsc convex, mI ⪯ H_k ⪯ MI (Assumption 1, p. 8); γ-strong convexity of f for the linear rate (Assumption 2, p. 8); σ_k ≥ σ > 0 for the accelerated rate (Theorem 9, p. 21), which the authors show can fail for general H_k (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Linear rate ρ = 1 − ηγ/(γ + M) from a one-step comparison with the segment tx* + (1−t)x_k (proof of Theorem 3, pp. 9–10); errors accumulate as ρ^k Σ ε_i/ρ^i, so ε_i ≤ Cρ^{iδ} suffices (p. 11); randomized coordinate descent is handled in expectation (Theorems 5–7, pp. 13–14). Accelerated rate: the weighted FISTA energy 2σ_k t_k² v_k + σ_k u_kᵀH_k u_k is nonincreasing (Lemma 4, pp. 19–20), giving F(x_k) − F* ≤ ‖x0 − x*‖²/(2σ_k t_k²) (Theorem 8, p. 20) and σ_k t_k² ≥ (Σ√σ_i/2)² (Lemma 5, p. 21). Counterexample: alternating diag(10,1)/diag(1,10) forces σ_k ≤ 10^(−k), so no rate follows (p. 22); σ_k ≥ σ/k still gives O(1/k) (Theorem 10, p. 22). Remark 3: the worst-case linear rate equals PGA's; the practical gain is explained by smaller local bounds M_k (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: Sparse logistic regression (λ = 10⁻³) on a9a, mnist, connect-4, HAPT; APGA, APQNA-FH, PQNA-FH, PQNA-LBFGS, APQNA-LBFGS compared by iterations, function evaluations (acceleration costs two evaluations per iteration) and time, with L-BFGS memory swept from 1 to 10 (pp. 23–29). The accelerated L-BFGS variant is worse than the plain one in function evaluations (p. 29). The caption of Table 3 names PQNA-LBFGS, but its rows are APQNA-LBFGS (p. 28).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: First linear rate for this proximal quasi-Newton class, preserved under inexact subproblems, a practical inner-iteration rule, a relaxed decrease condition, and APQNA with O(1/k²) under a condition proven for fixed-matrix scaling (pp. 29–30). Limits stated concretely: the σ_k condition may fail for general H_k (p. 22); no practical way was found to enforce it with L-BFGS (p. 24); strong convexity might be relaxed to quadratic growth, left for future work (p. 9). Future: other acceleration schemes for proximal quasi-Newton methods (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (p. 4, pp. 15–16): PQNA and FISTA are kept; only the Hessian-sequence condition that makes the earlier analysis unrealistic is changed, using the full-backtracking device of [17].
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 11–14): the contract is on the inner solver — subproblem accuracy ε_k ≤ Cρ^{kδ}, tied to the outer iteration and met by an iteration-count rule (in expectation for randomized coordinate descent) — rather than a probability-p oracle.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pp. 10–11, 20–22): deterministic potential-function analysis; parity is checked explicitly — the worst-case linear rate equals PGA's (Remark 3, p. 11), and APQNA matches APGA's O(1/k²) only under σ_k ≥ σ.
- Taste mark 5 (minimal safeguard backed by a negative result) ✅ evidence (p. 22): a two-matrix counterexample shows the σ_k condition can collapse, and a relaxed theorem (σ_k ≥ σ/k) shows what survives.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 26–29): accelerated and non-accelerated methods are compared in function evaluations as well as iterations, which changes the verdict.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (p. 12): diagonal-plus-low-rank L-BFGS makes each coordinate step O(m).
- new-pattern candidate: negative-result paper — the headline finding is that a popular add-on (acceleration) does not help here, backed by theory and experiment (p. 1, p. 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Relax the sufficient-decrease test to F(new) − F(old) ≤ η·(model decrease) and absorb the slack into an error term ξ_k ≤ (1 − 1/η)(F(x_{k+1}) − F(x_k)), so the exact-case proof goes through (p. 7, p. 9).
- To make an inexact inner solver analysable, require ε_k ≤ Cρ^{kδ} and implement it as an inner iteration count r(k) ≥ k log_{1/α}(k/ℓ), which needs no knowledge of ρ (Theorem 4, Remark 4, pp. 11–12).
- When extending an accelerated method to a variable metric, put the metric into the Lyapunov function with a weight σ_k, require σ_{k+1}H_{k+1} ⪯ σ_kH_k, and test the requirement with an alternating two-matrix counterexample (p. 16, pp. 19–22).
- Compare accelerated methods in function evaluations, not only iterations, because acceleration costs extra evaluations per iteration (p. 26, p. 29).

**Quotes**:
- "Our analysis and computational results show that acceleration may not bring any benefit in the quasi-Newton setting." (p. 1)
- "we believe that the practical experiments support our theoretical analysis in that applying acceleration scheme in the case of variable Hessian estimates may not result in a faster algorithm." (p. 29)

### S063 · When does stochastic gradient algorithm work well? (arXiv preprint 2018, arXiv 1801.06159)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: L. M. Nguyen, N. H. Nguyen, D. T. Phan, J. R. Kalagnanam · **Position in trajectory**: An SGD side-project with IBM co-authors from the SARAH period (SARAH is ref. [9], p. 12); it explains fixed-step SGD rather than proposing a method. **Read**: partial — §1–§4, the appendix lemmas (eq. (17), Lemma 6) and the proof of Theorem 1 (pp. 13–15). Proofs of Theorems 2–3 and Corollaries 1–3 (pp. 16–21) were not read. The ε glyph is missing in this text file; bounds with ε below are restored from the definitions.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Observation: SGD with a fixed, fairly large step converges fast to a neighbourhood of the solution without variance reduction (p. 2). A two-regime toy (a quartic signal-recovery problem with small vs large noise) shows SGD succeeds when most component gradients are small at the optimum and wanders otherwise (pp. 2–3). Entry: turn this into an assumption on the distribution of stochastic gradients at the solution (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep plain minibatch SGD with a fixed step (Algorithm 1, p. 5); define p = P(‖g*‖² ≤ ε) and M = E[‖g*‖² | ‖g*‖² > ε] at the solution (Definition 2, p. 5) and state all rates in terms of max{ε, 1 − p} (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: F μ-strongly convex with every f(·; ξ) L-smooth and convex (Theorem 1, p. 6); convex case (Theorem 2, p. 6); nonconvex case needs Assumption 1 — along the algorithm's own iterates, the average squared deviation of the stochastic gradients from those at some stationary point is at most N times the average ‖∇F‖² (p. 7) — with p and M taken as inf/sup over the set W* of such points (Definition 4, p. 7). Unbiased i.i.d. minibatches (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Key device: split the second moment at the optimum by conditioning, E‖g*‖² ≤ εp + (1 − p)M (eq. (17), p. 13), and plug it into the standard SGD recursion with the bound E‖∇f(w; ξ) − ∇f(w*; ξ)‖² ≤ 2L[F(w) − F(w*)] (Lemma 6, pp. 13–14; proof of Theorem 1, p. 15). Results (ε restored): linear convergence to a ball of size 4η(1 + M)ε/μ when 1 − p ≤ ε (Corollary 1, p. 6); an O(ε) gap in O(1/ε) iterations in the convex case (Corollary 2, p. 6) and a similar bound on the average squared gradient in the nonconvex case (Corollary 3, p. 8). Lemma 1: a larger minibatch b lowers the variance, so 1 − p ≤ ε holds for smaller ε (pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Experiments**: They measure the assumption itself: the fraction p of per-sample gradients below thresholds at an L-BFGS-computed w* for ℓ2-regularized logistic regression on 8 data sets (7 from LIBSVM) (Table 1, p. 9), plus SGD vs SVRG vs L-BFGS curves (Fig. 2, pp. 9–10); for FFN/CNN on MNIST, SVHN, CIFAR10 and CIFAR100, p at the Adam solution, minimum over 10 seeds, and an estimate of N through the ratio r_t (Table 2 p. 10, Fig. 3 p. 11). They note that Adam did not reach a true local solution for SVHN-FFN and CIFAR100-CNN (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: An explanatory assumption, rates under it, and empirical checks (p. 4). One limit is stated (Adam non-convergence, p. 11). Unstated: the nonconvex solutions that are checked come from Adam, not from the analysed SGD (p. 10), and Assumption 1 depends on the algorithm's own iterates (p. 7). The conclusion links ε to the minibatch size (pp. 11–12); no future work is listed.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (p. 5): the accurate-enough-often-enough form reappears — a fraction p of stochastic gradients must be small, and results are stated in max{ε, 1 − p} — but as a property of the problem at w*, not as a per-iteration oracle contract.
- Method 1 step 3 ✅ evidence (p. 11): the reachable neighbourhood is fixed by the variance at the solution, and the rate is full-gradient-like until that floor.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence, weak (p. 2, p. 5): the method practitioners use (fixed-step SGD) is analysed, not replaced; ⚠ it is not adaptive, so it sits outside the core of Method 2.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pp. 6–8): expected-rate bounds after a fixed number of steps; no stopping-time or high-probability analysis.
- new-pattern candidate: measure the assumption — a whole section verifies the new assumption numerically on standard data sets and networks (pp. 8–11).
- new-pattern candidate: two-regime toy example — a 2-D example in which the property holds in one regime and fails in the other motivates the assumption before any theory (pp. 2–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split E‖g(w*)‖² by conditioning on {‖g(w*)‖² ≤ ε} to get εp + (1 − p)M, then carry max{ε, 1 − p} through a standard SGD recursion (p. 13, p. 15).
- Verify a new structural assumption empirically: compute w* accurately (full-batch L-BFGS to ‖∇F‖ ≤ 10⁻¹²) and report the percentage of per-sample gradients below several thresholds (p. 9, Table 1).
- For nonconvex models, report the worst case over seeds (minimum over 10 seeds) when checking an assumption, not the average (p. 10).
- Open with a two-regime toy example in which the property holds in one regime and fails in the other (pp. 2–3).

**Quotes**:
- "a simple SGD with a fixed, reasonably large, step size can have a fast convergence rate to some neighborhood of the optimal solutions, without resorting to additional procedures for variance reduction." (p. 2)
- "The purpose of this section is to numerically validate our assumptions on p as defined in Definition 2." (p. 8)
