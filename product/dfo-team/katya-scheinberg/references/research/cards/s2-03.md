### S066 · Alternating direction methods for non convex optimization with applications to second-order least-squares and risk parity portfolio selection (Optimization-Online 2015, no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: X. Bai (Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh period. The paper carries the alternating linearization method (ALM) of Goldfarb–Ma–Scheinberg (cited as [12], p. 12; S005 in c01) from convex composite objectives to a nonconvex two-block objective that is not a sum of functions. The driver is the Bai–Scheinberg–Tütüncü least-squares model of risk parity ([3], p. 2). The paper is dated Sep 2, 2014 and was revised Feb 11, 2015 (p. 1). **Read**: abstract and §1–§4 in full (pp. 1–19), including the proofs of Lemma 3.1, Theorems 3.1–3.2, Lemma 4.1 and Theorem 4.1. I read §6 (experiments) and §7 (conclusion) (pp. 23–30). I only skimmed §5, the SOS/DSOS/SDSOS background (pp. 20–23). References were not read beyond [3], [12] and [14].

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem comes from an application.
  - The motivating problem: risk parity asks for equal risk contributions x_i(Σx)_i, and [3] writes it as a least-squares problem (p. 2). The general problem is to minimise a sum of squares of quadratics over a convex set. This is a quartic, and it is NP-hard in general (p. 2).
  - The entry point: ADMM and ALM are well studied for convex composite (sum-of-functions) objectives, but in the nonconvex case they are "not well understood" (p. 7). Existing nonconvex ADMM proofs assume that the differences between successive iterates go to zero (pp. 7–8).
  - The generalisation: the paper covers F(x) = h(f(x), g(x)), where fixing either block leaves a convex function (blockwise convexity). F is not assumed to be a sum (pp. 3, 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Splitting**: variable splitting x = y makes every block subproblem convex. For risk parity the subproblems are QPs (pp. 6–7).
  - **Two schemes**:
    - ADMM with a fixed, small penalty/prox parameter µ (Algorithm 2, p. 7).
    - ALM, which linearises the other block through its partial gradient. It chooses µ_k by backtracking until F(x_{k+1}) ≤ Q_µ(x_{k+1}, y_k) (Algorithms 3–4, pp. 12, 16).

    Linearising only one block, by always skipping the other, gives the partial linearization method, PLM (Algorithm 5, pp. 16–17).
  - **Two bridges to classical methods**:
    - ADMM is an inexact ALM whose multiplier is a "mixture" of gradient information. This is why ADMM has no descent guarantee and why its analysis needs a constant µ (p. 17).
    - PLM and ALM act as a cheap Levenberg–Marquardt method that keeps only part of the JᵀJ term (pp. 18–19).
  - **Kept**: the ADMM and ALM templates, and the FISTA-style backtracking condition (p. 12).
  - **Changed**: the analysis, which now rests on blockwise convexity instead of composite structure (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - ∇F, ∇₁F and ∇₂F are L-Lipschitz. F1 and F2 are convex in the free block. F is bounded below (p. 5).
  - ADMM needs µ < 1/(4L) (Theorem 3.1, p. 9). ALM needs µ_k ≥ τ > 0 (Theorem 4.1, p. 14).
  - In the experiments, µ for ADMM and AL is tuned instead, to avoid computing the Lipschitz constant (p. 24).
  - The results cover two blocks only. The authors say that extending them to more blocks is not trivial (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **ADMM**: optimality of the y-update gives the new multiplier as −∇₂F(x_{k+1}, y_{k+1}). The change in the multiplier is therefore at most L times the change in the primal variables ((3.4)–(3.5), p. 8). The block subproblems are strongly convex. So the augmented Lagrangian decreases by (1/(2µ) − 2µL²)(‖Δx‖² + ‖Δy‖²), and it is bounded below by F (p. 9). The paper calls this a relaxed version of Hong et al.'s Lemma 2.3 (p. 8).
  - **Rate**: summing the decreases gives min_{i≤k}(‖Δx_i‖ + ‖Δy_i‖) = O(1/√k). This bounds the stationarity error at the best iterate (Theorem 3.2, pp. 10–11). In the unconstrained case the paper says this recovers the standard O(1/ε²) complexity result (p. 12).
  - **ALM**: Lemma 4.1 gives F(u) − F(x̂) ≥ ‖x̂ − u‖²/(2µ). It follows from blockwise convexity plus the subproblem's optimality condition evaluated at x = u (pp. 13–14). The same best-iterate argument then gives the rate (pp. 14–16).
- **D5 Experiments**: Risk-parity instances are random 20×20 and 200×200 covariances, a hard 5-asset case, a 14-asset allocation and a 482-asset group risk parity. Six local methods are compared by iterations and by QP solves, the dominant cost, to KKT tolerances 10⁻³–10⁻⁷ (pp. 24–28), and global lower bounds come from SOS, a sequential SOS scheme and DSOS/SDSOS (pp. 27–30). fmincon is left out because [3] had already shown that ALM beats it (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and theoretical (convergence and an O(1/√k) rate for nonconvex ADMM and ALM), plus an application with global certificates (pp. 29–30). The stated limits are concrete:
  - the results cover two blocks only (p. 8);
  - the ADMM analysis needs a small constant µ (p. 17);
  - the variant with a separate θ update falls outside the theory and is also worse in practice (p. 24);
  - on the 14-asset case, the sequential SOS scheme returns an inaccurate positive bound after more than an hour, and SOSTOOLS applied directly gives no answer within 24 hours (p. 28);
  - DSOS and SDSOS need "Further extensive testing" (p. 29).

  Future work: new applications (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (pp. 12, 16–17): ADMM and ALM with FISTA-type backtracking are kept from the convex literature, including the authors' own ALM [12]. The new work is a proof under a weaker structure: blockwise convexity with no sum form. The analysis also explains why ADMM needs a fixed µ: its multiplier is not an exact partial gradient, so descent is not guaranteed (p. 17).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (pp. 3, 5–6, 18–19): the two-block structure h(f, g) makes every subproblem convex. For least squares, the scheme is read as a cheaper, structured Levenberg–Marquardt method.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant, deterministic (pp. 11–12, 16): there is no stochastic process, but the rate is checked against the standard deterministic order (p. 12), as Workflow C step 4 requires.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (p. 25): the tables count the expensive unit (QP subproblem solves) next to iterations. This is Method 5's rule of counting what costs, applied outside DFO. There is no noise and no function-evaluation count.
- new-pattern candidate: **counterexample-first / hidden-assumption audit**, here without a counterexample. Prior nonconvex ADMM proofs rest on a condition that cannot be verified in advance or enforced during a run (p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate: **local upper bound plus global lower-bound certificate**. The local method gives an upper bound. An SOS relaxation gives a lower bound. The relaxation is grown by adding only the bounds that are active at the local solution, or violated by the relaxation's own solution. A matching lower bound certifies global optimality, and a positive one certifies that no risk-parity solution exists (pp. 20, 27–29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove that the augmented Lagrangian of a nonconvex ADMM decreases, use the last block's optimality condition to write the multiplier as a partial gradient. The multiplier change is then Lipschitz-bounded by the primal change, and the sufficient decrease absorbs it (pp. 8–9).
- To get a rate without assuming that the iterates converge, sum the sufficient decreases and bound the smallest step over the first k iterations by O(1/√k). Then bound stationarity at that best iterate (pp. 10–11, 15).
- Keep in the comparison table a variant that breaks your theory's assumptions (here, a separate θ update). If it is also worse in practice, that supports the theory-driven design (p. 24).
- When methods do different amounts of work per iteration, report the count of the dominant-cost operation (subproblem solves), not only iterations (p. 25).
- When a global relaxation is too large, seed it with the active constraints of a local solution. Then add only the constraints that the relaxation's solution violates (Algorithm 6, pp. 28–29).

**Quotes**:
- "Our focus and main contribution is to analyze these methods in a nonconvex setting where the objective function cannot be represented as a sum of multiple functions." (p. 3)
- "While such assumption seems reasonable in that the iterates produced by the algorithm do not exhibit any erratic behavior, it is not clear if this condition can be verified in advance or enforced during the progress of the algorithm." (p. 8)

### S094 · Superposition of protein structures using electrostatic isopotentials (IEEE BIBM 2015, DOI 10.1109/bibm.2015.7359658)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Z. Guo, J. Hong and B. Y. Chen; Chen is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: this is the follow-up of DFO-VASP (S085 in s2-02, cited here as [40], p. 2). It has the same group, the same 14 serine proteases and 10 enolases (p. 4) and the same Ska backbone baseline. The objective is now the overlap of electrostatic isopotentials, computed by VASP-E. The work was funded by an NSF grant to Chen and Scheinberg (p. 7). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–8).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Structure comparison normally superposes atoms. But electrostatics act at long range, before the backbone makes contact, so an atom-based superposition can miss binding preferences (p. 1). The paper claims the first algorithm that superposes proteins using electric fields alone. It frames the work as a test of a hypothesis: proteins with the same binding preference should show a larger isopotential overlap (p. 1). The authors call it a "first proof of concept" (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Objective**: VASP-E, a lattice-based constructive-solid-geometry approximation of the intersection volume of the positive and negative isopotentials (p. 3).
  - **Variables**: a 7-vector made of a rotation axis, an angle and a translation (p. 3).
  - **Optimizer**: the trust-region DFO method of the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente book [21], used unchanged. The only preprocessing is to translate the centroids to the origin (p. 3).
  - **Oddity**: formulation (1) is written as min f = f⁺ + f⁻, although the aim is to maximise the overlap. The sign convention is not explained (p. 3).
  - **Contrast with S085**: S085 had a dynamic accuracy rule. Here the VASP-E resolution is fixed after an offline calibration (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - The optimizer finds a local optimum only. The paper says the global optimum cannot be guaranteed (p. 3).
  - The objective is noisy at any finite resolution. Relative noise is δ_r = (f*(x) − f_r(x))/f*(x), with f* approximated at the finest affordable resolution r* = 0.25. The definition is credited to [53] (pp. 4–5).
  - Smoothness is not discussed.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: None.
- **D5 Experiments**: The steps are resolution calibration on one representative pair, which leads to r = 1.0 (Fig. 5, pp. 4–5); 10 Latin-hypercube starts against a Ska warm start (Fig. 6, p. 5); same- vs different-specificity overlaps at ±2.5–10 kT/e (Figs. 7–8, pp. 5–6); a single-isopotential variant (Fig. 9, p. 6); and all-atom RMSD against Ska (Fig. 10, p. 7). There is no optimizer baseline and no function-evaluation count; the paper says only that DFO evaluates "dozens" of superpositions (p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an application and proof of concept (pp. 1–2).
  - Stated limits:
    - chymotrypsins and muconate-lactonizing enzymes are not separated from proteins with different binding preferences (p. 6);
    - the optimizer finds local optima only (p. 3);
    - a large overlap does not guarantee functional similarity (p. 7).
  - Future work: comparing electric fields, and molecular design (p. 7).
  - One claim is stronger than its evidence: two or three restarts are said to guarantee a high-quality superposition, based on 10 starts against one reference protein (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 4–5): the noise is measured before optimizing. Relative noise and runtime are plotted across the fidelity knob, then a fixed fidelity is chosen. This is the noise-estimation experiment of Workflow A's checkpoint. There is no accuracy contract tied to Δ, and S085's adaptive rule is dropped.
- Method 2 ✅ weak evidence (p. 3): the book's trust-region DFO method is applied unchanged to a new black box.
- Method 5: no link. There is no competing optimizer and no evaluation count, as in S085.
- new-pattern candidate: **validate against an independent domain criterion**. A large objective value does not ensure functional similarity (p. 7), so the solutions are checked with a metric the optimizer did not use (RMSD to the backbone alignment), and the outliers are traced to low-overlap runs (p. 7).
- Bibliographic note: [53], cited for δ_r, is "Methodologies and software for derivative-free optimization" by R. Chen, Z. Guo, B. Y. Chen and K. Scheinberg, listed as submitted (p. 8). S046 below has the same title but different authors (Custódio–Scheinberg–Vicente), and it contains no δ_r definition (S046 read in full). The texts do not settle how the two are related.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Calibrate a simulator's fidelity knob before you optimize. Take the finest affordable setting as the truth, plot relative error and runtime across settings, and fix the cheapest setting whose noise you can accept (pp. 4–5).
- Show where a domain-knowledge warm start falls within the distribution of final values from space-filling random starts, for example with a box plot per instance (Fig. 6, p. 5).
- Check the optimizer's output with an independent structural metric, and trace any outliers back to low-objective runs (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "To compare proteins based on their electric fields, we have developed the first algorithm designed to superpose protein structures using electric fields alone." (p. 1)
- "Restarting DFO two or three times can thereby guarantee a high quality superposition." (p. 5)

### S121 · Um método de Região de Confiança sem Derivadas com Modelos Construídos por máquinas de Vetores Suporte para Regressão (Proceeding Series of the Brazilian Society of Computational and Applied Mathematics 2015, DOI 10.5540/03.2015.003.01.0439)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. Verdério (UFPR/UFSC), E. W. Karas and L. G. Pedroso (UFPR) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a 7-page extended abstract in Portuguese, presented at XXXV CNMAC (Natal, 2014; p. 1). It plugs support-vector-regression (SVR) models into the convex-constrained trust-region DFO algorithm of Conejo, Karas, Pedroso et al. ([2], 2013), which accepts any model that is accurate enough (pp. 1–2). The IDFO book is the DFO reference ([4], p. 2). Definitions 2–3 adapt Λ-poisedness without a citation (p. 5). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–7).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem (1) is to minimise f over a nonempty, closed, convex Ω, where f is differentiable but its derivatives are unavailable (p. 1). The algorithm of [2] allows any model that approximates f accurately enough. The stated goal is to show that SVR models meet this hypothesis, which convergence requires (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **The SVR part**: the model is an ε-SVR fit (Vapnik's ε-insensitive loss with regularized risk ½‖w‖² + C·Σ|·|_ε), solved through its dual QP with box and equality constraints (pp. 3–4). Quadratic models use a feature map φ with the kernel xᵢᵀxⱼ + (xᵢᵀxⱼ)² (pp. 4–5).
  - **The DFO-specific moves**:
    1. The SVR tube width and slacks are tied to the radius: ε ≤ c1Δ² and ξ, ξ' ≤ c2Δ² (Theorems 1–2, pp. 5–6).
    2. Geometry is controlled by Λ-poisedness adapted to SVR: on the sample points for linear models, and in φ-space for quadratic models (Definitions 2–3, p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - The sample is treated as i.i.d. from some measure dP(x, y), the standard SVR framing (p. 2).
  - A Λ-poised set needs at least n+1 points for linear models and (n+1)(n+2)/2 points for quadratic models (p. 5).
  - As printed, Definition 2 asks for n+1 difference vectors x^j − x^0 in Rⁿ to be linearly independent, which is impossible. Probably n points are meant. Definition 3 has the same off-by-one issue in φ-space (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorems 1–2 state |f(x) − m(x)| ≤ κΔ² on B(x0, Δ) for linear and for quadratic SVR models (pp. 5–6). The text gives no proofs.
  - Only a function-value bound is stated. There is no gradient-error bound, and there is no Δ³ order for the quadratic case. The text does not say whether [2] needs more than this.
  - With exactly n+1 or (n+1)(n+2)/2 points, the slacks are zero (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Experiments**: An illustration only: SVR models of Rosenbrock near (1, 1) with radii 0.5 and 0.1 (Figs. 1–2, p. 6). No optimization runs.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory in preliminary form (theorem statements only). The conclusion says the SVR technique can build models when only one point of f is known, an advantage for expensive functions (p. 7). The theorems do not cover that case, because Λ-poisedness needs at least n+1 points (p. 5). No limits or future work are stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (p. 5): the new model class (SVR) enters DFO only together with a poisedness definition and an error bound scaled by Δ, as in the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente framework.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant, deterministic (pp. 5–6): the fitting tolerance (the SVR tube ε and the slacks) scales as Δ². This is the error ≤ κΔ^q shape of the oracle contract, applied to model fitting and without a probability.
- new-pattern candidate: **reverse field import** (s01/S033): here the theory of an ML regression method (SVR) becomes the model builder of a DFO method (pp. 2–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To bring any regression method into model-based DFO, make its fitting tolerance an explicit function of the trust-region radius (here O(Δ²)). State the geometry condition in the method's own feature space (pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "No algoritmo proposto em [2] qualquer modelo pode ser utilizado desde que forne¸ca uma aproxima¸c˜ao suficientemente precisa da fun¸c˜ao objetivo." (p. 2)
- "pois a t´ecnica permite a constru¸c˜ao de modelos conhecendo apenas um ponto da fun¸c˜ao original." (p. 7)

### S046 · Methodologies and software for derivative-free optimization (Advances and Trends in Optimization with Engineering Applications 2017, DOI 10.1137/1.9781611974683.ch37)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. L. Custódio and L. N. Vicente (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: chapter 37 of a SIAM volume on engineering optimization, dated March 14, 2017 (p. 1). It appeared eight years after the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente book [53]. It is a map of DFO methods and software for users, not a research paper. **Read**: full main text (pp. 1–13; p. 14 is blank). I checked the bibliography (pp. 15–21) only for the entries cited below.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The user's problem: derivatives are neither available nor approximable, and function values often come from a simulation with noise (p. 2). The chapter is organized by problem features, not by method class as in [53], so that users can find the algorithm that suits their problem (p. 2). The features are smooth, non-smooth and noisy objectives; bound and linear constraints; nonlinear constraints; and global, multiobjective and mixed-integer extensions.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design** (what the survey puts forward):
  - **Model-based trust region**:
    - definitions of fully linear and fully quadratic models (p. 3);
    - the geometry debate. [70] (Fasano–Morales–Nocedal) reports good results while ignoring geometry. [123] (Scheinberg–Toint) shows that geometry cannot be ignored entirely, and it uses geometry steps only when the model gradient is small (pp. 3–4);
    - minimum-Frobenius-norm models, ℓ1 (sparse-Hessian) models [30], and RBF models (p. 4).
  - **Direct search**:
    - Nelder–Mead, with a C² strictly convex counterexample for n = 2 [110] (pp. 4–5);
    - polling on positive spanning sets, sufficient decrease with forcing functions, and integer lattices (p. 5);
    - asymptotically dense directions (MADS, RdDS) and Clarke analysis for non-smooth problems (p. 6).
  - **Noise** (p. 7): implicit filtering, regression, interpolation relaxed by the noise level, weighted regression, replication and hypothesis tests.
  - **Worst-case complexity with its n-dependence** (p. 8):
    - direct search: O(ε⁻²) iterations and O(n²ε⁻²) evaluations; DFO trust region is similar;
    - ARC with finite differences: O(n²ε^{−3/2}) evaluations;
    - the factor n² is approximately optimal.
  - **Probabilistic methods** (pp. 8–9): probabilistic models (BSV [31]); probabilistic descent, with O(nmε⁻²) evaluations; STORM [42].
  - **Constraints** (pp. 10–12): relaxable vs unrelaxable constraints, hidden constraints, extreme and progressive barriers, filters, SQP-type trust regions, and trust funnels.
  - **Software**: every class is paired with named codes, for example NEWUOA, BOBYQA, DFO, ORBIT, NOMAD, HOPSPACK, SID-PSM, COBYLA and NOWPAC (pp. 4, 6, 10, 12).
- **D3 Assumptions**: stated only at a general level. O(A) hides only constants that do not depend on the iteration counter, and the dependence on n is made explicit whenever appropriate (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: None. Results are cited, not proved.
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a survey and a software map. The stated limit is that methods get only short descriptions (p. 2). There is no conclusion and no list of open problems; the text ends with multiobjective DFO (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 3–4): geometry is presented as a settled trade-off. It is not free (the counterexample in [123]), but it is needed only when the model gradient is small. This states Heuristic 4 more precisely than "confine them to the final stage": what triggers geometry steps is a small model gradient, i.e. the criticality step (p. 4).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 7–8): weighted regression keeps global convergence when the noise is small relative to the trust-region radius (p. 7). Probabilistic models are fully linear with some probability, conditioned on the past (p. 8). Both have the contract's shape.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 8): complexity is stated separately in iterations and in function evaluations, with the n-dependence explicit, which answers Taste warning 6.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence, stated (p. 8): in DFO, effort must also be counted in function evaluations.
- new-pattern candidate: **organize by the user's problem features, not by method class** (p. 2). This is a writing move for surveys and tutorials.

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a survey or a related-work section, organize by the reader's problem features, and attach a named, available code to every method class (pp. 2, 4–13).
- When you state a DFO complexity result, give iterations and function evaluations separately, and make the n-dependence explicit (pp. 2, 8).
- Present a contested design choice as a pair: the evidence that it can be dropped, and the counterexample showing that it cannot be dropped entirely. Then give the minimal rule (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "In [123] an example was given showing that geometry cannot be totally ignored and that some form of model improvement is necessary" (p. 3)
- "In DFO it becomes also important to measure the effort in terms of the number of function evaluations" (p. 8)

### S074 · A novel l0-constrained gaussian graphical model for anomaly localization (IEEE ICDM Workshops 2017, DOI 10.1109/icdmw.2017.115)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: D. T. Phan, T. Idé and J. Kalagnanam (IBM Watson); M. Menickelly (Lehigh) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a 4-page IBM collaboration on sparse inverse covariance, the topic of S010 and S049. It does not cite them (references, p. 4). It replaces the usual ℓ1 model with an ℓ0 constraint to serve a downstream task. **Read**: full text (pp. 1–4). The ROC curves of Fig. 1 are not in the file; only the caption survives (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Anomaly localization asks for an anomaly score per variable. The scores come from comparing sparse Gaussian graphical models (GGMs) learned on training and test windows (p. 1). Only ℓ1-regularized GGMs have been used for this task, and little is known about the best way to learn the sparse graphs here (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Model (2)**: min tr(SX) − log det X + (λ/2)‖X‖_F² s.t. ‖X‖₀ ≤ κ (p. 2). The ℓ0 constraint fixes the sparsity directly. The Frobenius term keeps entry magnitudes similar, so that a few large entries do not dominate the anomaly scores and hide faulty variables (p. 2).
  - **Algorithm 1**: projected gradient with a Barzilai–Borwein (BB) initial step. It backtracks along the projection arc until two conditions hold: sufficient decrease, f(X_{k+1}) ≤ f(X_k) − (δ/2)‖X_{k+1} − X_k‖², and X_{k+1} ≻ 0 (p. 3).
  - **Projection**: it keeps the κ largest entries, at O(n² log n) cost. Ties are broken by a dictionary order that keeps the result symmetric.
  - **Positive definiteness**: this constraint is open, so the line search enforces it instead of a projection (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - f is smooth and convex, and the feasible set is nonconvex and not closed (p. 3).
  - The tie-breaking rule is introduced for the sake of convergence, yet no convergence result is stated (p. 3).
  - The text does not say how λ and κ were chosen.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: None.
- **D5 Experiments**: One real data set: 42 sensors on prototype cars, with one normal run and two runs that each have two known miswired sensors (p. 3). ### S061 · Stochastic adaptive regularization method with cubics: A high probability complexity bound (Winter Simulation Conference 2023, DOI 10.1109/wsc60868.2023.10408598)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Xie (Cornell ORIE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the conference version of S093 (c09), which adds second-order results and sample complexity. It moves the adaptive regularization with cubics (ARC) method of Cartis–Scheinberg 2018 into the high-probability framework of Jin–Scheinberg–Xie 2021 (S043/D001), using the oracles of Cao–Berahas–Scheinberg (S041) (pp. 2–3). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–11). References (pp. 11–12) were skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: ARC is optimal in the deterministic case: O(ε^{−3/2}) against O(ε^{−2}) for line search and trust region (p. 2). Earlier stochastic ARC work falls short in two ways (p. 2):
  - it gives expected bounds only, and needs an exact or deterministically bounded zeroth-order oracle;
  - or the method is non-adaptive, and its bound holds only on the event that every estimate is accurate. As the paper says, "No expected complexity bound can thus be derived" in that case (p. 2).

  The gap is a high-probability bound for an adaptive ARC with stochastic zeroth-, first- and second-order oracles (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Stochastic ARC (SARC, Algorithm 1, p. 4) has three stochastic ingredients:
  1. g_k and H_k come from the stochastic first- and second-order oracles (SFO, SSO), with accuracy inputs µ/σ_k and √(µ/σ_k) and fixed failure probabilities δ1, δ2 (p. 4);
  2. the acceptance ratio gets a +2ε'_f "error correction" term, because function estimates have irreducible error (pp. 3–4);
  3. σ_k is multiplied by γ after a success (floored at σ_min) and by 1/γ otherwise (p. 4).

  The step conditions (5)–(6) are kept from Cartis–Gould–Toint 2011 (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - **Function**: φ is bounded below and C², with an L-Lipschitz gradient and an L_H-Lipschitz Hessian (p. 1).
  - **Oracles**: the zeroth-order oracle (SZO) has mean error ≤ ε_f and a sub-exponential tail ((1), p. 1). SFO and SSO deliver accuracy κµ with probability ≥ 1 − δ (pp. 1–2).
  - **Admitted strengthening**: SFO and SSO must be implementable for arbitrarily small µ1 and µ2, unlike in JSX21 and CBS22. The authors argue that small inputs occur only with small probability (p. 3).
  - **Assumption 2** (p. 9): ε'_f > ε_f; δ1 + δ2 is small enough that p > 1/2; and ε lies above a floor set by µ and ε'_f.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The proof has two layers.
  - **(a) Deterministic, per realization**:
    - A "true iteration" is defined through max{µ/σ_k, ‖s_k‖²} (Definition 1, p. 4).
    - Lemma 2: a true iteration with σ_k ≥ σ̄ is either successful or has ‖s_k‖² < µ/σ_k (p. 5).
    - Lemma 4: before ε-stationarity is reached, true iterations have ‖s_k‖² ≥ µ/σ_k (p. 7).
    - Lemma 5: the decrease is proportional to ‖∇φ(x_{k+1})‖^{3/2}, minus noise terms (pp. 7–8).
  - **(b) Stochastic**:
    - A union bound gives P(J_k = 1 | F_{k−1}) ≥ 1 − δ1 − δ2 (Lemma 6, pp. 8–9).
    - Theorem 2 checks the five properties required by the JSX21 process (p. 10).
    - Theorem 3 imports its tail bound: P(T_ε ≤ t+1) ≥ 1 − exp(−(p − p̂)²t/(2p²)) − exp(−min{s²t/(8K²), st/(4K)}) (p. 11).

  Consequences (Remark 3, p. 11): O(ε^{−3/2}) with overwhelmingly high probability, matching the deterministic order; finite termination almost surely; and the same order in expectation. The noise floor is ε ≳ ε_f^{2/3}, against √ε_f for step search (SASS) and stochastic trust region (Remark 2, p. 9). A one-sided geometric random walk bounds σ_k, which in turn bounds the required oracle accuracy from below and yields a sample complexity (§5.1, p. 11, citing Jin et al. 2023, i.e. S057).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The paper does not discuss numerical performance, because in practice ARC's efficiency depends on the subproblem solver (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory: the first high-probability bound for stochastic ARC (p. 2).
  - Limits:
    - the stronger oracle assumption (p. 3);
    - relaxed, step-dependent oracle inputs are left open, because s_k depends on the oracle outputs (Remark 1, p. 4);
    - there are no numerical experiments (p. 3);
    - the stopping time is measured by the gradient at the trial point (p. 9).
  - Future work: numerical study (p. 3) and the relaxed inputs (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 1–4, 9): all three oracles are defined by accuracy and reliability, with accuracy tied to 1/σ_k. The irreducible error ε_f fixes the reachable ε (p. 9).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (pp. 3–4): ARC and the Cartis–Gould–Toint 2011 step conditions are unchanged. The repairs are the 2ε'_f slack and the oracle inputs tied to σ_k.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 9–11): a stochastic process, a high-probability tail bound and the deterministic order.
  - Caveat for SKILL.md: the abstract's claim that SARC "is superior to other stochastic adaptive methods" (p. 1) is a claim about complexity order (O(ε^{−3/2}) vs O(ε^{−2}), and the ε_f^{2/3} floor, p. 9). The paper has no experiments (p. 3). SKILL.md's Method 3 evidence line renders this as "outperforms", which should not be read as an empirical result.
- Heuristic 10 ✅ evidence (p. 11): the bound on σ_k is what turns the iteration bound into a sample bound.
- new-pattern candidate: **convergence by embedding in an existing framework** (s2-02), in stochastic form. Prove per-realization lemmas, check the listed properties of an abstract process (Theorem 2), then import that process's tail bound (pp. 8, 10–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Suppose an accuracy requirement depends on the step, which is unknown before the oracle is called. Define the "true iteration" with max{computable input, step-dependent term}, and pass the computable input to the oracle (Definition 1 and Remark 1, p. 4).
- When function estimates carry irreducible error, add a 2ε'_f slack to the numerator of the acceptance ratio (pp. 3–4).
- State the noise floor as a power of the oracle's irreducible error, and compare that exponent with sibling methods (Remark 2, p. 9).
- Organise the proof as deterministic per-realization lemmas, followed by a checklist of the process properties that a published stochastic theorem requires (pp. 4–10).

**Quotes**:
- "We do not discuss the numerical performance of our method in this paper." (p. 3)
- "This is because function value estimates have an irreducible error, so without this correction term, the algorithm may always reject improvement steps." (p. 3)

### S137 · High Probability Step Size Lower Bound for Adaptive Stochastic Optimization (OPT 2021 workshop; the year is from the header, p. 1; no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: B. Jin and M. Xie (Cornell, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a 10-page workshop paper that isolates the lower bound on the step size. S057 (c07, Math. Program. 2025) later builds its sample-complexity analysis on the same random-walk coupling. S061 cites S057 for its bound on σ_k (S061 p. 11). **Read**: full text, including Appendices A–D (pp. 1–10).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Adaptive stochastic methods (line search, trust region, ARC) tie oracle accuracy to the step size α_k (pp. 1, 3).
  - A lower bound on α is easy to derive deterministically. With oracle failures, no such bound had been derived (p. 1).
  - The bound matters because the number of samples per oracle call grows as α shrinks (p. 3).
  - The paper presents itself as resolving "one of the open problems" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: There is no new algorithm. Algorithm 1 is an abstract framework (p. 2): a model is built from j-th order oracles with accuracy M_j and reliability 1 − δ_j. On success α ← min{α/γ, α_max}; on failure α ← γα. The key idea: log_{1/γ}(ᾱ/α_k) is an integer walk that steps down on each success. It is coupled with a one-sided random walk that is reflected at 0 (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - Assumption 1 (p. 4) has two parts: (i) P(true | past) ≥ p > 1/2; (ii) true iterations with α_k ≤ ᾱ are successful.
  - The algorithms do not know the Lipschitz constants (pp. 1–2).
  - The cited algorithms [1–6] satisfy Assumption 1 (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - **Theorem 1** (p. 4): for n < T_ε and ν > 0, with probability at least 1 − n^{−ν} − c·n^{−(1+ν)/2}, the minimum step min_{k≤n} α_k is at least ᾱγ·n^{−(1+ν)·log_{p/(1−p)}(1/γ)}.
  - **Proof devices**:
    - a coupling proof of stochastic dominance (Lemma 3, Appendix A, p. 8);
    - the probability that level ℓ is reached within n steps, from the explicit spectral form of a truncated birth–death chain (Proposition 1, Appendix B, p. 8). The acknowledgments credit James Allen Fill for references and for this proof (p. 7);
    - the choice ℓ = ⌈log_a n⌉ with a = (p/q)^{1/(1+ν)} (Appendix C, p. 9).
  - **Tightness**: the bound is essentially tight, because q^ℓ is a lower bound on the hitting probability (Remark 5, p. 5).
  - **Consequences** (Remark 2, pp. 4–5):
    - as p → 1 the bound recovers the deterministic ᾱγ;
    - lower reliability calls for a larger γ;
    - γ = (p/(1−p))^{−1/4} gives ᾱγ·n^{−1/2}, the usual SGD decay.
- **D5 Experiments**: MNIST with an MLP (784-512-256-10) and a small CNN, comparing step-size trajectories of ALOE (γ = 0.7 and 0.9) with SLS, which violates Assumption 1 (Fig. 1, p. 6). After the same number of iterations on the CNN, ALOE reaches loss ≈ 0.3 and accuracy > 99%, against 1.4 and ≈ 49% for SLS; on the MLP both reach about 98% (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a theoretical lemma for a whole class of methods (p. 1).
  - There is no conclusion or future-work section, and limits are not discussed.
  - The numerical comparison is at equal iterations, not equal samples (p. 6).
  - Appendix B cites "[1, Section XVI.3]" for the diagonalization, but [1] in the reference list is Berahas–Cao–Scheinberg. The intended source is not in the list (pp. 7–8).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 4–5): the step parameter is treated as a random walk; the bound holds with high probability; and the deterministic bound is the limit p → 1.
- Heuristic 10 ✅ evidence (p. 3): lower-bound α with high probability, then turn it into a bound on samples per oracle call. In this corpus it is the earliest statement of the step that leads to S057.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (p. 3): accuracy M_j is tied to α_k (for trust region, M0 = κα² and M1 = κα), and reliability δ is fixed below 1/2. Accuracy is achieved through the batch size.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence, stated (p. 1): adaptive methods borrow from deterministic optimization, and they avoid the per-application step-size tuning that SGD needs (Taste warning 1). The SGD n^{−1/2} schedule reappears as one particular choice of γ (p. 5).
- new-pattern candidate: **spin off the enabling lemma** (s01). The step-size bound that sample complexity needs is published on its own as a workshop paper (pp. 1, 3).
- new-pattern candidate: **analysis-derived parameter rule**. The bound says how to set γ as a function of the oracle reliability p (Remark 2, pp. 4–5; S057 makes the same move).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To lower-bound an adaptive parameter that moves by a constant factor, map its logarithm to an integer walk. Couple that walk with a reflected walk that dominates it, and bound the reflected walk's maximum over n steps (pp. 4–5, 8–9).
- Take the level ℓ = ⌈log_a n⌉ with a slightly below the drift ratio p/q. This turns a geometric hitting probability into a failure probability that is polynomial in n (p. 9).
- Include a competitor that violates your assumption (here SLS), to show what the assumption buys in practice (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "In the deterministic setting, a lower bound on the step size is easily derived, however, in the stochastic setting, due to possible oracle failures, bounds on the step size have not been previously derived." (p. 1)
- "Providing a lower bound on the step size parameter in turn gives an upper bound on the number of samples needed for each oracle evaluation." (p. 3)
