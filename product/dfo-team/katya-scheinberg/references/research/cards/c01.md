### S004 · Recent progress in unconstrained nonlinear optimization without derivatives (Mathematical Programming 1997, DOI 10.1007/bf02614326)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The founding survey of the DFO line. Scheinberg's affiliation is Columbia IEOR, when she was a PhD student (p. 2). It was an invited semi-plenary at ISMP97 (p. 2) and is the Namur Report 97/12 preprint version. **Read**: full text. The reference list was skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is practitioner demand. f is expensive, and derivatives are unavailable because f comes from physical measurement or from proprietary simulation code (p. 3). The paper rules out the alternatives one by one (p. 3–4):
  - direct search (Nelder–Mead, PDS) does not exploit smoothness and uses many evaluations;
  - automatic differentiation needs callable source code;
  - finite differences with quasi-Newton updates are "useful and sometimes surprisingly efficient", but they spend function values on derivatives.

  Powell's secant-vs-Newton argument (rate per function value ≈1.618 vs √2) supplies the reason to model f itself rather than its derivatives (p. 4). The problem is stated as min f(x), smooth, unconstrained, with no ∇f (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: This is an algorithm *class*, not a single algorithm (p. 4–5). It is an interpolation-model trust-region method with three ingredients (p. 7–9):
  1. a quadratic model fitted by interpolation on a set Y, where |Y| = (n+1)(n+2)/2 for full quadratics (p. 7);
  2. a rule for inserting the accepted trial point and dropping a point from Y (p. 8);
  3. a geometry-improvement step, used when progress stalls and Y is "inadequate" (p. 9).

  The radius is reduced only if Y *was adequate* when the step was computed. Otherwise it is held (Step 4, p. 9–10). A separate Step 5 moves to the best interpolation point if it gives enough decrease (p. 10). Two realizations of "adequate geometry" are given:
  - Powell's Lagrange-polynomial approach: the determinant ratio equals |L(y+, y−)|, and a scaled criterion S(y, y−) handles far points (§4, p. 11–12);
  - the authors' Newton-fundamental-polynomial approach, which uses pivot thresholds θ in procedure CNP (§5, p. 12–16).

  The interpolating model is unique for poised Y. The two approaches therefore differ only in the procedures for improving and updating geometry (p. 13). Kept from classical trust-region methods: the ratio test and radius logic (p. 7). Changed: the model comes from interpolation rather than from derivatives (p. 7), and the radius decision now also depends on model quality (p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is smooth, derivatives cannot be computed, and there are no constraints (p. 3). The cost of f dominates all other costs, which justifies solving 2(p−1) constrained quadratic maximizations to check geometry (p. 12). Poisedness is required for existence and uniqueness of the interpolant; six points on a line or on a circle in R² show why (p. 7–8). The Newton approach needs sufficiently large pivots ("well-poisedness"), and the constant κ0 is independent of x_k and Δ_k (Theorem 1, p. 15). No assumptions are flagged as conveniences for the proof.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No new proofs. The convergence theory is deferred to Conn–Scheinberg–Toint 1997 ("Approximation Theory and Optimization", Powell tribute) (p. 13, p. 16). The devices stated:
  - Poisedness as a nonzero interpolation determinant δ(Y) (3.5) (p. 8).
  - Powell's identity: replacing y− by y+ multiplies |δ(Y)| by |L(y+, y−)|, and the factor does not depend on the basis (p. 11).
  - The Sauer–Xu path-sum error bound (5.4): the interpolation error is controlled by products of Newton-polynomial values along paths through the blocks (p. 15).
  - Theorem 1: a well-poised set with |N_j^[ℓ](x)| ≤ κ0 on B_k can always be constructed (p. 15).
  - *Adequate* is defined as all points in B_k plus bounds (5.8)–(5.9) with κ1 > κ0 (p. 15–16).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The paper points to preliminary numerics in Conn–Toint 1996 and Powell 1996 (p. 6, p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: History runs from Powell 1964 (conjugate directions) and Winfield 1969/1973. Winfield's interpolation-plus-trust-region idea is called a very early statement of a trust-region method that "went mostly unnoticed" (p. 5–6). Powell 1994b is credited with the crucial difference: updating Y so that geometry is preserved (p. 6). The authors place their Newton approach next to Powell's Lagrange approach, not against it: Lagrange is "very elegant, if also potentially expensive", while Newton gives a framework where global convergence can be proved (p. 17). Direct search is treated fairly: it needs "little additional effort from the user" (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A survey voice with explicit hedges ("hopefully", p. 4, p. 7). The paper admits its own abstraction: "Our outline is admittedly broad and simplistic" (p. 10). An organization paragraph comes early (p. 5). The main thesis appears first in the abstract ("geometrical quality", p. 3) and is restated in §6: geometry is "the main feature that distinguish modern methods" from Winfield's (p. 17). There are no theorems of its own; intuition dominates.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Conceptual framing plus an algorithm class. The open problems are concrete (p. 17–18):
  - the trade-off between "best possible geometry" and linear-algebra cost;
  - initial models when |Y| is below full size;
  - the theoretical meaning of pivot values;
  - larger n through structure: drop monomials x_i x_j where the Hessian is known to be sparse, or exploit partial separability;
  - noise: replace interpolation by approximation and combine geometry with "sufficient sampling";
  - the need for high-quality software.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. Geometry is presented as the defining ingredient of modern model-based DFO, and its cost is already posed as a trade-off to be minimized (p. 8–9, p. 17). This is the 1997 origin of the *pay only the minimum* framing.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence for the **stated** side, which SKILL.md says is missing. The 1997 co-authored discussion explicitly names structure exploitation (Hessian sparsity pattern, partial separability, one interpolation model per element) as the route to larger n (p. 17).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (deterministic precursor). For noisy f, the paper foresees that geometry conditions must be "combined with that of sufficient sampling" (p. 17). This is the model-accuracy-vs-sampling idea without a probability.
- Method 5 ✅ weak evidence. Methods are compared by convergence rate *per function value* (Powell's secant argument, p. 4). This is the evaluation-count criterion in embryo.
- New-pattern candidate: *abstract algorithm class with pluggable quality predicates*. The algorithm is stated with undefined predicates (*adequate*, *improve geometry*), and concrete realizations are plugged in later (p. 8, p. 10–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Open with a table-like walk through the practitioner's alternatives and give each one's failure mode for your cost model (p. 3–4). The reader then arrives at your method already convinced.
- [D1/D5] Justify a design choice with a rate-per-function-evaluation calculation (secant 1.618 vs Newton-with-FD √2, p. 4). It is a one-paragraph, checkable argument.
- [D3] Show why a condition is needed with the smallest degenerate configuration (six points on a circle cannot determine a 2-D quadratic, p. 7–8).
- [D2] Write the algorithm with abstract predicates (*Y is adequate in B_k*) and prove things at that level. Put the concrete measures in separate sections so that alternatives can be swapped in (p. 9–10, §4–§5).

**Quotes**:
- "In this case, one expects that interpolation will be replaced by approximation in the model’s definition, and the conditions on geometry will have then to be combined with that of sufficient sampling." (p. 17)
- "thinks of exploiting any structure present in the problem as efficiently as possible." (p. 17)

### S020 · IBM Research TRECVID-2006 Video Retrieval System (TRECVID 2006, no DOI)
**Role**: core (outlier) · **Coauthors**: M. Campbell, A. Haubold, S. Ebadollahi, D. Joshi, M. R. Naphade, A. Natsev, J. Seidl, J. R. Smith, J. Tešić, L. Xie (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Scheinberg's IBM T. J. Watson period (affiliation p. 1). This is a team benchmark-system notebook paper. The paper does not attribute sections to authors, so Scheinberg's specific contribution cannot be identified from the text. The only optimization-formulation content is the multiple-kernel-learning (MKL) subsection (§3.2, p. 4–5). **Read**: full text. Figures 4 and 6–13 are images and are absent from the text file.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is set by the benchmark: the TRECVID high-level feature detection, search and rushes tasks (p. 1). This year's system focus was "ensemble learning and fusion" (p. 1). Entry for MKL (p. 4–5): early fusion (averaging kernels) and late fusion (averaging SVMs) ignore the relative importance of kernels, and late fusion costs k trainings and a larger model.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The system combines:
  - many SVMs trained on different random partitions and on several automatic interpretations of multi-annotator ground truth (p. 3–4);
  - MKL with learned kernel weights μ_j and shared support-vector weights, used for 4 concepts (p. 4–6);
  - grid-searched score normalization and aggregation, in flat or hierarchical fusion (p. 5–6);
  - query-class-dependent fusion weights learned by exhaustive search on 24 TRECVID-2005 training queries (p. 13–14);
  - clustering-based pseudo-negative and pseudo-positive sampling for content-based search (p. 10–12).

  The MKL dual (4) is a second-order-cone program solved with SeDuMi (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: SVM class imbalance is kept at N/P < 10, following [AKJ04] (p. 11). Pseudo-relevance feedback assumes the top-ranked documents are relevant (p. 9). The MKL trace normalization fixes tr(Σμ_j K_j) = tr(ΣK_j) (p. 5). None of these is analysed.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: none. The only mathematical object is the SOCP reformulation of the MKL dual, taken from Bach–Lanckriet–Jordan (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: TRECVID-2006 official evaluation, measured by mean inferred AP and precision@100 (p. 6–7), with MAP for search (p. 14). Six feature-detection runs are reported (Table 2, p. 7). Fusion over views, models and methods gives about 22% over the visual baseline (p. 1, p. 7). Query-dependent fusion gives 13–14% over query-independent fusion, and the top runs reach MAP 0.0855/0.0867 (p. 13–14). Negative and failure reports are included:
  - context fusion did not help Waterscape (p. 7);
  - the MKL concepts "were not among the twenty concepts evaluated" (p. 7);
  - NIST's precision@100 appeared to be half the internal value (p. 7);
  - the interactive-run cut-off was set too high, so a third of the data was never ingested (p. 18).

  Fusion weights for non-differentiable metrics are found by "exhaustive search on a few dimensions, or heuristic search with restart on a few dozen dimensions" (p. 13). This is a black-box tuning problem solved without any DFO method.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on the team's own earlier TRECVID systems ([AAC+05], [NNT05], p. 7, p. 10). MKL is attributed to Bach et al. (p. 5). Critique is limited to named drawbacks of early and late fusion (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: An engineering report. Gains are given as relative percentages (p. 1, p. 7, p. 14). Some wording is loose for empirical results ("we have proved that the sophisticated approach to modeling of the training samples improves the visual search", p. 12). The conclusion calls the results "preliminary" and defers details to the workshop (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is at the system and application level. The stated future work is context-based modelling of negative samples and up-sampling positives (p. 12), and the paper defers further details and performance analysis to the workshop (p. 18). The MKL component remained unevaluated (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ⚠ variant (weak). The multi-kernel structure is modelled directly (learned μ_j over kernels, p. 4–5), but it is solved with a generic conic solver (SeDuMi). There is no structure-specific algorithm, which contrasts with S010 and S005.
- No link to Methods 1–5. This card mainly serves as trajectory evidence for the IBM applied-ML exposure recorded in SKILL.md *Research Trajectory* (≈1997–≈2010 row).
- New-pattern candidate: none. The paper is not written in Scheinberg's research voice.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D5] Report operational failures and evaluation discrepancies next to the scores: the cut-off error and the precision@100 mismatch (p. 7, p. 18). This keeps a benchmark report honest.
- [D2] Replace early and late fusion with a single learned-weight combination that has an interpretable weight vector and about the same number of parameters as early fusion (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "This problem can in turn be converted into a standard form of second-order-cone programming, and we obtain its solutions with the convex solver Sedumi [Stu99]." (p. 5)
- "but the four concepts for which we used this idea were not among the twenty concepts evaluated this year." (p. 7)

### S010 · Sparse inverse covariance selection via alternating linearization methods (NIPS 2010, arXiv:1011.0097)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Ma, D. Goldfarb · **Position in trajectory**: Written just after the move to Lehigh (affiliation p. 1). It is the ML-venue application paper of the general ALM method in S005, which it cites as the Columbia technical report [25] (p. 9). It shares S005's key lemma verbatim in structure (Lemma 5.1, p. 10 = S005 Lemma 2.2). **Read**: full text including the appendix (p. 10–11).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Sparse inverse covariance selection (SICS): minimize −log det X + ⟨Σ̂, X⟩ + ρ‖X‖₁ (p. 1). It comes from Gaussian graphical model structure learning (p. 1). The entry is a survey of competitors, each assigned a precise deficit (p. 2):
  - interior-point methods cost too much per iteration;
  - BCD, glasso, SINCO and the projected-gradient method (PSM) "lack iteration complexity bounds";
  - Nesterov-smoothing variants have bounds but perform poorly in practice;
  - Lu's VSM (a practical variant of Lu's smooth method) has no known complexity;
  - Yuan's ADAL-based method has no bound.

  The authors solve the primal because the ℓ1 term there "directly promotes sparsity" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Symmetric ADAL updates the multiplier after both subproblems, which makes λ equal to the gradient. It can therefore be read as *alternating linearization*: minimize f plus a linearized-and-proximal g, then the reverse (Algorithm 1, p. 3). For nonsmooth g, a **skipping step** is added: if F(x^{k+1}) exceeds the model value, set x^{k+1} := y^k (Algorithm 2, p. 4). For SICS (Algorithm 3, p. 5), the subproblems are closed-form:
  - the X-step is one eigendecomposition with γ_i = (d_i + √(d_i² + 4μ))/2, the same O(n³) cost as a gradient (p. 5);
  - the Y-step is an entrywise shrinkage, O(n²) (p. 5–6).

  The constraint X ⪰ (α/2)I is imposed in the X-step (cheap) but dropped in the Y-step, because imposing it degrades performance substantially in practice (p. 6). A learning interpretation follows: each step is a MAP estimate with a Gaussian prior centred at the other block's gradient-step point (p. 6). Kept: the augmented-Lagrangian splitting framework. Changed: the multiplier update, a skip safeguard, and a continuation schedule on μ (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The theory (Theorem 2.1) needs ∇f Lipschitz with constant L(f) and β/L(f) ≤ μ ≤ 1/L(f) (p. 4). SICS violates this, since neither term has a Lipschitz gradient and f is defined only on PD matrices (p. 4). The fix borrows Lu's bound X* ⪰ αI and restricts to C = {X ⪰ (α/2)I} (p. 5). The authors admit the gap: L ≤ 1/α² makes the theoretical μ impractically small, so the rate holds "only a small neighborhood of the optimal solution" (p. 5). In practice "this value is too small" (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Lemma 5.1 generalizes Beck–Teboulle's Lemma 2.3 to a prox step built on a linearization of the *other* function. If Φ(p(v)) ≤ Q(p(v), v), then 2μ(Φ(u) − Φ(p(v))) ≥ ‖p(v) − u‖² − ‖v − u‖² (p. 10). The proof of Theorem 2.1 (p. 10–11):
  - applies the lemma twice per iteration (u = x*) and sums;
  - handles skipped iterations with the y-inequality alone;
  - telescopes ‖y^n − x*‖²;
  - applies the lemma again with u = x^{n+1} and u = y^n to show F(x^n) and F(y^n) are non-increasing.

  Result: F(y^k) − F* ≤ ‖x⁰ − x*‖²/(2μ(k + k_n)), where k_n counts non-skipping iterations (p. 4). This is O(1/ε), with a bonus for every non-skipped step. An accelerated O(1/√ε) version is possible, but the authors do not present it because it did worse on SICS (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: MATLAB on a named workstation (p. 6). Baselines are PSM (Schmidt's code) and VSM, at default parameters (p. 7). Comparing *iterations* is justified by equal per-iteration cost, and CPU time is reported too (p. 7). All methods stop at duality gap ≤ 1e−3. ALM's gap is checked only every N_gap = 20 iterations because it needs an extra eigendecomposition (p. 7).
  - Synthetic data: the Scheinberg–Rish generator, n = 200–2000, ρ ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1} (Table 1, p. 7).
  - Real data: five gene-expression sets from Li–Toh with ρ = 0.5 (Table 2, p. 8).
  - Fairness check: sparsity patterns are compared using complementary slackness for the dual methods, whose inverted X is never exactly sparse (Table 3, p. 8).
  - All ALM parameter values and the μ₀ rule, which depends on ρ, are listed (p. 7).

  Not remarked on in the text: ALM's reported Dgap at n = 1500, ρ = 0.1 is 1.73e−3, above the 1e−3 target (Table 1, p. 7). No failure cases are discussed.
- **D6 Positioning**: The closest rival is acknowledged head-on: "Although developed independently, our method is closely related to Yuan’s method". The difference is framed as complexity plus interpretation (p. 2). Baselines are chosen transitively: PSM and VSM were shown to beat BCD and glasso, so glasso is not rerun (p. 2). The authors' own SINCO is listed among the methods without bounds (p. 2). Criticism is factual ("very slow and did not produce good results" for one implementation, p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: An explicit "Our contribution." paragraph and an organization paragraph (p. 2). A learning-intuition paragraph is written for the NIPS audience (p. 6). The theory claim is carefully hedged (p. 5). There is **no conclusion section**: the paper ends with the sparsity table and acknowledgements (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Algorithm, complexity bound, and a practical code that beats the state of the art (p. 2, p. 7–8). Stated limits: the theory holds only near the solution (p. 5); the Y iterates may be non-PD until late (p. 6); acceleration did not help (p. 4). No future directions are given.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. Both subproblems are closed-form (an eigendecomposition and a shrinkage) at gradient cost (p. 5–6). SKILL.md Heuristic 9 is confirmed.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. The classical ADAL/augmented-Lagrangian scheme is kept and reinterpreted, and the one added safeguard (skipping) triggers exactly where smoothness fails (p. 3–4). Here the *classical method* is a convex splitting method, not an adaptive stochastic one.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (deterministic analogue). The value criterion is *has an iteration complexity bound*. Rivals are faulted precisely for lacking one (p. 2), and the bound matches ISTA's O(1/ε) order (p. 4).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Iteration counts are used only after arguing equal per-iteration cost. The unequal cost of ALM's duality-gap check is disclosed (p. 7). Sparsity is compared by a fair device (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: *safeguard test with fallback to the analysable step*. If the step fails a sufficient-decrease/majorization test, fall back to the step the theory covers (skipping, p. 4). The same device appears in S005.
- New-pattern candidate: *state the theory–practice parameter gap*. The theoretical step is too small, so a continuation rule is used, and the paper says so (p. 5–7). The same move appears in S005.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Build the related-work paragraph as a deficit ledger, giving each competitor one precise missing property (no bound / slow / reformulation blows up size). Then the contribution sentence fills exactly the missing cell (p. 2).
- [D2] If your splitting has a multiplier, check whether a symmetric update makes it equal to a gradient. If it does, the method is a linearization method and prox-gradient proofs transfer (p. 3).
- [D4] To handle nonsmooth g, add a one-line test (*did the linearized model upper-bound F?*) and fall back to the provable step when it fails. Count the good steps k_n explicitly in the bound (p. 4, p. 10–11).
- [D5] When a dual method's primal is never exactly sparse, read its support off complementary slackness (|W_ij − Σ̂_ij| = ρ) instead of thresholding (p. 8).
- [D5] State why iteration counts are a fair metric (equal per-iteration cost), and disclose any metric-computation cost you avoid (gap checked every 20 iterations) (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "All of these BCD and CD approaches lack iteration complexity bounds." (p. 2)
- "Hence, for a practical approach we can only claim that the theoretical convergence rate bound holds in only a small neighborhood of the optimal solution." (p. 5)

### S030 · Self-correcting geometry in model-based algorithms for derivative-free unconstrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2010, DOI 10.1137/090748536)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The last deterministic DFO theory paper before the probabilistic-model pivot (BSV 2014). It was written at IBM (affiliation p. 2) as Namur Report 09/06, Feb 2009. It bridges the 1997 geometry agenda (S004) and the later *minimal safeguard / randomize geometry* stance. **Read**: full text. Figures 1–2 are garbled axis residue in the text file, but their captions are readable (p. 10–11). References were skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an **unexplained empirical anomaly**. Fasano, Nocedal and Morales observed that an algorithm ignoring geometry "may in fact perform quite well in practice" (p. 4; also the abstract, p. 3). The question posed: are the expensive geometry-improving steps "truly necessary" for global convergence (p. 4)? The acknowledgement credits Nocedal with raising the issue (p. 18).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: First, a *negative* result: ignoring geometry can converge to a non-stationary point (§3, p. 8–11). Then Algorithm 2 (p. 12), which drops geometry-improving iterations from the main loop.
  - An unsuccessful trial point x⁺ is *reused* to repair geometry. It replaces a far point (set F_k: distance > βΔ_k, ℓ_j(x⁺) ≠ 0; Step 4b). If there is none, it replaces a close point with |ℓ_j(x⁺)| > Λ (set C_k; Step 4c). The radius is not reduced in either case.
  - The radius shrinks only when F_k ∪ C_k = ∅ (Step 4d, p. 12).
  - A Λ-poised model is built *only* in the criticality test (Step 1), in a ball of radius ε_{i+1} = μ‖∇m̂_i‖ (p. 12).
  - The replacement choice weighs distance² × |ℓ_j(x⁺)| (4.1)–(4.3) (p. 12).

  Kept: the trust-region framework, the Cauchy condition (2.7), and the feature of DFO and NEWUOA that the trial point may be included to improve geometry (p. 4, p. 7–8). Changed: the separate geometry iterations are gone, and Lagrange polynomials are monitored instead (p. 18).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: A1: f is C¹ with Lipschitz gradient (constant L/2). A2: f is bounded below. A3: 1 + ‖H_k‖ ≤ κ_H (p. 13). Models are of degree ≥ 1 for all large k (Lemmas 5.2–5.5, Theorem 5.8, p. 14–17). The examples assume infinite precision, with a remark that finite precision leads to the same failure near degeneracy (p. 9). Example 1 uses f with a discontinuous Hessian but a Lipschitz gradient, and notes that a C² version exists (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - (i) **Counterexamples by hand-computable 2-D runs.** In Example 1, the set drifts onto x₂ = 0, the quadratic model degenerates to x₁², and the method stops at (0, 0), which is non-stationary (p. 8–9). In Example 2, a linear model loses the x₂ direction because the exiting point is chosen by distance only (p. 9–11).
  - (ii) **Volume potential** (Lemma 2.4, p. 6). |ℓ_j(x)| equals the ratio of simplex volumes of Φ(Y) after and before the swap (2.5). Each swap with |ℓ_j| ≥ Λ > 1 multiplies a volume that is bounded above, so only finitely many swaps are possible.
  - (iii) **Partition-of-unity counting lemma** (Lemma 5.1, p. 13). If Σ|α_i| > 2Σα_i, then the second-largest |α_j| is ≥ (σ_abs − 2σ)/(2p).
  - (iv) **Self-correction** (Lemma 5.2, p. 14–15). Suppose the iteration is unsuccessful, F_k = ∅ and Δ_k ≤ κ_Λ‖g_k‖. Failure gives |f(x⁺) − m(x⁺)| > (1−η)·(model decrease). Bounding the interpolation error by Σ‖y_j − x⁺‖²|ℓ_j(x⁺)|, together with Cauchy decrease, gives Σ|ℓ_j(x⁺)| ≥ 2(pΛ + 1). Since Σℓ_j = 1, a non-maximal |ℓ_j(x⁺)| ≥ Λ exists, so C_k ≠ ∅.
  - (v) The rest is the standard trust-region skeleton. The radius is bounded below away from criticality (Lemma 5.3). Finitely many successes lead to a contradiction with (ii) (Lemma 5.4). Infinitely many successes make f unbounded below (Lemma 5.5). Conn–Gould–Toint Theorems 8.4.2/8.4.3 are reused (Lemmas 5.6–5.7).
  - Theorem 5.8 gives liminf ‖∇f(x_k)‖ = 0 by driving ε_i → 0 (p. 17–18). Constants are explicit: κ_Λ = min{1/κ_H, (1−η)κ_C/(2κ_ef(β+1)²(pΛ+1))} (p. 14). There is no rate and no complexity bound.
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The figures only illustrate the counterexamples (p. 10–11). The mechanism is offered as an *explanation* of Fasano et al.'s numerics, and the paper says "This remains to be verified in details" (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper extends the authors' own earlier work "at variance with" Conn–Scheinberg–Toint 1997a and Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente 2008b (p. 11). It stands in a double relation to Fasano et al.: it shows their method's convergence "cannot be ensured", and it also explains why it works (p. 18). The new algorithm's features are aligned with the practical codes DFO and NEWUOA (p. 4). The literature list is broad and non-partisan (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution is announced as a two-step "We will first show" (impossibility) followed by "but we will also provide an algorithm" (p. 4). The objective is stated modestly: understanding, "rather than to suggest a new practical optimization scheme" (p. 4). After the key Lemma 5.2 comes a plain-language paragraph explaining what it means and which constants drive it (p. 15). The discussion restates the mechanism and hedges the explanatory claim ("one might expect", p. 18). The theorem-to-intuition ratio is balanced.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Theory: necessity (counterexamples) plus sufficiency (algorithm and proof). The saving is function evaluations and Lagrange maximizations; the linear algebra for Lagrange polynomials remains (p. 18). Stated future work: second-order criticality, unbounded Hessians, convex constraints via projections, noisy objectives (p. 18). Unstated limits: no complexity rate, and no numerical test of the new algorithm.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ strong evidence. The paper establishes the minimum geometry needed: geometry cannot be dropped (p. 8–11), but dedicated geometry evaluations can be confined to the criticality stage while points generated by the algorithm self-correct the set (p. 12, p. 14–15). This confirms SKILL.md Heuristic 4 and Research Taste mark 5.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The trust-region method and its standard lemmas are kept (p. 16–17), and only the point-replacement and radius-update rule is changed (p. 12).
- Nuance for SKILL.md *Inner Tensions*: the *disciplines and vindicates practice* tension is already present *inside this 2010 paper*. It refutes the geometry-free method's guarantee and explains its practical success (p. 18). The tension is not only visible across 2010 vs 2025–26.
- New-pattern candidate: *explain the practitioners' anomaly*. Take an observation that a safeguard seems unnecessary. Prove by counterexample that it is necessary in the worst case. Then find the mechanism that makes it rarely needed, and design the algorithm around that mechanism (p. 4, p. 8–15, p. 18).
- Proof-device candidate for the proof playbook: *volume (determinant) potential for finite termination of point exchanges* (p. 6) and *partition of unity + large ℓ1 mass ⇒ a large non-maximal component* (p. 13–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Turn a colleague's surprising empirical observation into a necessity/sufficiency question: *is X truly necessary for guarantee G?* (p. 4).
- [D4] Prove necessity with a 2-D example whose iterates you can compute by hand, and draw the iterates (p. 8–11). Reviewers can check it in minutes.
- [D4] For finite termination of a swap procedure, find a bounded quantity that each swap multiplies by a factor > 1. The determinant/volume works for interpolation sets (Lemma 2.4, p. 6).
- [D4] When a failed step gives you a large residual, use identities of the basis (Σℓ_j = 1) to turn the residual into a *usable* improving move (Lemma 5.2, p. 14–15).

### S005 · Fast alternating linearization methods for minimizing the sum of two convex functions (Mathematical Programming 2013, DOI 10.1007/s10107-012-0530-2; arXiv:0912.4571)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: D. Goldfarb, S. Ma · **Position in trajectory**: The methods paper behind S010, with Scheinberg at Lehigh (p. 1). Read from arXiv v2 (13 Oct 2010), not the journal version, so page numbers are arXiv pages. It is the deepest excursion into accelerated first-order complexity. The Nesterov/FISTA acceleration machinery here is absent from her later adaptive stochastic work (cf. SKILL.md *Inner Tensions*: acceleration is a gap in the lens). **Read**: full text. References were skimmed. For the proof of Lemma 3.3 in the regular-first case, the authors write "we leave this to the reader" (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: min f(x) + g(x), where both prox problems (1.2)–(1.3) are easy, ideally at the cost of one gradient (p. 1). Four motivating examples: compressed sensing, nuclear-norm minimization, RPCA and SICS (p. 1–3). Gap 1: ADMs have global convergence results but no iteration-complexity bounds (p. 4). Gap 2: ISTA/FISTA treat f and g asymmetrically (they always linearize f). They "do not work well" for RPCA, where both terms are complicated, and cannot be used for SICS, where iterates may be non-PD (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Symmetric ADAL (SADAL) updates λ after both subproblems. When both functions are smooth, λ equals a gradient, so SADAL = ALM, which alternately keeps one function, linearizes the other and adds a prox term (Algorithms 2–3, p. 6–7). The variants:
  - ALM-S adds the skipping test: if F(x^{k+1}) > L_μ(x^{k+1}, y^k; λ^k), set x^{k+1} := y^k, which reduces to an ISTA step (Algorithm 4, p. 7–8).
  - Algorithm 5 is a cheaper equivalent that switches to SADAL when no skip occurs (p. 7).
  - FALM and FALM-S add FISTA extrapolation on y. FALM-S modifies the t_k recursion according to whether the current and previous steps were skipping or regular (√(1+8t²), √(1+2t²) variants; Algorithms 6–7, p. 12).
  - Gauss–Seidel vs the Jacobi-type multiple splitting of Goldfarb–Ma (p. 5).

  Kept: the ADAL framework and FISTA's momentum. Changed: symmetric multiplier updates, the skip test, and the adjusted t_k rules.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: ALM-S/FALM-S need only ∇f Lipschitz and μ ≤ 1/L(f). ALM/FALM need both gradients Lipschitz and μ ≤ 1/max L (p. 9, p. 11, p. 15–16). Unknown L can be handled by Beck–Teboulle backtracking (Remarks 2.8, 3.8, p. 11, p. 16). Convex constraints are allowed with a one-line change in the lemma (Remark 2.7, p. 11). No bound is given for SADAL when both terms are nonsmooth (p. 7). Nonsmooth terms can instead be handled by Nesterov smoothing with σ = ε/(nρ²), at the price of O(1/ε²) for ALM and O(1/ε) for FALM (Theorem 4.1, p. 17–18; Theorem 5.1, p. 20–21).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Lemma 2.2 is the *three-point* inequality generalizing Beck–Teboulle Lemma 2.3 (p. 8–9).
  - Theorem 2.3 uses the same telescoping-plus-monotonicity skeleton as S010, giving ‖x⁰ − x*‖²/(2μ(k + k_n)) (p. 9–11).
  - Corollary 2.4: when no skips occur (k_n = k), the bound is ‖x⁰ − x*‖²/(4μk), **a factor 2 better than ISTA** (Remark 2.5, p. 11).
  - For FALM-S, Lemma 3.2 proves the potential 2μt_k²v_k + ‖u^k‖² is non-increasing. The weight is **mode-dependent**: v_k = 2(F − F*) on regular steps and (F − F*) on skipping steps. The proof covers all four regular/skipping transitions, and the t_k recursions are chosen so that the weights match: t_k² = t_{k+1}(t_{k+1} − 1), 2t_k² = …, t_k²/2 = … (p. 12–14).
  - Lemma 3.3 lower-bounds t_k by induction over blocks of consecutive regular/skipping steps (p. 14–15).
  - Theorem 3.4 gives F(y^k) − F* ≤ 2‖x⁰ − x*‖²/(μ(k + 1 + α r̂(k))²), and Corollary 3.5 is **√2 better than FISTA** (p. 15–16).
  - Theorem 5.2 is a small exact-reformulation lemma for RPCA with missing data (p. 22).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - (a) Wavelet image deblurring (Cameraman 256², 9×9 blur, Gaussian noise, Haar with 4 levels) against ISTA, FISTA, SALSA and SADAL, all at downloaded defaults, 1000 iterations each (p. 16–18). Objective values are tabulated at iterations 10–1000, together with CPU time and iterations to reach a threshold (Tables 4.1–4.2, p. 18–19), plus iteration plots (Fig. 4.1, p. 20). μ = 1 is used even where the theory requires μ ≤ 1/L(g_σ), and the paper says so (p. 18). Skip counts are reported: FALM-S skipped 891/981 of 1000 steps, ALM-S none (p. 19). The per-iteration cost is conceded honestly: FISTA takes 35% (4%) more iterations but only 7% more (20% less) time (p. 19).
  - (b) RPCA on surveillance video (Hall 25344×200, Campus 20480×960) against EADM and IADM. "ALM is slightly worse than IADM" but much faster than EADM (Table 5.1, p. 24).
  - (c) Matrix completion with gross corruption (n = 500, 1000; SR = 80–90%; about 36 iterations regardless of size; Table 5.2, p. 24–25).
  - Continuation on μ violates the theory, again disclosed (p. 23). With continuation, FALM showed no advantage over ALM, so only ALM is compared (p. 23). FALM showed no advantage on SICS either (p. 26).
- **D6 Positioning**: The closest prior art is named and differentiated precisely. Kiwiel–Rosa–Ruszczyński use the same approximations but different prox terms and give no complexity (p. 5–6). Goldfarb–Ma is Jacobi-type (p. 5). The methods are presented as extensions of ISTA/FISTA with better constants (p. 5, p. 11, p. 16). Priority is claimed carefully, prefixed with "To the best of our knowledge" (see the first quote below). A footnote credits Monteiro–Svaiter's later ADM complexity result (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - An "Our contribution." paragraph states both orders and the practical per-iteration claim (p. 5).
  - Every main theorem is followed by a Remark comparing constants to the classical baseline (Remarks 2.5, 3.6, p. 11, p. 16).
  - Theory-parameter violations in experiments are disclosed in the text (p. 18, p. 23).
  - Negative findings are stated plainly (FALM no better on SICS, p. 26).
  - The conclusion only summarizes (p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Algorithms plus complexity theory, the first for Gauss–Seidel ADMs per the authors (p. 6), with large-scale demonstrations ("roughly 40 million variables", p. 19). Limits stated: no bound when both f and g are nonsmooth (p. 7); smoothing worsens the order (p. 18, p. 21); SICS theory applies only near the solution (p. 25–26); acceleration does not help with continuation or on SICS (p. 23, p. 26). No future directions are stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The trusted splitting method (ADAL/Peaceman–Rachford) is kept. The analysis reruns the ISTA/FISTA proofs, and a single safeguard (the skip test) is added only where the smooth-g inequality can fail (p. 7–8, p. 12).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. The whole problem class is defined by cheap, closed-form prox subproblems (SVD, shrinkage, linear system) costing about one gradient (p. 1–3, p. 21).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (deterministic). The target is to match the optimal first-order order O(√(L/ε)), with constants no worse than the classical method's (p. 5, p. 16). This is a deterministic ancestor of *deterministic parity*.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Iteration-based comparison is paired with CPU time, and the per-iteration cost difference with FISTA is conceded (p. 19). All competitors run at their downloaded default settings (p. 18, p. 23).
