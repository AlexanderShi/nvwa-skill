### S073 · Finding optimal policy for queueing models: New parameterization (arXiv preprint 2022, arXiv:2206.10073)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: T. H. Tran (Cornell ORIE), L. M. Nguyen (IBM Research) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Cornell period (affiliations p. 1; ONR grant N00014-22-1-2154, p. 9). The paper applies the group's line search with noisy oracles (ALOE, attributed to [Berahas et al., 2019], with [Jin et al., 2021] cited for its guarantees, p. 7) to policy optimisation in RL. Nguyen is a coauthor of the group's earlier SGD papers (SARAH and Hogwild, cited on pp. 12–13). The FoCM 2022 comparison of gradient estimators is not cited (references, pp. 12–14). **Read**: full. All 14 pages were read: the main text (pp. 1–9), Appendix A proofs (pp. 10–11), Appendix B settings (p. 11) and a scan of the references (pp. 12–14). The figure images were not available (see batch note).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper enters from understanding rather than from a new method. RL succeeds in practice, but the authors say its algorithmic frameworks, and especially the optimisation process, are not well understood (p. 1). The authors pick a problem whose optimal policy is known analytically. For one server with m parallel queues and exponential interarrival and service times, the c-µ priority rule is optimal (Theorem 1, p. 4). The optimiser's choices can then be judged against the truth (pp. 1, 3). The paper names three gaps. (i) Earlier RL work on queueing is model-based: the agent estimates system parameters (Liu et al. 2019; Dai–Gluzman 2020, p. 1). (ii) REINFORCE has not been compared with other gradient estimators (p. 1), because each estimator ships inside an algorithm customised to it (p. 6). (iii) Reward and parameterisation choices shape the optimisation problem, and their effect is poorly explored (pp. 1–2). The problem is min_A J(A) = E_{τ∼π(A)}[lim (1/T) Σ c(s_t, a_t)], the long-run average holding cost (eq. 2, p. 3).
  - Transferable: To study an optimiser or an estimator, choose a testbed whose optimum is known in closed form. Then report distance to the truth (here, the rate of correct actions against π_P), not only the objective value (pp. 3, 8).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - The base class is a linear softmax policy π(A) = softmax(As) with A ∈ R^{(m+1)×m}. It has m+1 actions, one of them idle (eqs. 4–5, p. 4).
  - Theorem 2 (pp. 4–5) exhibits a sequence A_k, with entries of the form (Q+1)^i·k + 1, that starts at the uniform random policy and converges to the priority policy π_P. It also proves that no bounded sequence converges to π_P. The authors conclude that the parameters must grow without bound, possibly exponentially fast in the number of queues (p. 5).
  - The design move is a log-scale parameterisation, B = ln A (element-wise) with π̄(B) = softmax(e^B s) (eq. 7, p. 5). Along the Theorem 2 path, B_k grows like ln k instead of k (p. 5).
  - Theorem 3 uses the shift invariance of softmax to show that every A has an equivalent positive A′. So the log-scale class loses no policies (p. 5; proof p. 11).
  - Unchanged parts: the zeroth-order oracle J̃ averages the cost over n sample paths (eq. 8, p. 6). The gradient estimators are standard: finite differences along unit vectors (eq. 9) and REINFORCE with a variance-reducing baseline (eq. 10) (p. 6). The optimiser is ALOE, a modified Armijo test with slack 2ε_f. On success α grows by a factor 1/γ, capped at α_max. On failure it shrinks by γ (Algorithm 1, p. 7).
  - Why ALOE: the paper calls it robust to the choice of gradient estimator, with performance that can be derived from the estimators' cost and error (p. 2). It is simpler than TRPO or PPO but has complexity guarantees and allows biased oracles (p. 7).
  - Transferable: Suppose a softmax or logit parameterisation reaches a deterministic optimum only at infinity. First use shift invariance to make the weights positive. Then reparameterise them as w = e^b, so that the path to the optimum grows logarithmically (pp. 5, 11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Queueing model: Poisson arrivals with rates λ_i and exponential service with rates µ_i. There is one server. The policy is preemptive and allows idling. Uniformization turns the system into an MDP (pp. 3–4). Buffers are finite, with capacity N in §2.2 (p. 4) and Q = 100 in Appendix B (p. 11).
  - Theorem 1, the optimality of c-µ, requires an infinite buffer and ρ < 1. For a finite buffer, the paper only states the expectation that π_P stays approximately optimal when the capacity is large (p. 4). Theorem 2 assumes s_i ≤ Q (p. 4).
  - No smoothness, variance or accuracy assumption is stated for J, J̃ or either gradient estimator. ALOE's guarantees are cited [Berahas et al., 2019; Jin et al., 2021], but the oracle constants for these estimators are never derived (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The theory covers only representation, not optimisation.
  - Theorem 2 (proof p. 10). First drop the +1 offset, using Theorem 3. This leaves z_k = k·y, where queue i is weighted by (Q+1)^{m−i}. Because s_i ≤ Q, the highest-priority non-empty queue then has the strictly largest score, a base-(Q+1) encoding of a lexicographic rule. So softmax(k·y) tends to a unit vector. For the non-attainability part: if all entries are bounded by R, then |z| ≤ mRQ and the softmax denominator is at most (m+1)e^{mRQ}. The probabilities of wrong actions then cannot go to 0, a contradiction (p. 10).
  - Theorem 3 (p. 11): adding µ to every entry of A adds µ·Σ s_k to every logit, which leaves softmax unchanged. Choosing µ = 1 − min A_ij makes all entries positive.
  - One minor slip: the displayed y has leading weight (Q+1)^m, but the text then uses y_j = (Q+1)^{m−j} s_j (p. 10). The argument is not affected.
  - The claim that the log-scale landscape is smoother is hedged (the log scale may give a smoother objective, p. 5) and is not proved. There is no convergence or complexity analysis of the optimisation itself.
  - Transferable: To show that a linear score over bounded integer states realises a lexicographic priority rule, weight coordinate i by (Q+1)^{m−i}. Then scale the score by k → ∞ inside the softmax (p. 10).
  - Transferable: To prove an optimum is not attainable with bounded parameters, bound the softmax denominator and show that some probability must stay away from 0 (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Variance probe (pp. 6–7): covariance matrices of the finite-difference (FD) and REINFORCE (PG) estimators at equal sample complexity. They are evaluated at the random policy and at π_P (Figure 2). FD has similar or lower variance at the random policy and much lower variance at π_P (p. 6). The paper does not say how the estimators are evaluated at π_P, which lies outside the differentiable class (p. 5).
  - Setup: OpenAI gym with m = 3 and Q = 100. The four service-rate settings give ρ = 1/3, 2/3, 1 and 11/6 (Table 1, p. 8). T = 100, with 2000 ALOE iterations from A = 0 and a 1000-step warm-up (p. 11). The zeroth-order oracle uses n = 20 paths. FD uses m(m+1) = 12 unit directions, and PG uses N = [m(m+1)+1]n paths to match (p. 11).
  - Tuning: ALOE uses ε_f = 0.01, α_max = 2, α_0 = 0.01, θ = 0.01 and γ = 0.5. These were chosen by grid search in each setting. Results are averaged over 5 seeds (pp. 7, 11).
  - Not reported: the FD step u, the arrival rates and the holding costs (p. 11). The ρ values are consistent with all arrival rates equal to 1 (my arithmetic, not stated).
  - Metrics: the best normalised cost reached (Table 1, Figure 3) and the rate of correct actions (Table 2, Figure 4) (pp. 8–9).
  - Results: the log scale is better in most settings (in the low-load cost, linear is slightly better: 0.0003 vs 0.0004, Table 1, p. 8), and FD is best from medium to heavy load (p. 7). The best correct-action rates fall from 94.4% (low load) to 58.8% (heavy load) (Table 2, p. 9). The text and tables report no failure cases and no spread across seeds (whether the figures show spread could not be checked, since the images are absent).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a modelling / parameterisation idea supported by two small representation theorems (Theorems 2–3, pp. 4–5), plus an empirical comparison (pp. 6–9). There is no optimisation theory, and no code release is mentioned.
  - Limits the authors write: none as such. The conclusion restates the findings (p. 9). The only step beyond this setting is a hedged belief that the approach applies to more complex queueing systems and to other RL applications (p. 3).
  - Limits visible from the text but not discussed:
    - (i) The balanced and heavy settings have ρ = 1 and 11/6, outside condition (3), ρ < 1, which Theorem 1 needs (pp. 3–4, 8).
    - (ii) In two settings the reported best cost falls below the confidence interval of the optimal (priority) policy. Balanced load: 0.0066 vs [0.0079, 0.0093]. Heavy load: 0.2586 vs [0.2611, 0.4037] (Table 1, p. 8). The authors attribute costs near the lower end of the CIs to ALOE's acceptance rule (p. 7). But the figures plot the minimum cost achieved over noisy estimates (Figure 3 caption, p. 8), which biases the reported values downward.
    - (iii) Only m = 3 queues are tested, and hyperparameters are tuned per setting (pp. 7, 11).
    - (iv) ALOE's oracle conditions are not checked for these estimators (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 6, 11): FD and REINFORCE are compared at equal sample cost (N = [m(m+1)+1]n) inside the same outer method. The paper names why this comparison is rare: each estimator comes bundled with its own algorithm (p. 6). Weaker than in the core papers: the comparison uses empirical covariances at two points and the best cost per iteration, not derived sample-to-accuracy bounds. Per-iteration sample budgets are matched, so the iteration axis stands in for samples.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 7): the group's Armijo line search with a noise slack (ALOE) is reused unchanged as a neutral outer method. It is chosen over RL-specific methods (TRPO, PPO) because it is simple, has guarantees and is robust to the estimator.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (p. 7): the oracle contract is invoked only by citation: ALOE is said to allow bias in both oracles. No accuracy or probability is derived for FD or PG in this setting, unlike the oracle-implementation sections of the core papers.
- Method 6 ⚠ variant (pp. 1, 5): the structure exploited is the known form of the optimal *solution* (a deterministic priority policy) plus softmax shift invariance. It is used to design the parameterisation, not to model components of the objective.
- Methods 3 and 4: no link (no complexity analysis, no model geometry).
- new-pattern candidate: known-optimum testbed. Study an optimiser or estimator on an application whose optimum is known analytically, and measure agreement with it (pp. 1, 3, 8).
- new-pattern candidate: reparameterise along the path to the optimum. Prove the optimum is reached only as parameters diverge, possibly exponentially fast, then change variables so the path grows slowly (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Match the sample budget per iteration across estimators. With d finite-difference directions and n paths per function estimate, give the likelihood-ratio estimator (d+1)·n paths (p. 11).
- Before running the optimiser, probe estimator covariance at two reference points, the starting policy and the known optimum. Variance near the solution is what decides the endgame (pp. 6–7).
- Use an estimator-agnostic adaptive method (Armijo with a noise slack) as the harness. Then only the estimator changes between runs (pp. 6–7).
- Log-reparameterise softmax weights after shifting them positive, when the optimum is a deterministic policy (pp. 5, 11).
