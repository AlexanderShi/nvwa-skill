**Batch intent**: See how Scheinberg's group extends one analysis framework (classical adaptive methods driven by probabilistic oracles) along three axes: the algorithm family (step search → trust region; line search → proximal and accelerated methods), the guarantee type (expected → high-probability, with irreducible noise and second-order points), and the cost measure (iterations → total samples). For each extension, record which proof device carries it.
**Focus dimensions**: D1–D8 (all three papers are core). Emphasis on D2 (what changes relative to the classical method and what does not), D3 (the oracle contract) and D4 (proof devices).

### S041 · First-and second-order high probability complexity bounds for trust-region methods with noisy oracles (Mathematical Programming 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10107-023-01999-5; arXiv:2205.03667)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: L. Cao (corresponding author, p. 1), A. S. Berahas · **Position in research trajectory**: Cornell period. This is the trust-region counterpart of the Jin–Scheinberg–Xie high-probability step-search analysis ([14], p. 3). It moves the STORM / Blanchet et al. line, which had expected-complexity bounds ([6, 10]), to high-probability tail bounds with irreducible noise (p. 3). It leaves total oracle cost open (p. 3), and S057 later closes that gap. **Read level**: full text, including Appendix A (pp. 39–40) and Appendix B (pp. 40–42); the version read is arXiv v3 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The paper enters through an assumption it judges too strong in earlier trust-region work. [2, 13] assume exact function values and first-order oracles whose error shrinks with the radius, so in effect they assume arbitrarily precise gradients, even if only with some probability (p. 2). [6, 10] allow random function values but no irreducible noise, and give only expected bounds (pp. 2–3). Two settings motivate the work: DFO on simulation or ML-training outputs whose noise comes from limited precision or randomness, and ERM with mini-batches (pp. 1–2). The problem is unconstrained minimisation of a (twice) Lipschitz-differentiable φ, where neither φ nor its derivatives can be computed accurately (p. 1).
  - Transferable: Open with one general definition, an oracle that is implementable over a set S_j of (accuracy, probability) pairs (Definition 1.1, p. 2). Then restate every prior paper as a choice of S_j (pp. 2–3; Figure 1, p. 6), so the gap in the literature shows up as a picture.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The classical trust-region method is kept (Cauchy-decrease step, radius factor γ), with two changes. (i) The ratio is relaxed: ρ_k = (f_k − f_k^+ + r)/(m_k(x_k) − m_k(x_k+s_k)), with r ≥ 2ε_f for the first-order method and r ≥ 2ε_f + ε_g^{3/2} for the second-order one (Algorithms 1–2, pp. 11–12; Remark 3.2, p. 11). (ii) The radius update is cautious: the radius grows only if the step is accepted *and* ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2 δ_k (second order: β^m_k = max{‖g_k‖, −λ_min(H_k)} ≥ η2 δ_k). Otherwise it shrinks, even when the step is accepted (pp. 11–12). The gradient oracle is called with accuracy input δ_k in the first-order method and δ_k² in the second-order one (p. 12). The model form, the subproblem decrease conditions (3.3)/(3.4) and the radius factors stay as they are (pp. 10–12). The slack r offsets the function-evaluation errors at x_k and x_k+s_k (p. 11). The relaxation mirrors earlier relaxed line/step-search methods [3, 5, 14] (p. 11).
  - Transferable: To port a noise-tolerant acceptance test from line search to trust region, add the noise slack to the numerator of ρ_k and keep the rest of the method. Set the slack from the oracle's intrinsic constant (r = 2ε_f), and report what happens when it is set wrongly (pp. 11, 33–36).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: φ has an L1-Lipschitz gradient (plus an L2-Lipschitz Hessian for the second-order method) and is bounded below (p. 4). Oracle 0 has either bounded noise |e| ≤ ε_f (Oracle 0.1) or a two-parameter subexponential tail P(|e| > t) ≤ exp(a(ε_f − t)) (Oracle 0.2), which leaves the noise unrestricted inside [−ε_f, ε_f] (p. 4). Oracle 1 satisfies P(‖g − ∇φ‖ ≤ ε_g + κ_eg δ^(1)) ≥ p1 with p1 ∈ [0.5, 1] (p. 4). Oracle 2 is the Hessian analogue with ε_H (p. 5). The constants ε_f, ε_g, ε_H and the κ's belong to the oracle; δ is chosen by the algorithm from the radius (Remark 2.5, p. 5). The model Hessian is bounded (Assumption 3.1, p. 10).
  - The authors admit one convenience assumption, Assumption 5.1 (scaling ε' ≤ 1 and ε_g, ε_H ≪ 1). They say it can be avoided, at the cost of a much more complex analysis (p. 26).
  - They also admit a trade-off. Oracle 0 is more relaxed than the oracle in [6] (irreducible noise is allowed) but also stronger (light tails only) (p. 3). It therefore excludes the heavy-tailed errors allowed in [6, 10] (p. 7).
  - Oracle costs are not modelled (pp. 2–3).
  - The paper shows the oracles can be implemented. For ERM, a mini-batch plus Markov's inequality gives ε_g = σ/((1−p1)√N) and κ_eg = σ (p. 8). Forward finite differences, finite-difference Hessians and linear interpolation built from Oracle 0 give Oracles 1 and 2 (Props 2.6–2.7, pp. 8–9); under Oracle 0.2 the resulting probability is p1 = (1 − e^{a(ε_f − ε̂_f)})^{n+1} (p. 9).
  - Transferable: For each oracle you assume, add a short implementation subsection that derives its constants (ε_g, κ_eg, p) from primitive assumptions such as variance or noise bounds (pp. 8–9).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**: The proof has four stages (pp. 14–25).
  1. Deterministic lemmas that hold on each sample path. When the model is accurate and r ≥ e_k^+ − e_k, the paper gives sufficient conditions for a step to be accepted or successful (Lemmas 4.1–4.2, pp. 14–15). A progress lemma gives φ(x_k) − φ(x_{k+1}) ≥ h(δ_k) − e_k + e_k^+ − r with h(δ) = C3 δ² (Lemma 4.3, p. 16).
  2. Indicator variables: I_k (accurate model), J_k (evaluation errors offset by r), Θ_k (successful), Θ'_k (accepted), and Λ_k, Λ'_k (radius above a threshold) (p. 16). The threshold Δ̄ = C1 min_{k<T} ‖∇φ(X_k)‖ − C2 ε_g is itself a random variable. The authors name this as the new technique relative to the constant step threshold ᾱ of [14] (pp. 3, 16).
  3. Three counting lemmas. A telescoping bound on φ limits the number of successful iterations with a large radius (Lemma 4.7, p. 17). A count of radius moves uses ζ_k = max{log(Δ_k/Δ̄'), 0} (Lemma 4.8, pp. 17–18). Azuma–Hoeffding is applied to the submartingale Σ I_k − p1 t (Lemma 4.9, p. 18).
  4. The counts are combined (Lemma 4.10, p. 19), and the theorem follows by contradiction through a monotone function Q(y) of the minimum gradient norm (Theorem 4.11, pp. 20–21).
  - The subexponential case needs three more pieces: a second Azuma bound on Σ J_k with p0 = 1 − 2exp(a(ε_f − r/2)) (Lemma 4.14, p. 22); a moment-generating-function bound E exp(λ|X|) ≤ exp(λb)/(1 − λ/a) for the two-parameter tail (Prop 4.15, p. 22; proof in App. A, p. 40); and a Chernoff-plus-induction bound on the errors accumulated over accepted steps (Lemma 4.16, p. 23).
  - Results:
    - First order: T = O(ε^{-2}) with tail probability 1 − exp(−(1 − p̂1/p1)² T/2), for ε > sqrt((4ε_f + 2r)/(C3 γ² C1² (2p1 − 1))) + (C2/C1) ε_g, which means ε ≥ O(√ε_f) + O(ε_g) (Theorem 4.11, p. 20; Remark 4.12, p. 21).
    - Second order: O(ε^{-3}) for ε ≥ O(ε_f^{1/3}) + O(√ε_g) + O(ε_H), with p12 = p1·p2 (Lemma 5.6 and Theorem 5.7, p. 29; Remark 5.8, p. 30; Conclusions, p. 37).
  - The proofs of Theorems 4.18 and 5.10 are omitted as analogous, and Theorem 5.7 is only sketched (pp. 25, 29). The overall argument counts iterations over a fixed horizon. It does not use a supermartingale on a potential or a stopping-time bound.
  - Transferable: To get a high-probability iteration bound for an adaptive method:
    1. Classify iterations by accurate? / errors offset? / successful? / radius large?
    2. Bound the count of each class deterministically on every sample path.
    3. Apply Azuma–Hoeffding only to Σ I_k (and Σ J_k).
    4. Invert through a monotone Q(·) to get the statement T ≥ Q(ε) ⇒ min ‖∇φ‖ ≤ ε with high probability (pp. 16–21).
  - Transferable: If the radius threshold depends on the unknown minimum gradient norm, define it as a random variable over the horizon instead of fixing ε in advance (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: There are two sets (pp. 30–37).
  - (a) Tightness probe (pp. 30–33). The test problem is φ = L1‖x‖²/2 with n = 20 and linear models. The noise is adversarial. Function errors are set to ±ε_f so that good steps are rejected and bad steps accepted (6.2). The gradient is chosen by solving small optimisation problems that maximise the loss subject to the step being accepted, and gradient accuracy is imposed only when a Bernoulli(p1) flag I_k equals 1 (pp. 30–32; closed-form solutions in App. B, pp. 40–42). Four (ε_f, ε_g) settings were run. The plateaus of ‖∇φ‖ (≈4.8, 4, 1.2, 0) are compared with the theoretical floors (21.58, 9.33, 12.25, 0). The ε_g coefficient is within 7/3 of optimal, and the √ε_f coefficient is up to 10× too large (pp. 32–33). The authors note openly that κ_fcd was set to 2, outside the assumed (0, 1], which is harmless because that bound is never used (p. 32).
  - (b) Effect of r (pp. 33–37).
    - In the adversarial setup, r ranges over {0, ε_f, 2ε_f, 4ε_f, 8ε_f}. Theory says r < 2ε_f may stall. In the run with r = 0 the method did not stall, but φ rose late in the run. The authors report this mismatch between theory and experiment (p. 33).
    - The practical code is DFO-TR [1], a quadratic-interpolation trust-region method with r added to ρ_k, run on the Moré–Wild set (p. 33). The 53 problems are scaled to φ(x0) = 100 and φ̂ = 0 and then replicated; the text says each problem is replicated 4 times but gives a total of 53 × 5 = 265 problems, so the count is ambiguous (p. 33). Noise is uniform with ε_f = 0.2 (p. 33). The budget is 2000 evaluations, and the hyperparameters were tuned so that the best variant gets close to 0 (p. 36). Results are shown as performance and data profiles at τ = 10^-3 and 10^-5 (p. 36).
    - The best value is r = 4ε_f, which the authors read as consistent with the second-order theory (r > 2ε_f) (p. 36).
    - A repeat with subexponential noise (uniform[0, ε_f] plus Exp(a), with a random sign; ε_f = 0.1, a = 20) finds r = 0.5 or 1.25 best (p. 36).
  - The only methods compared are DFO-TR variants; there is no external baseline (pp. 36–37).
  - Transferable: Build an adversarial oracle that picks the worst admissible noise at every iteration, within the contract and with Bernoulli(p) flags marking the good iterations. Compare the resulting plateau with the theorem's noise floor to measure how loose the constants are (pp. 30–33).
  - Transferable: When theory sets a hyperparameter from an unknown constant (r = 2ε_f), sweep multiples {0, 1, 2, 4, 8}× in a practical code and compare the best value with the theory (pp. 33–36).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper acknowledges that it draws inspiration from [14], the JSX21 step-search analysis (p. 3). It states its improvements against three named works (p. 3):
  - against [6]: irreducible noise and high-probability bounds;
  - against [13]: relaxed first- and second-order oracles, and an inexact zeroth-order oracle;
  - against [14]: first- and second-order trust-region methods, which need new techniques.
  - It credits the relaxed trust-region method of Sun–Nocedal for bounded noise [21] (p. 2) and relaxed Armijo [3, 5] (p. 3).
  - The critique works by restating prior work rather than attacking it. All prior assumptions are redrawn as sets S_j (Figure 1, p. 6), and the paper concedes where its own oracle is stronger (pp. 3, 7). It calls its iteration bounds similar to those of [6, 13, 14] (p. 3).
  - Transferable: In the contribution list, write one bullet per closest competitor (*In comparison to [X], we …*), and in the same bullet say where your own assumption is stronger (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution appears first in the abstract (relaxed acceptance plus cautious radius gives exponentially decaying tail bounds, p. 1). It appears again as a bulleted comparison list at the end of the introduction (p. 3). Theorems are followed by remarks that translate them. Remark 4.12 admits that bound (4.14) is complicated, then rewrites it to show the O(ε^{-2}) term, the radius-adjustment term and the noise floor (p. 21). Limits are stated inline before the results; for example, the target cannot be reached for arbitrarily small ε unless ε_f = ε_g = 0 (p. 14). There is little hedging, and the ratio of theorems to intuitive explanation is moderate (whole-paper judgement, pp. 1–37). The many constants C1–C8 are defined inline (pp. 15, 27–28). Some proofs are shortened as analogous (pp. 25, 29).
  - Transferable: After a messy tail-bound theorem, add a remark that splits the bound into three parts, each with one sentence of interpretation: the optimal-order term, the initial radius-adjustment term and the noise floor (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contributions at three levels:
    - Theory: the first high-probability bounds for first- and second-order trust-region methods with irreducible noise. The second-order analysis is claimed as the first of its kind for irreducible noise (p. 3).
    - Algorithm: a two-line modification of the trust-region method (p. 11).
    - Experiments: an empirical tightness check and a test in a practical code (pp. 30–37).
  - The limits are stated concretely:
    - Oracle costs are not accounted for, and the adaptive accuracy makes total work harder to analyse (p. 3).
    - The zeroth-order oracle must be light-tailed (p. 7).
    - Assumption 5.1 imposes a scaling (p. 26).
    - Poisedness and how to implement Oracle 2 are out of scope (p. 10).
    - The theory does not explain the behaviour when r < 2ε_f (p. 33).
  - The conclusion only restates the results and lists no future work (p. 37). Studying further oracle settings is flagged as worthwhile but beyond scope (p. 10).

### S057 · Sample complexity analysis for adaptive optimization algorithms with stochastic oracles (Mathematical Programming 2025, 209(1):651–679, DOI 10.1007/s10107-024-02078-z; arXiv:2303.06838)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: B. Jin, M. Xie (Cornell ORIE, p. 1) · **Position in research trajectory**: Cornell period. The paper closes the gap left by S041 (oracle cost not accounted for, S041 p. 3). It turns the known iteration bounds of STORM [BCMS19] and SASS [JSX21a] into total sample complexity (p. 2). S068 later cites it as the tool that makes its own step-parameter lower bound straightforward (S068 p. 22). **Read level**: full text, pp. 1–21 (there is no appendix); the version read is arXiv v3 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The paper enters through an obstacle in the existing analyses. Adaptive stochastic methods tie oracle accuracy to the step parameter. Unlike in the deterministic case, the step parameter is not bounded away from zero, because oracles can fail. Oracle cost grows as the step parameter shrinks, so until now only iteration complexity was known (pp. 1–2). The task is recast: bounding total oracle cost is the same as lower-bounding the step parameter α (p. 7). The motivation comes from ML. Stochastic gradient methods are popular despite known shortcomings, while adaptive methods avoid the cost of tuning step sizes for every application (p. 1).
  - Transferable: If your proof tool leaves one quantity uncontrolled (here, α_k → 0), make bounding that quantity the subject of the paper, and show that it unlocks a cost measure people care about (pp. 1–2, 7).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key insight is that the log of the step parameter behaves like a random walk that drifts upward whenever α_k ≤ ᾱ and p > 1/2. It can be coupled with a one-sided reflected random walk, whose maximum over n steps can be bounded with high probability (pp. 2, 8–11). There is no new algorithm. Instead an abstract framework (Algorithm 1: model, step, sufficient-reduction test with parameter θ; α_{k+1} = min{γ^{-1}α_k, α_max} on success, γα_k otherwise) covers step search, trust region and cubic regularisation (pp. 4–7). The only thing abstracted is Assumption 1 on the step-parameter process (p. 7).
  - The analysis has a consequence for algorithm design: choose γ as a function of p and n (Remarks 3–4 and Corollary 1, p. 9). When oracles are less reliable, γ should be larger, because oracle failures then cause many unsuccessful steps (p. 9). With γ = (1/2q)^{-1/4} and ω = 1, the minimum step decays like n^{-1/2}, which matches the usual SGD step-size decay (p. 9).
  - Transferable: Find the one property shared by every algorithm in a class (here Assumption 1, p. 7), prove the theorem for that property only, then instantiate it algorithm by algorithm (pp. 13–19).
  - Transferable: Turn the analysis into a parameter rule. Here the probability-dependent choice of γ bounds the step parameter by a constant times ᾱ (Corollary 1, p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumption 1 (p. 7) has two parts: (i) A_k ∈ {γA_{k−1}, min{α_max, γ^{-1}A_{k−1}}}; (ii) there are ᾱ > 0 and p > 1/2 with P(A_{k+1} = γ^{-1}A_k | F_k, k < T_ε, A_k ≤ ᾱ) ≥ p. The authors state that [CS17, PS20, BCS19, BCMS19, GRVZ18, JSX21a, CBS22] satisfy it under suitable oracle probabilities (p. 7).
  - Oracle cost oc(α) is non-increasing in α (Assumption 2, p. 12).
  - The generic oracle has accuracy bounded by a function of α with probability 1 − δ_j, and a cost that depends on x and α (Definition 1, p. 4). A per-method catalogue lists SS.0/SS.1, TR1.0/1.1, TR2.0/2.1/2.2 and CR.1/2 (pp. 5–7).
  - ERM instantiation for STORM: bounded variances σ_f², σ_g² and unbiased sample gradients. Chebyshev's inequality gives oc0 = σ_f²/(δ0 κ_ef² α⁴) and oc1 = σ_g²/(δ1 κ_eg² α²) (p. 14).
  - ERM instantiation for SASS: subexponential function noise and gradient variance M_c + M_v‖∇ϕ‖² (p. 17). Here the zeroth-order cost does not depend on α (p. 17).
  - Admitted simplifications: the same factor γ is used for increases and decreases (p. 11); constants from prior papers are absorbed into O(·) (p. 13); filtration details are omitted for brevity (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  1. Coupling (Lemma 1, pp. 9–10). Write A_k = γ^{Y_k} ᾱ and extend Y after T_ε with a drifted walk. Then build Z_k ≥ Y_k on every sample path by keeping a down-move of Y with probability p/p′.
  2. Hitting probability for level ℓ of the reflected walk (Proposition 1, p. 10). P(N(ℓ,n) > 0) ≤ (n − ℓ + 1)·(1 − q/p)/(1 − (q/p)^{ℓ+1})·(q/p)^ℓ + c(2q)^ℓ, using Feller's explicit diagonalisation of the transition matrix. The bound is essentially tight (Remark 1, p. 11).
  3. Choosing ℓ = ⌈(1+ω) log_{1/2q} n⌉ gives Theorem 1 (pp. 8, 11). With probability ≥ 1 − n^{-ω} − c n^{-(1+ω)}, either T_ε < n or min α_k ≥ ᾱ γ n^{-(1+ω) log_{1/2q}(1/γ)}. As p → 1 this recovers the deterministic bound ᾱγ (Remark 1, p. 8).
  4. Expected total oracle complexity follows from a layer-cake sum over levels (Theorem 2, p. 12). The high-probability version is TOC(T_ε) ≤ n·oc(α*(n)), obtained by a union bound with P(T_ε > n) from earlier iteration bounds (Theorem 3, p. 13).
  5. Plug-in results:
     - STORM, expected: gradient samples O(σ_g² ε^{-4} log(1/ε)) and function samples of order σ_f² ε^{-6} (p. 16).
     - STORM, high probability: obtained through Markov's inequality on E[T_ε] (Theorem 5, p. 16).
     - SASS, nonconvex: gradient samples O(M_c/ε⁴ + M_v/ε²), the same as SGD, and M_v/ε² under interpolation (Remark 3, p. 19).
     - SASS, strongly convex: O((M_c/ε + M_v) log(1/ε)) (Remark 3, p. 19).
  - The proof of Proposition 1 is credited to J. A. Fill (p. 20).
  - Transferable: To bound the total cost of an adaptive stochastic method:
    1. Couple the log step parameter with a reflected random walk.
    2. Bound the walk's maximum over n steps with high probability.
    3. Multiply n by the oracle cost at the resulting floor.
    4. Union-bound with any high-probability iteration bound (pp. 9–13).
  - Transferable: Get the expected cost by summing P(TOC > n·oc(ᾱγ^ℓ)) over levels ℓ (a layer-cake argument) (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The only figure simulates one-sided random walks against the lower bound for p = 0.8 and several γ, as an illustration (Figure 1, p. 8).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents itself as the missing total-cost layer for about ten prior adaptive stochastic methods, most of them from the group (p. 3). Methods with different step dynamics (Adam, Barzilai–Borwein SGD) are set aside as not needing this analysis and not fitting it. Methods with similar dynamics (Rinaldi–Vicente–Zeffiro, ASTRO-DF, Regier et al.) are said to be out of direct reach because they have no iteration bounds, and applying the result to them is suggested as future work (p. 2). The yardstick is the stochastic lower bound of Arjevani et al. [ACD+19] and SGD, not the deterministic method. The authors flag that ACD+19 defines complexity differently (pp. 15–16). A probabilist is credited for the key proposition (p. 20).
  - Transferable: Explain which neighbouring methods your framework cannot cover and why (different step dynamics, or no iteration bound). This fences off the scope before reviewers raise it (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract runs gap → obstacle → contribution (p. 1). A three-bullet contribution list names the proof device (a coupling) (p. 2). An *Illustration of Theorem 1* section with plots and four remarks on algorithm design comes before the proof (pp. 8–9). The applications section says openly that it omits constants and refers readers to earlier papers (p. 13). Hedging is moderate and targeted: results are said to essentially match lower bounds (pp. 2, 16, 19), and the applicability of the lower bound is stated as a belief left for separate study (p. 16).
  - Transferable: Put an illustration and remarks on design consequences between a theorem statement and its proof (pp. 8–9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contributions: an analytical tool (the step-parameter lower bound), a total-cost framework, and the first total sample complexity results for STORM and SASS (pp. 2, 13, 19).
  - The limits are concrete:
    - Increase and decrease factors must be equal; the unequal case is left open (p. 11).
    - The lower-bound comparison uses a different definition of complexity (p. 16).
    - For STORM, the high-probability result comes only through Markov's inequality on E[T_ε], so its probability guarantee is weak. The trust-region variants in Remark 2 do better (p. 16).
    - Function-value sample complexity, ε^{-6}, is worse than gradient complexity unless σ_f² ≤ O(σ_g² ε²) (p. 16).
    - The convex case is omitted for brevity (p. 19).
  - Future work: unequal increase and decrease factors (p. 11), and methods such as RVZ23, SHP18 and RJM17 once a stopping time is specified (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A coupling that dominates the log step parameter by a one-sided reflected random walk (pp. 9–10).
- A high-probability bound on the walk's maximum via its explicit transition-matrix spectrum, with the level set to ℓ ≍ log n (pp. 10–11).
- A total-oracle-cost recipe: n times the cost at the high-probability step floor, union-bounded with a high-probability iteration bound (p. 13).
- A layer-cake expected-cost bound over step-parameter levels (p. 12).
- A design rule derived from the analysis: raise the step-decrease factor γ when the oracle success probability p is low (p. 9).
- Per-algorithm oracle cost tables derived by Chebyshev's inequality for mini-batch oracles (pp. 14, 17).

**Quotes**:

### S068 · Stochastic ISTA/FISTA adaptive step search algorithms for convex composite optimization (Journal of Optimization Theory and Applications 2025, 205(1):10, DOI 10.1007/s10957-025-02621-8; arXiv:2402.15646)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: L. M. Nguyen (IBM Research), T. H. Tran (Cornell; corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in research trajectory**: Scheinberg is listed at Georgia Tech (p. 1). The paper extends the Cartis–Scheinberg 2018 line-search framework (exact function values) to proximal and accelerated methods, and uses Scheinberg–Goldfarb–Bai 2014's FISTA-BKTR as the deterministic base (pp. 1–2). An earlier workshop version, Tran et al. 2023, treated the smooth case with decaying function-value error (p. 2; not read). **Read level**: full text, including the appendix (pp. 25–26); the version read is arXiv v2 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The paper enters through a missing extension. Line search, trust region and ARC already have stochastic versions whose complexity matches the deterministic order, but accelerated methods such as FISTA do not (p. 2). Two obstacles are named:
  - (i) FISTA's step parameter may only decrease. Deterministically this is harmless, because the step stays bounded below by a constant. Stochastically it is fatal, because a bad estimate may give no decrease however small α is (pp. 2, 13–14).
  - (ii) Accelerated analyses lack the per-successful-step decrease in f that the martingale framework relies on (p. 2).
  - Earlier fixes (Vaswani et al. 2019b; Franchini et al. 2023) test sufficient decrease on the stochastic function and need strong assumptions; Jin et al. show this is inferior (pp. 2, 14).
  - The problem is min f + h, with f convex and L-smooth and h proper, closed and convex with a cheap prox (p. 1).
  - Transferable: State the obstacle as the specific property of the deterministic method that breaks under the weaker oracle, here the monotone step parameter and the missing per-step decrease (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key idea is to start from the full-backtracking FISTA-BKTR of Scheinberg–Goldfarb–Bai 2014. Its θ_k bookkeeping lets α increase while keeping FISTA's key inequality α_{k−1}t²_{k−1} ≥ α_k t_k(t_k − 1) (pp. 2, 14).
  - Changes relative to the deterministic method:
    - The gradient is re-estimated on every attempt, including unsuccessful ones, so unsuccessful attempts count as iterations (pp. 5, 14–15).
    - α is multiplied by 1/γ after each success. The deterministic ISTA analysis still holds with such increases, and the stochastic version needs them (p. 5).
    - θ_k = γ after a success and θ_{k−1}/γ after a failure (Algorithm 3, p. 15).
  - Unchanged: exact function values, the exact prox, and the sufficient-decrease test F(p̃) ≤ Q̃ (pp. 2, 5).
  - Transferable: If the classical accelerated method forbids step increases, look for (or build) a deterministic variant whose bookkeeping allows them, then make that variant stochastic (pp. 2, 14–15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The oracle SFO_{κ,p} satisfies P(‖G(y,ξ) − ∇f(y)‖ ≤ κ‖D_α(y)‖) ≥ p, where D_α is the gradient mapping, κ ≤ 1/3 and p > 1/2. The estimate is not assumed unbiased (p. 4). The bound κ ≤ 1/3 is forced by the sufficient-decrease test (p. 5).
  - Relation to the strong growth condition (SGC): SGC implies Assumption 3 when h ≡ 0, so Assumption 3 is strictly weaker in the smooth case (pp. 3–4). SGC with a large constant can be turned into this oracle by averaging (p. 5).
  - The analysis adds a variance-decay condition in expectation:
    - ISTA: E‖∇f − G‖² ≤ 1/(α_k² k^{2+β}) (Assumption 4, p. 11);
    - FISTA: E‖∇f − G‖² ≤ 1/(α_k² t_k² k^{2+β}) (Assumption 5, p. 20).
    - Both mirror the deterministic error decay of Schmidt et al. (pp. 11, 20).
  - Exact function values and an exact prox are admitted to be strong assumptions. They are justified as a first step and by model-based DFO, where only derivatives are approximated (p. 2).
  - How to implement conditions based on the gradient mapping is acknowledged to be unresolved in the literature (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**: The proof has two parts.
  1. **Generic part (Theorem 1, p. 7).** E(N_ε − 1) ≤ 2p/(2p−1)² (2E(N_G) + log_γ(ᾱ/α1)), where N_G counts good iterations (true, successful, with α ≥ ᾱ). The proof reuses the Cartis–Scheinberg 2018 counting lemmas (Lemmas 2.3 and 2.7) over true/false × successful/unsuccessful × α above/below ᾱ. Here ᾱ = (1/L)(1 − 2κ/(1 − κ)) comes from Lemma 1 (a true iteration with a small step succeeds, via nonexpansiveness of the prox) (pp. 6–9). Because both algorithms have the same step-parameter dynamics, the result holds for both (p. 15).
  2. **Algorithm-specific part.** E(N_G) is bounded with the error-accumulation argument of Schmidt et al. 2011.
     - ISTA: a per-successful-step inequality with error term λ_k (Lemma 2, p. 9) and Schmidt's Lemma 1 on accumulated λ's (Lemma 3, p. 10). The expectations of Σλ_k² and Σλ_kΣλ_i are controlled by Assumption 4 and Wald's identity at the hitting time (pp. 11–13).
     - FISTA: the potential m_k = 2α_k^succ t_k² v_k + ‖u_k‖² does not change on unsuccessful steps (Lemma 5, p. 16) and grows by at most λ_k‖u_{k−1}‖ on successful ones (Lemma 6, p. 17). Together with α_K^succ t_K² ≥ (Σ_{k∈S}√α_k/2)² (Lemma 4, pp. 15–16), this bounds N_G² by a constant times 1/(ᾱε) (Theorem 4, p. 20).
  - Results: ISTA has E(N_ε) = O(1/ε) (Theorem 3, p. 13) and FISTA has O(1/√ε) (Theorem 5, p. 21), the deterministic orders up to constants (p. 2).
  - An appendix gives counterexamples showing that, for stochastic processes, bounds on E[N_ε] and expected-gap rates do not imply each other (pp. 25–26).
  - Transferable: Split the proof into a generic, algorithm-agnostic part, E(N_ε) ≲ E(# good iterations), and an algorithm-specific Lyapunov bound on E(# good iterations) (pp. 7, 11, 20).
  - Transferable: Use a potential that stays constant on unsuccessful steps, so only successful steps enter the telescoping sum (p. 16).
  - Transferable: Bound error sums up to a hitting time with Wald's identity, given summable per-step error moments (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper claims to extend three lines of work (p. 3):
  - Schmidt–Le Roux–Bach 2011: inexact ISTA/FISTA with a fixed step and deterministic error;
  - Scheinberg–Goldfarb–Bai 2014: FISTA-BKTR;
  - Berahas–Cao–Scheinberg 2021 and Cartis–Scheinberg 2018: the search-method framework with probabilistic oracles.
  - It contrasts itself with the stochastic proximal literature, which assumes unbiased gradients, and with results that bound E[F(x_k) − F*] instead of E[N_ε] (p. 3; appendix pp. 25–26).
  - It criticises Vaswani et al. 2019b and Franchini et al. 2023 directly, but with evidence (pp. 2, 14).
  - It compares itself frankly with Berahas et al. 2021. That paper gets O(1/ε) without variance decay but assumes bounded iterates and may need O(1/ε²) samples per step (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper is framed around an open question: whether the martingale framework extends to accelerated and proximal methods (p. 2). The conclusion says the question is resolved positively (p. 21). The contribution list is phrased as extensions of three named papers (p. 3). A dedicated paragraph, *Discussion of Condition (10)*, works out the implied sample cost, the risk that it is not optimal, and the unknown tightness (p. 13). Hedging is dense in the discussion paragraphs (p. 13) and absent from the theorem statements (pp. 7, 11, 13, 20–21). Proofs are long and fully written out (pp. 17–20).
  - Transferable: After a result that needs a strong extra assumption, add a named paragraph (*Discussion of Condition (X)*) that computes the sample cost it implies and says plainly whether optimality is known (p. 13).
  - Transferable: If your result type (expected hitting time) differs from the literature's (expected gap), include small counterexamples showing that neither implies the other (pp. 25–26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contributions: theory plus an algorithm, stochastic FISTA with full backtracking (pp. 1, 15).
  - The limits are stated concretely:
    - Function values and the prox are exact (p. 2).
    - The gradient variance must decay faster than 1/k², which means sample sizes growing like O(k²) and O(1/ε³) total samples for ISTA; this may not be optimal (p. 13).
    - The optimal complexity with biased gradients is unknown (p. 13).
    - The analysis may be loose because it ignores how ‖x_k − x*‖ depends on ε (p. 13).
    - The effect of β on tightness is unknown (p. 13).
    - Only expected complexity is proved (Theorems 3 and 5, pp. 13, 21).
  - Future work (pp. 2, 13, 22):
    - stochastic function estimates;
    - high-probability bounds, where the step-parameter lower bound is said to be straightforward via Jin et al. 2025 (= S057);
    - the strongly convex case;
    - inexact stochastic prox;
    - shuffling momentum.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 5, 14–15). ISTA/FISTA with backtracking is kept. The first property that fails (a monotone step parameter) is identified, and the repair (allow α to increase; recompute the gradient on every attempt) is taken from the authors' own earlier deterministic variant.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 4, 11, 13, 20). The probabilistic norm-type contract is there, but it is supplemented by an expected variance decay indexed by the iteration counter, E‖error‖² ≤ 1/(α_k² k^{2+β}). In effect this is a sample-size schedule of O(k²) (p. 13), not the pure contract in which accuracy is tied to the step and holds with a fixed probability.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 7, 13, 21). Expected hitting-time bounds match the deterministic orders O(1/ε) and O(1/√ε). The proof device is a ⚠ variant: a Lyapunov error-accumulation argument (Schmidt) plus Wald's identity, not a per-step-decrease potential. High-probability bounds are deferred (p. 22), which fits the sequencing in Heuristic 6.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (pp. 1, 4). The composite structure is kept exact through the prox, and only ∇f is stochastic.
- New-pattern candidate: port the probabilistic-oracle framework across algorithm families. Here it goes from line search to proximal and accelerated methods (pp. 1–3); S041 does the same from step search to trust region (S041 p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: relax one oracle at a time. Start with exact zeroth-order values, then bounded noise, then subexponential noise; the paper cites the history CS18 → BCS21 → JSX24 and BSV14/GRVZ18 → BCMS19/CMS18 (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: defend a nonstandard result type with toy counterexamples (pp. 25–26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A deterministic stepping-stone variant (FISTA-BKTR with θ_k) whose bookkeeping lets the step parameter grow, used as the base for the stochastic version (pp. 14–15).
- A two-level proof: generic counting of good iterations (Theorem 1), then an algorithm-specific bound on the number of good iterations (pp. 7, 11, 20).
- A FISTA potential that is constant on unsuccessful steps, with an error term λ_k‖u_{k−1}‖ on successful ones (pp. 16–17).
- Schmidt et al.'s recursive bound on a nonnegative sequence, used to turn an inequality involving ‖u_k‖ into one involving only the accumulated errors (pp. 10, 19–20).

- Toy counterexamples that separate E[N_ε] bounds from bounds on E[F(x_k) − F*] (pp. 25–26).
- An oracle defined relative to the gradient mapping ‖D_α(y)‖ for composite problems (p. 4).
