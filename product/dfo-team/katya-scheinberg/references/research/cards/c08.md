# Batch c08 · Katya Scheinberg · paper cards

**Batch intent**: Look at how three 2025 preprints close off two long-running programmes and check which SKILL.md methods they confirm or bend. The first programme is adaptive methods with probabilistic oracles, now pushed to weaker contracts: success probability below 1/2, heavy-tailed or corrupted values, function estimates used only through differences, and nonsmooth composite objectives. The second is model-based DFO, where Powell-style geometry handling finally gets complexity bounds.
**Focus dimensions**: D1–D8, all filled because every paper is core. The emphasis is on D2 (what was changed in the classical method), D3 (the oracle contract) and D4 (the proof skeleton).

### S083 · Stochastic adaptive optimization with unreliable inputs: A unified framework for high-probability complexity analysis (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2511.19411)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Miaolan Xie · **Read**: full text, pp. 1–23. There is no appendix and no numerical section. · **Position in trajectory**: the endpoint of the group's high-probability line (JSX24, CBS24, JSX25, SX23; cited p. 5). It generalises JSX24 and recovers it as a special case (p. 22).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters through an assumption to weaken, not through a new application. Almost all earlier adaptive stochastic analyses require every oracle output to be accurate with probability above 1/2 at every iteration (p. 5). The one exception, GRVZ18, lets gradients fail with probability above 1/2 but needs exact function values (p. 5). The question is then high-probability iteration complexity when gradients are arbitrarily corrupted with probability above 1/2 and function values carry heavy-tailed noise (abstract, p. 1). Two back-of-envelope computations make the regime concrete. With 5% outliers and batch size 32, only about 19% of minibatches are clean (p. 1). With finite differences in n = 100 and 1% corrupted values, the gradient is corrupted with probability about 64% (p. 2; δ1 = 0.64, pp. 4–5). ADAM, AdaGrad and bounded-variance analyses are named as not designed for this setting (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The core idea is to use function values to make the method reliable when gradients are badly corrupted (p. 2). A generic Algorithm 1 (pp. 6–7) builds a model, takes a trial step and runs a sufficient-decrease test relaxed by a +2ε_f slack. It then does one of three things: accept and increase the step, accept and decrease it, or reject and decrease it. The paper instantiates it twice:
  - trust region: Alg. 2 is CBS24's Algorithm 1 with separate γ_inc/γ_dec (p. 8);
  - line search: Alg. 3 is JSX24's SASS plus one extra test, ‖g_k‖ ≥ ε_rej, which blocks a step increase when the gradient estimate is small (pp. 8–9).

  What changed: only the separate increase/decrease factors and the ε_rej gate. What was kept: the classical acceptance logic. The real design lever is the random walk of the step size. The analysis needs p > 1/(m+1) with m = −ln γ_inc/ln γ_dec, which is the same as p ln γ_inc + (1−p) ln γ_dec > 0, i.e. an upward drift of α_k (p. 15). A large enough increase factor therefore lets p fall below 1/2 (pp. 15, 22). §2.4 explains why shrinking the step fixes both failure causes at once: it cures overshoot, and because r(α) tightens it also demands a more accurate oracle (p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: ∇ϕ is L-Lipschitz and ϕ is bounded below; the algorithm does not know L or ϕ* (Assumption 1, p. 1). There are three oracles:
  - SZO(ε_f, q, ζ_q) bounds the expected error and its q-th centred moment, q ≥ 2 (p. 2). The sub-exponential case is written separately (p. 3).
  - CZO(ε_f, ε_c, δ0): the error is at most ε_f with probability 1−δ0 and at most ε_f+ε_c always (p. 3).
  - SFO(r(·), δ1): ‖g − ∇ϕ‖ ≤ r(α) with probability at least 1−δ1, and arbitrary otherwise (p. 4). Here r(α) = ε_g+κα for trust region and max{ε_g, min{τ,κα}‖g‖} for line search (p. 4).

  An optional weaker assumption bounds moments of function-value differences instead of values. The paper says this helps with common random numbers (eqs. (6)–(7), p. 3). Key definitions:
  - A true iteration has the gradient within r(A_k) and E_k+E_k^+ ≤ 2ε_f (Def. 1, p. 10).
  - For CZO, p ≥ 1−δ1−2δ0 (p. 10).
  - A highly unreliable environment means p < 1/2 (Def. 2, p. 10).

  Two assumptions define the algorithm class: a small true iteration must be successful (Assumption 2), and a large successful iteration must decrease ϕ by at least h(ε) minus the noise (Assumption 3) (p. 12). Stated conveniences: m is taken to be an integer (p. 14), and α0 ≥ ᾱ without loss of generality (p. 13). The reachable ε has a floor of order max{ε_g, √ε_f} (p. 15; Inequalities 1–2, pp. 18, 20).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The skeleton is deterministic counting plus two concentration events. There is no supermartingale on a combined potential: the progress measure is simply Z_k = ϕ(X_k) − ϕ* (Def. 6, p. 11). The steps:
  1. Lemma 2 (pp. 12–13) bounds the number of true iterations by the number of large iterations plus a 1/(⌊m⌋+1) share of t. It uses only the step-size dynamics: among small iterations, each successful one is matched by at least ⌊m⌋ unsuccessful ones.
  2. Lemma 3 (p. 13) bounds large unsuccessful iterations by ⌈m⌉ times the large successful ones, plus a log term.
  3. Proposition 2 (pp. 13–14) caps large successful iterations at (Z0 + accumulated noise)/h(ε).
  4. Theorem 1 (pp. 14–15) argues by contradiction on two events. A = {Σ I_k ≥ p̂t} is controlled by Azuma–Hoeffding on the submartingale Σ I_k − pt. B = {accumulated noise ≤ t(2ε_f+μ+s)}. The result is P(T_ε ≤ t) ≥ 1 − exp(−(p−p̂)²t/(2p²)) − P(B̄).
  5. The tail of B̄ depends on the oracle. Fuk–Nagaev for q-th moments gives a polynomial tail in t^(q−1) (Lemma 1, pp. 11–12; Theorem 2, p. 16). Chebyshev covers q = 2 and Bernstein the sub-exponential case (p. 16). Hoeffding for CZO gives an exponential tail (pp. 16–17).
  6. Summing the tails gives expected complexity (Cor. 1) and almost-sure convergence (Thm. 4) (p. 17).

  Instantiation lemmas verify Assumptions 2–3 for trust region (Lemmas 4–5, pp. 18–19, reusing CBS24 Lemmas 4.1 and 4.3) and for line search (Lemmas 6–7, pp. 20–21). Both give h(ε) = O(ε²) and hence E[T_ε] = O(ε⁻²) (pp. 20, 22). Reader's note: Lemma 1 is stated for independent summands (p. 11). The text applies it to the per-iteration noise terms Y_k (p. 15) without an explicit independence or martingale argument; the cited source [FGL17] is about martingales (p. 23).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The paper has no numerical section: all 23 pages were read, and §5 is the concluding-remarks section (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues the group's own line (p. 5):
  - foundations: CS17 and GRVZ18;
  - line search: BCS21, PS20, JSX24 and JSX25;
  - trust region: BCMS19 and CBS24;
  - ARC: SX23;
  - SQP and quasi-Newton: BXZ25 and MWX23.

  Earlier work is criticised by restating its probability-above-1/2 assumption, not by attacking its results (p. 5). GRVZ18 is singled out as the only exception, and its key limitation (exact function values) is named (p. 5). Popular ML methods get one sentence of direct criticism (p. 2). The paper recovers JSX24 when p > 1/2 and the noise is sub-exponential (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: There are three bold-labelled contribution bullets, two of them with a first-ever claim (p. 6). The core idea is stated in one sentence on p. 2, before any formalism. Numeric motivating examples come before the definitions (pp. 1–2, 4–5). After the general theorem, a discussion paragraph attached to Theorem 1 turns the condition p > p_m into a parameter-setting guideline and admits that p is usually unknown (p. 15); the concluding remarks repeat this (p. 22). Hedging is sparse. The proofs of Lemmas 2–3 are short and partly verbal (pp. 12–13).
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: This is a theory and algorithm-framework paper. It unifies trust region and line search, handles p < 1/2 with heavy-tailed function values, and shows that the tail of the stopping time is exponential or polynomial depending on the zeroth-order oracle (p. 6). Limitations are concrete:
  - p is usually unknown and has to be replaced by a conservative lower bound (pp. 15, 22);
  - the convergence neighbourhood is fixed by the oracle bias and noise (pp. 15, 22).

  The conclusion also argues that convergence to the true risk says something about generalisation (p. 22). There is no future-work list. Experiments and second-order or constrained variants are not discussed (not in the text).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: three explicit oracle contracts, each with accuracy r(α) tied to the step parameter and a failure probability (pp. 2–5). The irreducible parts ε_f and ε_g set the reachable ε (p. 15).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the probability threshold is not a fixed 1/2-type number. It is p > 1/(m+1), set by the step-size update factors (pp. 15, 22), so the designer can move it.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: both instantiations are earlier algorithms with one change each. The trust region is CBS24 up to the factors; the line search is SASS plus the ε_rej gate (p. 8).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: stopping time T_ε, indicators I_k/Θ_k/U_k, high-probability tails and O(ε⁻²) (pp. 10–22).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the proof uses a plain progress measure with deterministic counting and Azuma–Hoeffding, not a combined potential with a supermartingale (pp. 11–15).
- Heuristic 11 ✅ evidence: the complexity tail follows the oracle tail. It is polynomial for q-th moments and exponential for CZO or sub-exponential noise (pp. 16–17).
- New-pattern candidate: unified framework by abstract assumptions. State two algorithm-level assumptions, prove the general theorem once, then verify the assumptions for each method (pp. 12, 18–21).
- New-pattern candidate: an asymmetric step-size update lowers the reliability threshold (pp. 15, 22).
- New-pattern candidate: a contract on function-value differences rather than values (eqs. (6)–(7), p. 3). It also appears in S098.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To tolerate oracles that are wrong more often than right, make the step-size increase factor larger than the decrease factor. Then check p ln γ_inc + (1−p) ln γ_dec > 0 for a conservative lower bound on p (pp. 15, 22).
- Prove complexity by counting instead of with a supermartingale:
  - classify iterations as true/false, successful/unsuccessful and large/small;
  - use the step-size dynamics to bound small successful steps by small unsuccessful ones;
  - use the objective decrease to bound large successful ones;
  - intersect a concentration event on Σ I_k with a concentration event on the accumulated noise (Lemma 2 to Theorem 1, pp. 12–15).
- Keep the noise-accumulation event separate. Each new noise model then changes only one concentration inequality: Fuk–Nagaev, Chebyshev, Bernstein or Hoeffding (pp. 15–17).
- Add a gate that blocks step-size increases when ‖g_k‖ is below a threshold tied to the oracle bias, so that noise-driven steps are not rewarded (Alg. 3, pp. 8–9).
- Writing move: justify an unusual regime (here p < 1/2) with one line of arithmetic. Examples are 0.95^32 ≈ 19% clean minibatches and 0.99^101 ≈ 36% clean finite-difference stencils (pp. 1–2).

**Quotes**:
- "The key idea is to leverage function value information to ensure reliability when gradient estimates are severely corrupted." (p. 2)
- "In such a regime, the local model provided to the algorithm is more likely to be misleading than helpful in any given iteration." (p. 10)

### S088 · On Complexity of Model-Based Derivative-Free Methods (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2510.14935)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Chaudhry · **Read**: full text, pp. 1–20. There is no appendix and no numerical section. · **Position in trajectory**: the first complexity theory for Powell-style geometry-correcting trust-region methods (p. 1). It is the 2025 paper that SKILL.md Method 4 and Heuristic 8 cite. It builds on the IDFO book [9] and on the group's stochastic trust-region analyses [4, 5, 7] (pp. 3, 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap between practice and theory. Model-based trust-region methods pioneered by Powell are described as arguably the most effective in practice (p. 1), and they ship in MATLAB and SciPy (abstract, p. 1). Yet their complexity analysis lags behind (p. 1). The existing convergence theory relies on a criticality step, which departs from the main idea of the algorithms (p. 1). The problem is posed as counting the function evaluations needed to reach ‖∇ϕ‖ ≤ ε, with explicit dependence on n and ε (pp. 2, 6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Four moves:
  1. Replace the criticality step with the acceptance condition ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2Δ_k, imported from stochastic trust region (pp. 2–3). The radius then has two roles: step size and control of gradient accuracy (p. 2).
  2. Algorithm 2 is a geometry-correcting method in Powell's spirit (p. 7). On success it drops the farthest interpolation point. On failure it takes the first applicable step: add a point, replace a far point, or replace a bad point (the maximiser of |ℓ_j|, only if it exceeds Λ). Only if the geometry is already good does it shrink Δ. So Δ is never reduced on a geometry-correcting iteration (p. 7).
  3. Choose Λ = 1+O(1/n) (Theorem 4.5, p. 11).
  4. Run the same method in Haar-random q-dimensional subspaces (Alg. 3, p. 14; Alg. 4, p. 19). No new Q_k is drawn on geometry-correcting iterations, so those iterations involve no randomness (p. 18).

  What was kept: the standard ratio test, the radius update and fraction of Cauchy decrease (pp. 2–3). The paper is built as a ladder of methods: finite-difference trust region, then geometry-correcting, then a general polynomial basis, then subspaces (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: ϕ is C¹ with an L-Lipschitz gradient and bounded below (Assumptions 1.1–1.2, p. 2). The zeroth-order oracle is exact or has bounded noise |f−ϕ| ≤ ε_f (p. 2; §2.1, p. 5). Other assumptions:
  - fraction of Cauchy decrease and a bounded model Hessian (Assumption 2.1, p. 3);
  - exact reductions, assumed only temporarily (Assumption 2.3, p. 3);
  - the fully-linear model definition (Def. 2.2, p. 3);
  - without loss of generality, Y_k is poised or can be completed, and L ≥ ΛC2/2 (pp. 8, 11);
  - L is treated as dimension-independent (footnote 2, p. 6).

  The subspace sections assume an exact oracle (p. 13) and a subspace that is well aligned with probability θ > 1/2 (Assumption 6.8, p. 17). Haar sampling makes that attainable (Lemma 6.7, p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - (a) Deterministic trust-region counting (pp. 3–4). Lemma 2.4: a small radius, Δ_k ≤ C1‖∇ϕ‖, with a fully-linear model forces success. Lemma 2.5: success decreases ϕ by at least C2Δ². Hence Δ_k ≥ γC1ε (Lemma 2.6). Successful iterations number at most (f0−f*)/(C2(γC1ε)²) (Lemma 2.7), and unsuccessful ones at most the successful ones plus a log term (Lemma 2.8). This gives O(ε⁻²) iterations (Thm 2.9, p. 4). With noise, ε has a floor of √(2ε_f/(γ²τC2C̄1²)) (Thm 2.10, p. 5).
  - (b) Finite-difference models cost O(n²ε⁻²) evaluations with δ = Δ_k. With δ = Δ_k/√n they cost O(nε⁻²), but only when ε ≳ √(nε_f) (p. 6).
  - (c) Geometry-correcting steps are counted with a combinatorial potential (Thm 3.6, p. 8). The potential |I_k| is the number of points with norm Δ_k that are orthogonal to all the others. It never decreases and grows by one on every correcting step, so there are at most 3n oracle calls between non-correcting iterations. With the book's error bound this gives O(n³ε⁻²) (p. 9).
  - (d) An improved error bound (Thm 4.3, pp. 9–10): ‖∇ϕ−g‖ ≤ ½√n·LΔ·√(n(Λ²−1)+2). The proof splits the error into ‖Y^{−T}D‖ times a Taylor term. It bounds ‖A‖ through tr(A) + tr(A⁻¹) ≤ n(Λ²+1), using λ + 1/λ ≥ 2. With Λ = 1+O(1/n) this gives O(n²ε⁻²), the same as finite differences (Thm 4.5, p. 11). The noisy version adds a term √n·2ε_fΛ/Δ (Thm 4.4, pp. 10–11).
  - (e) Tightness (Thm 4.6, pp. 11–12). An explicit Λ-poised set U = √A with A = (1+ε)I − ε11ᵀ, inverted by Sherman–Morrison, applied to ϕ = ½uᵀu, has error at least ½√n·√(n(Λ²−1)+1).
  - (f) General polynomial basis (Thm 5.1, pp. 12–13). A volume-ratio potential with Hadamard's inequality bounds consecutive correcting calls by O(p log p).
  - (g) Subspaces. The squared projection norm under Haar sampling is Beta(q/2,(n−q)/2), and Paley–Zygmund gives P(well aligned) ≥ 243/443 > 1/2 (Lemma 6.7, p. 16). Expected complexity follows the Cartis–Scheinberg 2018 [7] process argument with indicators I_k, A_k and B_k: E[K_ε] ≤ 2θ/(2θ−1)² times the deterministic count (Lemmas 6.9–6.12, Thm 6.13, pp. 17–18). The total is O(nq/ε²) evaluations for finite differences in subspaces (p. 18) and O(nq^α/ε²) for the geometry-correcting subspace method (p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. There is no numerical section; §6 goes straight to the conclusions (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The DFO taxonomy on p. 1 has three classes: direct search, simplex-gradient/finite-difference methods, and model-based trust region. The first two are called simple to implement and analyse, and popular in ML, and their analyses rest on guaranteed descent (p. 1).
  - The IDFO book [9], which Scheinberg co-authored, is criticised for its criticality step (pp. 1–2). The paper improves the book's Λ-poisedness gradient bound, which leads to O(n³ε⁻²), to one leading to O(n²ε⁻²) (Thms 4.3–4.5, pp. 9–11).
  - Cartis–Roberts [6] (JL sketching, O(n²/ε²)) and Kozak et al. [11] (finite differences in subspaces, O(n/ε²)) are set up as the subspace competitors (p. 13).
  - Moré–Wild [13] is cited for practical effectiveness (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract states the parity claim, flagged as for the first time (p. 1). The organisation paragraph promises to show how the complexity analysis of the basic method adapts to the more sophisticated one (p. 2). The paper keeps that promise: later algorithms reuse Lemmas 2.4–2.5 unchanged (pp. 7, 14–15, 18). Theorems are followed by a one-line interpretation, e.g. that the geometry-correcting method loses nothing but linear algebra (p. 11). There are visible drafting traces: a leaked LaTeX label, assum:exact (p. 3), a missing letter in the conclusion (p. 19), and a same-as/than slip in the abstract (p. 1). Open issues are deferred to future research (pp. 13, 18).
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: Theory contribution:
  - the first complexity bounds for Powell-style geometry handling without a criticality step;
  - matching the finite-difference bound, O(n²ε⁻²), with a tightness example;
  - improved to O(nq/ε²) in subspaces (pp. 11–12, 18).

  Limitations are concrete:
  - the subspace analysis needs an exact oracle, because Δ_k is only bounded below often enough, not always (pp. 13, 18);
  - the κ_eg bounds for a general basis may not be optimal (p. 13);
  - underdetermined models (|Y_k| < p) are not analysed (p. 9).

  Future work:
  - noisy subspace analysis (p. 18);
  - combining JL embeddings with the new Λ-poised bounds (footnote 3, p. 16);
  - other ways to generate subspaces (p. 17);
  - practical refinements such as skipping ρ_k when ‖g_k‖ ≤ η2Δ_k and adding non-random directions (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (strong):
  - geometry is paid for only on unsuccessful iterations, and Δ never shrinks without certified geometry (p. 7);
  - the cost is bounded: 3n calls, or O(p log p) for a general basis (pp. 8, 12–13);
  - geometry is randomised through subspaces (pp. 13–19).
- Research Taste 5 and Method 2 step 4 ✅ evidence: the minimal safeguard is backed by a negative result. Theorem 4.6 shows that the Λ-dependence of the error bound cannot be improved, so Λ must be 1+O(1/n) to keep the n-dependence (p. 11).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the standard trust-region method is kept; only the criticality step is swapped for ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2Δ_k (pp. 2–3).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence for the subspace part: a stochastic process with θ > 1/2 and an expected stopping-time bound (pp. 17–18).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: only an expected bound is given; Heuristic 6 is not applied (p. 18).
- Heuristic 8 ✅ evidence (pp. 13–19).
- Research Taste mark 2 ✅ evidence: parity with finite-difference trust region (pp. 1, 11).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the estimators (finite differences with δ = Δ or Δ/√n, and interpolation) are compared only through derived complexity, with no experiments (pp. 6, 11). Taste mark 4 is not practised in this preprint.
- New-pattern candidate: back-port stochastic-analysis devices to simplify deterministic DFO (pp. 2–3).
- New-pattern candidate: count safeguard steps with a combinatorial potential, such as a growing orthogonal set or a volume ratio (pp. 8, 12–13).
- New-pattern candidate: keep safeguard iterations free of randomness, so the probabilistic analysis runs only on the other iterations (p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If a method needs a criticality loop or another safeguard loop, try replacing it with the acceptance condition ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2Δ_k. The radius then controls both the step size and the model accuracy (pp. 2–3).
- Count safeguard iterations with a monotone combinatorial potential. Two examples from the paper:
  - the number of mutually orthogonal points of norm Δ;
  - the ratio of the interpolation simplex's volume to the maximum volume, via Hadamard's inequality (pp. 8, 12–13).
- Bound ‖A‖ through tr(A) + tr(A⁻¹) and λ + 1/λ ≥ 2, instead of multiplying the poisedness constant by n. This takes the error factor from O(nΛ) to O(√n) when Λ = 1+O(1/n) (p. 10).
- Pair each upper bound with a construction that attains it, e.g. a Sherman–Morrison family of Λ-poised sets on a quadratic (pp. 11–12).
- To show random subspaces are well aligned with probability above 1/2, use the Beta(q/2,(n−q)/2) law of the projection and Paley–Zygmund. Then plug the result into the θ > 1/2 process analysis (pp. 16–18).
- Draw a new random subspace only on iterations that change the radius. The geometry-fixing iterations then stay deterministic and can be counted separately (p. 18).

**Quotes**:
- "We establish, for the first time, that these methods can have the same worst case complexity than any other known DFO method." (p. 1)
- "Thus nothing is lost by employing Algorithm 2, aside from additional linear algebra costs of maintaining the Lagrange polynomials." (p. 11)

### S098 · ProxSTORM--A Stochastic Trust-Region Algorithm for Nonsmooth Optimization (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2510.03187)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Robert J. Baraldi, Aurya Javeed, Drew P. Kouri (Sandia National Laboratories) · **Read**: full text, pp. 1–30, including Appendix A (pp. 29–30); references (pp. 31–33) skimmed. The text file is arXiv v2, dated 26 Jun 2026 (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: generalises STORM (Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg 2018 [15]) and its complexity analysis (BCMS19 [11]) to composite objectives f+ϕ. It is a collaboration with the Sandia authors of the deterministic inexact proximal trust-region method [5] (pp. 1–2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a merger of two frameworks, each missing what the other has:
  - Baraldi–Kouri's deterministic proximal trust region [5] requires inexactness bounds that go to zero deterministically. In practice, inexactness is stochastic: it comes from sampling or from sporadic failures of simulation subroutines (p. 2).
  - STORM handles stochastic models, but only for smooth f (p. 2).

  The problem is min f(x)+ϕ(x), with f L-smooth and possibly nonconvex, and ϕ convex, nonsmooth and with an exactly computable prox (pp. 1–2). The applications named are data science, operations research and PDE-constrained optimisation (abstract, p. 1). A second, internal entry: re-examining STORM shows that only function differences need to be estimated (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: ProxSTORM (Alg. 1, p. 4) is STORM with three changes:
  1. the proximal-gradient step h_k replaces ∇m_k, both for stationarity and in the η2 test (pp. 3–5);
  2. ϕ is kept exact in the trust-region subproblem and in pred/ared (p. 4);
  3. a generalised fraction-of-Cauchy-decrease condition (S2) (p. 3).

  It also has a cheap pre-check that skips the step computation when ‖h_k‖ < η2δ_k (p. 4). Unchanged: STORM's acceptance ratio and radius logic. The update uses a single γ with a δ_max cap, a restriction the authors call a matter of convenience (pp. 4–5). The analysis change: the computed-reduction contract is written on |ared − cred| ≤ η·pred instead of on two separate function values with Δ² accuracy (Assumption 4, p. 7). Subproblems are solved with the spectral proximal gradient solver from [5] (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Assumption 1: ϕ is proper, closed and convex; f is L-smooth on dom ϕ; f+ϕ is bounded below (pp. 1–2). The prox can be evaluated exactly (p. 2).
  - Assumption 2: the models have uniformly bounded curvature (p. 6).
  - Assumption 3: P(I_k|F_{k−1}) ≥ α, where I_k = {‖∇M_k − ∇f‖ ≤ κ_grad Δ_k} (pp. 6–7).
  - Assumption 4: P(J_k|F_{k−1/2}) ≥ β, where J_k = {|A_k − C_k| ≤ ηP_k}. A half-step filtration separates the model's randomness from the randomness of the computed reduction (pp. 5–7).
  - Assumption 5: on successful steps with an inaccurate reduction, the expected increase of the objective is at most c1Δ² + c2‖h‖Δ. It holds automatically in the smooth STORM setting and is implied by E|C_k − A_k| ≤ c3Δ² (Prop. 2, p. 8).
  - Assumption 6: α and β are close enough to 1 that (32) and (34) hold, which implies αβ > 1/2 (pp. 15, 20).

  Admitted gap: numerical values of α and β are hard to determine (Remark 12, p. 16). §5 additionally assumes unbiased gradients with variance σ_g², C² functions with Lipschitz Hessians, and common random numbers (pp. 21–23).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The skeleton is STORM's, with potential Ψ_k = ν(f+ϕ)(X_k) + (1−ν)Δ_k² (p. 11). Pointwise bounds are proved event by event:
  - a rejected step or an accurate reduction gives a decrease of −c5Δ² (Prop. 5, pp. 11–12);
  - an accurate model gives an increase of at most c6Δ² (Prop. 6, p. 12);
  - an accurate model, a large ‖h‖/Δ and a successful step together give a decrease of −νc4‖h‖Δ (Cor. 7, p. 12);
  - when both oracles are inaccurate, Assumption 5 gives an expected-increase bound, which the authors liken to CVaR (Prop. 8, Remark 9, p. 13).

  Lemma 10 (pp. 13–15) splits on B_k = {‖h(X_k)‖ ≥ ζΔ_k}. It offsets the positive ‖h‖Δ term of the worst case with the negative one of the best case (probability weight αβ), then picks α and β near 1. The result is E[Ψ_{k+1} − Ψ_k | F_{k−1}] ≤ −ΘΔ_k² (p. 13). Consequences:
  - E[ΣΔ_k²] < ∞, hence Δ_k → 0 almost surely (Cor. 16, p. 17);
  - liminf ‖h‖ = 0 almost surely, by comparing the ±1 submartingale W_k = Σ[2·1(I∩J) − 1] with log_γ Δ_k (Thm 19, pp. 17–19);
  - lim ‖h‖ = 0, using the fact that the prox-gradient map is Lipschitz, ‖h(y) − h(x)‖ ≤ (2/r + L)‖y − x‖, by nonexpansiveness (App. A, pp. 29–30);
  - E[T_ε] ≤ αβ/(2αβ−1)·Ψ̃0/(Θδ_ε²) + 1 = O(ε⁻²), from BCMS19's stopping-time theorem (Thm 21, Prop. 22, pp. 19–20).

  Sample complexity (§5) comes from a Taylor expansion of ared − cred, with the same minibatch used at both points. It bounds E|A_k − C_k| by σ_gΔ/√N + O(Δ³) + (a Hessian-variance term)·Δ²/√N (eq. (44), p. 22). Markov's inequality then shows that N = O(Δ⁻²) samples suffice, where STORM needed O(Δ⁻⁴) (pp. 9, 23). Remark 14 turns the constants into complexity dependencies: Θ ∝ 1−ν ∝ η1η2, and the complexity grows with ζ² (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two case studies, each with 100 independent realisations and histograms of the final test error (pp. 24–27).
  - (1) Neural network on HIGGS (11M samples, 28 features): a two-layer sigmoid network with h = 10, binary cross-entropy loss and ℓ1 regularisation with λ = 10⁻² (pp. 23–24). A fixed minibatch of n = 100 per iteration is shared by the gradient, the Hessian and cred. The authors list this as a departure from the theory (items i–ii, p. 24). Parameters are in Table 2 (η1 = 0.5, η2 = 5×10⁻⁵, δ0 = 10, γ = 5; p. 24). The baseline is Adam with the same seeds and the zero-subgradient convention for ℓ1. Adam runs 150 iterations against ProxSTORM's 50, to compensate for up to 2 Hessian products per ProxSTORM iteration (p. 24). The authors report where ProxSTORM loses: Adam gets lower test error as λ → 0 (p. 24). They also argue that a work-based handicap is unfair when sampling is the bottleneck, as in PDE solves (p. 24).
  - (2) Stochastic topology optimisation: compliance under a random load angle, with volume and box constraints as the indicator ϕ, on a 100×200 mesh, so d = 20,000 (pp. 25–26). The baseline is the deterministic proximal trust region [5] on a fixed-sample SAA, with 3 samples per iteration and 20 iterations (pp. 26–27). Figure 4 plots the median trust-region radius with min/max bands over the 100 trials (p. 28).

  Budgets are compared by iteration count with a work adjustment. There are no plots against function evaluations or sample counts.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Extends STORM [15] and BCMS19 [11], and generalises Baraldi–Kouri [5] (pp. 1–2).
  - Contrasts itself with stochastic SQP [10, 18] and interior-point methods [17, 18]. It covers only convex constraints, but claims its assumptions are much simpler and easier to enforce (p. 2).
  - Among stochastic trust-region methods [13, 20], it picks STORM as the most general framework and the one with expected complexity bounds (pp. 2–3).
  - Relates its reduction condition to the deterministic condition (16) of Kouri et al. [25] and notes that a probabilistic version of (16) could be used instead (pp. 8–9).
  - Cites Rinaldi–Vicente–Zeffiro [28] and Bellavia et al. [8, 9] as STORM follow-ups (p. 9), and leaves total sample complexity to JSX25 [23] (pp. 9, 23).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract makes two claims: ProxSTORM recovers STORM when there is no nonsmooth term, and the paper improves and simplifies parts of the STORM analysis (p. 1). The introduction spends a paragraph on what the paper teaches about STORM itself (p. 3). The analysis is scaffolded for the reader:
  - Table 1 recaps the constants (p. 15);
  - Remarks 11–15 interpret them (p. 16);
  - each lemma is introduced by a sentence naming the event it covers (pp. 10–13).

  Hedging is moderate and localised. The fixed n is presented as, in a sense, a heuristic choice of α and β (p. 24). Computing α and β is described as not a hard issue in practice (p. 16). The conclusion contrasts the stochastic and deterministic trust-region regimes (Δ_k is forced to zero; Robbins–Monro-like conditions) instead of restating the results (pp. 27–28). Minor inconsistency: η ∈ (1, …) on p. 6 but (0, …) on p. 9.
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: Contributions:
  - algorithmic: ProxSTORM;
  - theoretical: almost-sure limit convergence and O(ε⁻²) expected complexity, matching STORM and deterministic proximal trust region (Remark 23, p. 20);
  - a reinterpretation of STORM: difference-based reductions, and O(Δ⁻²) samples under common random numbers (pp. 9, 23);
  - two application studies.

  Limitations are specific:
  - only convex ϕ with an exact prox (p. 2);
  - a fixed trust-region update, chosen for convenience (p. 5);
  - α and β cannot be computed in practice (p. 16);
  - the experiments deviate from the assumptions (p. 24);
  - Adam wins for small λ (p. 24);
  - the radius must go to zero, so steps are rejected infinitely often (pp. 27–28).

  Future direction: under probabilistic conditions, deterministic inexactness loops such as [5, Alg. 4] amplify the chance of an erroneous exit, and rigorous yet practical stochastic designs are left open (p. 28). There is no high-probability bound; a high-probability lower bound on Δ_k is cited from [23] (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: STORM is kept and generalised, and ProxSTORM reduces exactly to STORM when ϕ ≡ 0 (pp. 1, 5, 27).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the known, cheap nonsmooth part ϕ is kept exact in the subproblem and in pred/ared; only f is stochastic (pp. 2, 4, 7). This is Method 6 step 2 in practice.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the contract has the familiar sufficiently-accurate, sufficiently-often form (p. 2).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the function-value side is rewritten as a relative accuracy on the reduction difference, scaled by pred_k instead of Δ² (p. 7). This lowers per-iteration samples from O(Δ⁻⁴) to O(Δ⁻²) under common random numbers (pp. 9, 23).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a potential νf + (1−ν)Δ², expected decrease, and a stopping-time bound matching the deterministic O(ε⁻²) (pp. 11–20).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: there is no high-probability upgrade, only expected complexity and almost-sure convergence (pp. 17–20). The high-probability radius bound is left to [23] (p. 23).
- Research Taste mark 4 ✅ evidence: the theory comes with two numerical case studies of 100 realisations each, including a case the method loses (pp. 23–28).
- New-pattern candidate: a contract on function-value differences, shared with S083 (pp. 3, 7, 21–23).
- New-pattern candidate: generalise and recover. The new method must reduce to the old one, and the rework is used to simplify and sharpen the old analysis (pp. 1, 3, 7; Remark 15, p. 16). S083 does the same when it recovers JSX24 (p. 22).
- New-pattern candidate: list the deviations between theory and experiments explicitly (p. 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Put the function-estimate contract on the reduction difference, |ared − cred| ≤ η·pred_k, not on two separate values with Δ² accuracy. With common random numbers, a Taylor expansion gives E|A−C| = O(σΔ/√N), so N = O(Δ⁻²) samples suffice (pp. 7, 21–23).
- Use a half-step filtration F_{k−1/2}. The model's randomness and the reduction's randomness can then be conditioned separately, and their probabilities multiply to αβ (pp. 5–6, 13).
- In the expected-decrease lemma, offset the positive worst-case term (both oracles bad) with the negative best-case term (both good, weight αβ), then choose α and β close to 1 (pp. 13–15).
- For a composite problem, prove from nonexpansiveness that the prox-gradient map is (2/r + L)-Lipschitz. The smooth argument that upgrades liminf to lim then carries over unchanged (App. A, p. 30).
- Experiment protocol:
  - run 100 seeded realisations and plot histograms of the final test objective against a baseline run with the same seeds;
  - adjust the baseline's iteration budget for per-iteration work;
  - say where the baseline wins (pp. 24–27).
- Give the reader a table of all analysis constants, plus a remark translating each one into its effect on complexity (Table 1, Remark 14, pp. 15–16).
