### S143 · OPTIMA 79: Geometry in model-based algorithms for derivative-free unconstrained optimization (feature article) (OPTIMA, Mathematical Programming Society Newsletter 79, May 2009, no DOI or arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (single author, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: An expository essay written while the Scheinberg–Toint paper (S030) was still a submitted technical report (ref. [20], "Technical report, submitted, 2009", p. 6), in the same year as the IDFO book (advertised on p. 12). It is the lead article of the first issue of the redesigned Optima, of which Scheinberg was a co-editor with Caprara, under editor Lodi (imprint, p. 10). The printed affiliation is Columbia IEOR (p. 5, p. 10). **Read**: the feature article in full (pp. 1–6), including its reference list. Figure 1 (p. 5) survives in the text file only as garbled axis numbers, but its caption is readable. For the rest of the issue, see the batch header.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The essay enters DFO from the application side. Function values come from a black-box simulation or a physical experiment, and high cost plus numerical noise make finite-difference derivatives typically prohibitive (p. 1). The abstract names circuit tuning, aircraft configuration, water pipe calibration and oil reservoir modelling (p. 1).
  - The specific question is the tension between theory and codes. The Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente convergence theory "strongly depends on the model improvement steps" (Steps 1 and 5 of Algorithm 3.1). Practical codes (NEWUOA, DFO) also compute such extra points, but it is still open how necessary they are (p. 3).
  - The trigger is a practical counter-example to that theory: the Fasano–Nocedal–Morales implementation ignores geometry entirely, spends one point per iteration, seems the "least wasteful" in function evaluations and gave "quite encouraging" results (p. 4).
  - The question is then asked in one plain sentence: "So do we need to be concerned about the geometry of sample sets or not?" (p. 4).
  - The scope is declared up front. The article focuses on maintaining sample-set geometry, and "Since this is not a survey the list of references is very limited." (p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: open with the theory–practice gap in one sentence. Here, the theory needs extra steps, the practical code seemingly does not, so which is right? (p. 3–4).

- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The essay contrasts three algorithms, all written in the same five-step trust-region template.
  - **Algorithm 3.1, the CSV framework** (p. 3). It has a criticality step and a trial step with Cauchy decrease. On an unsuccessful iteration, Δ is reduced only if the model is fully linear, and is otherwise kept. Step 5 runs at least one model-improvement step whenever the model is not fully linear.
  - **Algorithm 4.1, Fasano–Nocedal–Morales** (p. 4). It has no geometry control. On success it drops the point furthest from x_k. On failure it shrinks Δ, and it replaces the furthest point with x⁺ only if x⁺ is closer to x_k than every point of Y.
  - **Algorithm 5.1, Scheinberg–Toint** (p. 4–5):
    - Step 1 builds a fully-linear model only when the model gradient is below a threshold ε_k, and then decreases ε_k.
    - On success, x⁺ replaces a far-away point or the point with the largest |ℓ_j(x⁺)|.
    - On failure, x⁺ replaces (i) a far-away point, or else (ii) a y_j with |ℓ_j(x⁺)| > Λ > 1. Only if neither exists is (iii) Δ reduced.
  - **Key insight: self-correction** (p. 4). Suppose the trial step fails. Then |f(x⁺) − m(x⁺)| must be relatively large. By the error bound (3.3), that can only happen if some ‖x⁺ − y_i‖ is large or some |ℓ_i(x⁺)| is large, and either way swapping in x⁺ improves the set (Lemma 3.3). She attributes this property to *the trust-region framework*, not to her algorithm (p. 4).
  - **What is kept and what changes.** Kept: the trust-region template, the ratio test, Cauchy decrease and the criticality step. Changed: Step 5's model-improvement step disappears, and the decision to shrink the radius is now driven by geometry evidence from the failed trial point itself rather than by certification (compare Algorithm 3.1 Step 4b, p. 3, with Algorithm 5.1 Step 4b, p. 5).
  - **Her statement of the minimum.** Geometry is necessary, but extra sample points are not needed unless the model gradient becomes small (§5 opening sentence, p. 4; see Quotes).
  - **Why it is cheap.** Maintaining the Lagrange polynomials costs the same as maintaining the quadratic model (citing Powell [16]). So apart from Step-1 iterations, each iteration costs about as much as one of Fasano et al. (p. 5).
  - **Which safeguard is irreducible.** Without Step 1, Algorithm 5.1 also fails on the counterexample, because the example's outcome does not depend on the order in which points are removed (p. 5). The criticality step is therefore the one piece of dedicated geometry work that cannot go.
  - Transferable technique: when a safeguard is expensive, find the event in which it pays for itself (here, a failed step reveals a bad point) and make the repair piggyback on that event (p. 4–5).

- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Problem (3.1) is unconstrained. ∇f exists and is Lipschitz continuous, but cannot be evaluated because it is unavailable or too costly (p. 2).
  - Fully-quadratic models additionally need a Lipschitz ∇²f and second-order conditions in Steps 1–2 (p. 3).
  - The interpolation set must be poised, with Λ > 1 in Lemma 3.3 (p. 2).
  - Convergence of Algorithm 5.1 is claimed "under the usual and reasonable conditions", which the essay does not spell out (p. 5).
  - Noise appears only as a motivation in §1 (p. 1). The algorithms all assume exact f (pp. 3–5).
  - Reader's check (my own, not in the text): as printed, (3.3) pairs the exponent d+1 with a constant M that depends only on the Lipschitz constant of ∇f (p. 2).
    - Under that assumption, the standard argument gives exponent 2 for any degree d ≥ 1: reproduce the linear Taylor polynomial and use Σℓ_j = 1.
    - Exponent 3 for d = 2 needs a Lipschitz Hessian.
    - This is an expository looseness, not a proof step here. S030 uses the squared-distance form (c01 card, S030 D4).

- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The essay proves nothing itself. It relays the devices and points to [8], [9] and [20] for the proofs (p. 3, p. 5).
  - **Lagrange representation** m(x) = Σ f(y_j) ℓ_j(x) (3.2), together with the error bound |m(x) − f(x)| ≤ M Σ‖y_j − x‖^{d+1}|ℓ_j(x)| (3.3). She calls max |ℓ_j| the "key indicator" of geometry (p. 2).
  - **Why DFO differs from Taylor.** To get better agreement, one must shrink the ball *and* find a new set that fits into it while the Lagrange bound stays at the same constant (p. 2). This is why radius reduction alone does not repair a model.
  - **Λ-poisedness** (Def. 3.2) and **Lemma 3.3**. Repeatedly swap in any point where |ℓ_j| ≥ Λ > 1; the procedure terminates finitely in a Λ-poised set (p. 2). The volume argument behind the lemma is not given here; it is in S030 (Lemma 2.4, c01 card).
  - **Abstraction layer**: fully-linear and fully-quadratic models, plus the requirement of an algorithm that, in finite effort, either certifies such a model or constructs one (p. 3). The only descent condition the first-order analysis needs is the Cauchy decrease condition (p. 3).
  - **Hand-checkable counterexample** (p. 4):
    - Setup: six points around (10, 0), Λ-poised with Λ < 2.25 in the ball of radius 2. The function f is piecewise, with a Lipschitz gradient and a discontinuous Hessian, and reduces to x₁² on x₂ = 0. A C² version exists (p. 4).
    - Run: the model is m = x₁² + αx₂². The set aligns with x₂ = 0, the model degenerates to x₁², and Algorithm 4.1 stops at (0, 0).
    - Check: differentiating the stated f for x₁ < 10 gives ∇f(0, 0) = (0, 10α) ≠ 0 (my computation from p. 4).
    - Moral, as she states it: a vanishing model gradient does not imply a vanishing true gradient unless poisedness is maintained (p. 4).
  - **Self-correction proof route** (p. 5). The rigorous version in [20] shows that only finitely many consecutive unsuccessful steps can occur while the model gradient stays bounded away from zero. The proof relies on Lemma 3.3. The conclusion is that at least one limit point is first-order stationary: a liminf-type result, with no rate.
  - **Order-independence argument** (p. 5). Because the counterexample does not depend on which point is removed, no replacement rule alone can save the method, so the criticality step is necessary.
  - Transferable technique: read the error bound backwards: *step failed ⇒ error large ⇒ (by the bound) a controllable quantity is large ⇒ a cheap repair exists* (p. 4).

- **D5 Experiments**: none of her own.
  - She reports Fasano et al.'s results only qualitatively ("quite encouraging"). She adds the caveat that they used complete quadratic models, which need (n+1)(n+2)/2 points, and that inaccurate quadratic models may still carry useful curvature information, pointing to Nocedal's column (p. 4).
  - Figure 1 only illustrates the counterexample's iterates (p. 5).
  - Algorithm 5.1 is not tested numerically (pp. 4–5).

- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **History along one axis, how each method family treats sample-set geometry** (pp. 1–2):
    - Nelder–Mead: the simplex adapts its shape to curvature, which is why it often succeeds, and it can go flat and become orthogonal to the gradient, which is why it fails (p. 1).
    - Torczon's pattern search: a fixed shape brings global convergence but loses curvature information. Direct-search convergence "essentially relies on the predetermined geometry" of a pattern (p. 2).
    - Model-based trust-region methods: pioneered by Powell and developed by Conn, Scheinberg and Toint (p. 2). It "was, thus, believed" that special geometry steps are needed throughout (p. 2).
  - **Towards Fasano–Nocedal–Morales**: she first credits their efficiency and results, then refutes their convergence with the counterexample (p. 4).
  - **Towards Powell's codes**: Algorithm 5.1 is presented as the "closest theoretically convergent algorithm" to NEWUOA [19] and DFO [6] (p. 5).
  - **Citation profile**: 7 of 23 references are her own group's ([4]–[9], [20]) and 7 are Powell's ([13]–[19]). Direct search is covered by [1] Abramson–Audet, [21]–[22] Torczon and [23] Tseng (pp. 5–6).
  - **Moré–Wild is not cited** anywhere in her reference list (pp. 5–6).
  - **Attribution correction.** Two items that SKILL.md attributes to this essay come from Nocedal's discussion column in the same issue (p. 6). One is the Moré–Wild experiments showing that Powell's method works despite very low-accuracy quadratic models. The other is the wording that "only minimal quality controls" are needed. Nocedal describes her article as one that "explores the limits of inaccuracy in model-based methods" (p. 6); these are his words about her article, not hers.

  - There is no probability yet. This is the deterministic contract that BSV 2014 later makes hold with probability p (c02 card, S018).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D6/D7] Tell a field's history along one design axis, so that each earlier method is both a success and a failure on that axis and your contribution is the next point. Here the axis is how each method family treats sample-set geometry: Nelder–Mead adapts and collapses, pattern search freezes shape and gains convergence but loses curvature, and model-based methods must maintain geometry (pp. 1–2).
- [D2] Write the algorithm against an abstract quality property, such as fully linear with ‖∇f − ∇m‖ ≤ κ_eg Δ on B(x; Δ). Add the requirement of a finite certify-or-construct procedure, and only then instantiate it (here with Lagrange polynomials). The convergence proof then no longer depends on how the model is built (p. 3).
- [D4] Read the error bound backwards. A failed step means a large model error. The bound then says a controllable quantity (distance or |ℓ_j|) is large, and that gives an improving swap at no extra function evaluation (p. 4).
- [D4] Build a hand-checkable 2-D counterexample, and verify non-stationarity at the limit by direct differentiation (∇f(0, 0) = (0, 10α)). Then argue that its outcome does not depend on any tie-breaking or replacement rule, which identifies the one safeguard that cannot be removed (pp. 4–5).
- [D2] Close a cost objection with an accounting argument. The added bookkeeping (Lagrange polynomials) costs the same as updating the model, so per-iteration cost equals the heuristic competitor's except on the rare criticality iterations (p. 5).
- [D7] Credit the competitor's efficiency and results before refuting its guarantee. Also name what their experiment actually relied on (here, full quadratic models with (n+1)(n+2)/2 points), which shows exactly where your counterexample bites (p. 4).
- [D7] Put necessity and minimal sufficiency in one contribution sentence, of the form *X is necessary for G; however, X is not needed unless Y* (p. 4).
