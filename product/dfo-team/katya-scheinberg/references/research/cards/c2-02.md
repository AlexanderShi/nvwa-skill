# Paper cards · batch c2-02 (core)

Batch intent: See how the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente theory of model quality (2008–2009) was built, and how Scheinberg then used it and carried its habits elsewhere. Three questions: (a) how the geometry measure (Λ-poisedness) and the *fully linear* abstraction were set up, i.e. the deterministic ancestor of the Method 1 oracle contract and of the Method 4 *pay only the minimum* rule; (b) how the framework was carried into a structured solver for least squares (DFLS), with a benchmark protocol, which tests Methods 5 and 6; (c) whether the same structure-first, equal-accuracy habits appear in a non-DFO ML-optimization paper (Group Lasso block coordinate descent).
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all; core batch). Extra weight on D2 (what was kept from the classical method and what was changed), D3 (which assumptions are admitted conveniences), D4 (named proof devices) and D5 (the comparison protocol).
Material roles: S025 core (IMA J. Numer. Anal. 2008) · S007 core (SIAM J. Optim. 2009) · S023 core (SIAM J. Optim. 2010) · S011 core (Math. Program. Comput. 2013).

### S025 · Geometry of sample sets in derivative-free optimization: polynomial regression and underdetermined interpolation (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2008, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drn046)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, L. N. Vicente · **Position in trajectory**: The second of the two Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente geometry papers, after "Geometry of interpolation sets" (Math. Program. 111, 2008, cited as [8]). Written at IBM Watson (p. 1). It supplies the error bounds used by the global-convergence paper S007 (cited as [10], "under final review", p. 28) and by the IDFO book ([9], "to appear", p. 28). **Read**: full text, including both small numerical studies. The reference list was skimmed. The file is the preprint, so its page numbers are manuscript pages.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a theory gap under existing practice.
  - Regression is already used in practice: implicit filtering uses regression simplex gradients and diagonal simplex Hessians, and Custódio–Vicente use regression simplex gradients (p. 1). Yet regression is rare in DFO, and the authors attribute this, "in our opinion", to missing supporting theory (p. 1).
  - Underdetermined minimum-norm models are used by the COIN-OR DFO code in early iterations and by Powell's least-Frobenius method throughout (p. 2). But "the theory in [8] does not immediately extend" (p. 2).
  - Each case gets a practical scenario. Cheap but noisy evaluations suggest regression, for its smoothing effect (p. 1). Expensive evaluations, where a full quadratic is prohibitive but a linear model is inefficient, suggest underdetermined models (p. 2).

  The problem is stated as: show that models built on over- or underdetermined sample sets satisfy Taylor-like error bounds whose constant is controlled by a geometry measure, so that they can be used in a globally convergent framework such as [10] (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: There is no algorithm. The object is a geometry measure. The key move is to redefine Lagrange polynomials so that the model is still m(x) = Σ f(yⁱ) L_i(x):
  - regression: L_j(yⁱ) = δ_ij in the least-squares sense (Def. 2.2, Lemma 2.3, p. 7–8);
  - underdetermined: the minimum-norm solution of the interpolation conditions in the natural basis (Def. 5.2, Lemma 5.3, p. 21).

  Both come from one geometric system Σ λ_i(x) φ(yⁱ) = φ(x). Its minimum-norm solution gives the regression Lagrange polynomials (Lemma 2.4, p. 9). Its least-squares solution gives the minimum-norm Lagrange polynomials, which the paper calls "a kind of duality" (p. 22). Λ-poisedness is then the bound ‖λ(x)‖ ≤ Λ on B(1) (Defs. 2.6 and 5.5, p. 9, p. 22). It is tied to the condition number of the scaled natural-basis matrix M̄, so that the pivoting algorithms of [8] can maintain it (Theorems 2.8 and 5.7, p. 11, p. 24).

  Kept from [8]: the chain Λ-poisedness → condition number → Taylor-type bound. Changed: the notion of Lagrange polynomial, and the rule that selects one model when the model is not unique. For the underdetermined case the paper first considers the "best sub-basis" model, whose columns minimize Λ (Def. 5.1, p. 19). It then rejects that model with a perturbation example and adopts the minimum-norm model (p. 19–20). The natural basis is fixed by argument, with four reasons (Remark 5.1, p. 23).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Linear regression bounds: f is C¹ with ∇f Lipschitz (γ_L) on an open set containing B(Δ) (Theorem 3.1, p. 15). Quadratic bounds: f is C² with ∇²f Lipschitz (γ_Q) (Theorem 3.2, p. 15; Theorem 5.8, p. 25).
  - Normalizations called without loss of generality: the smallest ball enclosing Y is centred at the origin and scaled to radius 1 (p. 10), and y⁰ = 0, justified because the centre is usually the current iterate (p. 14).
  - Degree is restricted to d ≤ 2. The constant θ is expected to grow "most likely, exponentially" with the degree, and optimization frameworks rarely use higher degrees (p. 13).
  - Admitted definitional conveniences. Writing Λ-poisedness with a basis φ is "a definition defect", kept because it is useful (p. 10). In the underdetermined case the constant Λ can be made as large or as close to 1 as desired by changing the basis, so only the natural basis is used (Remark 5.1, p. 23).
  - Noise appears only as a remark. With i.i.d. zero-mean noise, the regression models of the noisy and the true function converge pointwise as the number of points grows; the proof is deferred to the book (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Lemma 1.1 (max-determinant subset).** Among m > n vectors, choose the n-subset with the largest |det|. By Cramer's rule every other vector is a combination of the subset with coefficients ≤ 1, so any combination with |λ_i| ≤ Λ becomes one on the subset with |γ_i| ≤ (m−n+1)Λ (p. 4). The acknowledgement credits D. Coppersmith with help on this proof (p. 27).
  - **Basis independence** of the regression polynomial, shown by a change of basis with a nonsingular P (p. 5).
  - **Theorem 2.7.** A set Λ-poised for regression has a q₁-subset that is (p−q+1)√q₁Λ-poised for interpolation. Conversely, if any q₁-subset is Λ-poised, the whole set is Λ-poised for regression (p. 10). Consequence used later: adding points never worsens poisedness (p. 14).
  - **Theorem 2.8.** ‖Σ⁻¹‖ ≤ Λ implies √q₁Λ-poisedness. Conversely, Λ-poisedness implies ‖Σ⁻¹‖ ≤ θΛ (p. 11–13). The converse uses a norm-equivalence device: ψ(v) = max_{x∈B(1)} |vᵀφ̄(x)| is a norm, so ψ(v) ≥ γ‖v‖ for some γ > 0, and θ = 1/γ (p. 12). For quadratics, the bound max |v̂ᵀφ̄| ≥ 1/4 from [8] gives θ ≤ 4√q₁ (Lemma 2.9 and (2.13), p. 13).
  - **Scaling.** Lagrange polynomials are invariant when Y is scaled; the condition number is not. So Y ⊂ B(Δ) is rescaled to B(1) before it is measured (Lemma 2.10, p. 13–14).
  - **Error bounds.** Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 are stated without proof, as "straightforward adaptations" of [8] (p. 14). Linear regression: ‖e_g‖ ≤ (5√p₁ γ_L Λ/2)Δ (p. 15). Quadratic regression: √(p₁q₁) factors with explicit constants α_Q (p. 15–16). The √p₁ growth drives the regression-versus-interpolation trade-off: Λ can only fall as points are added, but the bound's p₁ factor rises (p. 16).
  - **Theorem 5.6.** Sub-basis and minimum-norm Λ-poisedness are equivalent up to factors √(p₁q₁)θ and (q−p+1) (p. 22–23).
  - **Theorem 5.8.** For underdetermined quadratics the bound controls only Vᵀ applied to the scaled error vector, i.e. the projection of the error onto the p-dimensional space spanned by the data. With p = q it reduces to the full-interpolation bound (p. 25–26).
  - **Counterexamples.** Example 5.1: a set that is not poised for one sub-basis is well poised for another (p. 18). Example 5.2: two sample sets that differ by ε give sub-basis models that differ in a whole coefficient, while both minimum-norm models tend to [0, b₁, b₂/2, b₂/2]ᵀ (p. 19–20).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two small studies on 2-D functions. Three functions are listed, and only f₁ is reported because the others "follow the same pattern" (p. 16).
  - Regression. p random points in the unit square plus the origin. The best-subset (BS) interpolation model uses the subset with the best condition number of M̄ and is compared with least-squares regression (LSR) on all points. Error is measured on a 0.1 lattice as maximum error (E1) and ℓ₂-summed error (E2), over 200 runs. A win counts only if one model is better by more than 0.001 in both errors (p. 16–17). Fig. 4.1 covers p₁ = 7 to 31. With 21 points, BS was worse 138 times and LSR 28 times, and the authors still conclude "no model was consistently better than the other" (p. 17).
  - Underdetermined. p₁ = 5, 4, 3, minimum-norm (MN) vs best-basis (BB), same protocol, repeated on a square of radius 0.01 (p. 26–27). At radius 0.01, MN lost 0 times for every p₁ (Fig. 5.2, p. 27).
  - Oddity. In Fig. 5.1 (unit square, p₁ = 5) the extracted cumulative errors are BB 81950 and MN 653000. This contradicts both the win counts (BB worse 133 times) and the text, which says BB was worse (p. 26–27). The text does not comment. It may be a typesetting or extraction error.
  - Framing. The authors call the examples "very limited" (p. 26) and say a separate computational study would be needed to show the benefit of regression (p. 3). The metric is approximation error of the model. No optimization solver is run.
- **D6 Positioning**: A direct continuation of the authors' own [8] (p. 1).
  - Approximation theory (Ciarlet–Raviart [4], Davis [13]) is repositioned rather than criticized. Its definitions are "ill-suited for an optimization audience" (p. 2), so the paper restates them "in simple terms" with "straightforward proofs" (p. 7).
  - Practice is credited: implicit filtering (Kelley and co-authors [2, 3]), Custódio–Vicente simplex gradients [11, 12], the COIN-OR DFO code [1], and Powell's least-Frobenius updating and NEWUOA [17, 18] (p. 1–2, p. 26).
  - Powell's definition of underdetermined Lagrange polynomials is placed as a variant of Def. 5.2. It minimizes the norm of the second-order terms only, and it requires a subset of n+1 points poised for linear interpolation (Remark 5.2, p. 23–24).
  - The noisy-DFO precursor Deng–Ferris [14] samples repeatedly at the same points. Regression on randomly selected points "may be advantageous" when repeated sampling does not reduce the noise (p. 27).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution is framed as an extension: the new models "essentially have similar properties" to interpolation models (abstract, p. 1). Hedges are frequent and explicit: "in our opinion" (p. 1); the answer "seems to be ‘neither’" (p. 17); a claimed benefit of regression for spiky functions "has not been verified experimentally" (p. 17). Definitions are motivated by tiny worked examples (Examples 5.1 and 5.2, p. 18–20). Routine proofs are omitted as adaptations of [8] (p. 14, p. 26) or included "for the sake of completeness" (p. 11). The roadmap sits at the end of §1 (p. 3). Theorems dominate; intuition comes through Remarks and short "Notice" paragraphs (p. 10, p. 23, p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory: geometry measures and Taylor-type bounds for regression and minimum-norm underdetermined models. These models become admissible in globally convergent methods as long as Λ stays uniformly bounded (p. 16). The limits are stated concretely:
  - the error-bound proofs are omitted (p. 14);
  - θ grows fast with the degree (p. 13);
  - underdetermined bounds control only a projection of the error (p. 26);
  - the numerical evidence is 2-D and at the model level only (p. 16–17, p. 26).

  Future directions:
  - algorithms that maintain a regression set directly, rather than a well-poised interpolation subset (p. 14);
  - convergence theory for minimum-Frobenius models and algorithms for Λ-poised sample sets, deferred to the book [9] (p. 26–27);
  - regression on randomly selected sample points for noisy functions (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. Λ-poisedness is the certificate of model quality, and this paper extends it to the two model classes that Method 4 Step 1 offers besides full interpolation: regression and minimum-norm underdetermined models (p. 9, p. 22). The regression-versus-interpolation discussion is a trade-off between geometry and the number of points, and it ends without a winner (p. 16–17).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence for the **stated** side (co-authored). If the sparsity of the derivatives is known, "this advantage can be exploited trivially" by deleting columns, and partial separability [5] is cited for a more sophisticated version (p. 18). This adds to S004 (p. 17 there).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (deterministic precursor). Noise is to be handled by more sample points (regression) instead of repeated sampling, backed by a pointwise-consistency remark (p. 17, p. 27). There is no probability and no per-iteration accuracy requirement.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (model-level precursor). Competing constructions (best subset vs regression; best basis vs minimum norm) are compared on identical sample sets, with win counts under two error measures (p. 16–17, p. 26–27). Approximation quality is compared, not function evaluations inside a solver.
- New-pattern candidate: *approximation-theory concept → computable optimization certificate*. Simplify the definition, then prove it is within constants of a quantity an algorithm can monitor (the condition number of the scaled Vandermonde-type matrix) (p. 2, p. 7, p. 10–14, p. 24).
- New-pattern candidate: *perturbation counterexample to choose a definition* (Example 5.2, p. 19–20). It is related to *counterexample-first assumption audit* and *defend a nonstandard result type with toy counterexamples*.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D4] If your quality measure depends on the basis (a condition number) and the meaningful one does not (Λ, the Lebesgue constant), rescale to the unit ball and prove the two agree up to constants in both directions. Any algorithm that controls the computable measure then controls the meaningful one (Theorems 2.8 and 5.7, Lemma 2.10, p. 11–14, p. 24).
- [D4] Max-determinant subset plus Cramer's rule: a reusable lemma that turns a bound on an over-determined representation into a bound on a square subsystem, at the price of a factor (m−n+1) (Lemma 1.1, p. 4).
- [D4] Norm-equivalence trick: max_{x∈B(1)} |vᵀφ(x)| is a norm on coefficient vectors, which converts bounds on polynomial values into bounds on coefficients. Then compute the constant explicitly for the case you use (θ ≤ 4√q₁ for quadratics, p. 12–13).
- [D2/D7] Choose between two candidate definitions with an ε-perturbation example that shows one of them is discontinuous in the data (Example 5.2, p. 19–20).
- [D5] Model-level comparison protocol: the same random sample sets for both constructions, 200 runs, a win declared only when better by more than 0.001 in both a max and an ℓ₂ error, then the same test on a 100× smaller region to probe the asymptotic regime (p. 17, p. 26–27).
- [D3] When a bound can only control part of the error, say which part. Here it is the projection onto the span of the data (Theorem 5.8 and the paragraph after it, p. 25–26).

**Quotes**:
- "Approximation theory definitions in their original form are ill-suited for an optimization audience." (p. 2)
- "Hence, we see that the sub-basis approach suffers from a lack of robustness with respect to small perturbations in the sample set." (p. 20)

### S007 · Global convergence of general derivative-free trust-region algorithms to first-and second-order critical points (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2009, DOI 10.1137/060673424)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. R. Conn, L. N. Vicente · **Position in trajectory**: The convergence-framework companion of the geometry papers (S025 and [4]), written at IBM Watson (p. 1). It defines the **fully linear / fully quadratic** model classes and the **criticality step** that later stochastic work makes probabilistic (BSV 2014, card S018). The results are asymptotic only. Complexity for this class came much later (cards S088, S104). **Read**: full text (no appendix). The reference list was skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a split in the literature. On one side are DFO trust-region methods that target practical performance but had no convergence theory (Marazzi–Nocedal wedge [7], Powell [12]). On the other are methods with global convergence "at the expense of practicality" (Conn–Gould–Toint [2], Conn–Scheinberg–Toint 1997 [3]) (p. 2). The paper aims to bridge the two (p. 2). The problem is stated as global convergence to first- and second-order stationary points for a class of DFO trust-region methods, whose models need only satisfy Taylor-type bounds, with an analysis "independent of the sampling techniques" (abstract, p. 1). The practical features to be covered are listed up front (p. 2–3, p. 12):
  - one trust region instead of Powell's two;
  - acceptance on simple decrease;
  - models that need not be fully linear at every iteration;
  - at most one sample point replaced per iteration.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key abstraction is a *fully linear class* of models (Def. 3.1, p. 7–8). It has two parts:
  - error bounds scaled by the radius: ‖∇f − ∇m‖ ≤ κ_eg Δ and |f − m| ≤ κ_ef Δ² on B(x; Δ);
  - a *model-improvement algorithm* that, in a finite number of steps uniformly bounded in x and Δ, either certifies that a model is fully linear or produces one.

  *Fully quadratic* adds the Hessian and one more power of Δ (Def. 3.3, p. 9). Algorithm 4.1 (p. 13–14) has five steps:
  1. Criticality step: if ‖g‖ ≤ c, make the model fully linear on a radius ≤ μ‖g‖, shrinking geometrically by α (Algorithm 4.2), then reset Δ_k = min{max{Δ̃_k, β‖g̃_k‖}, Δ_k^icb}.
  2. A step with a fraction of Cauchy decrease.
  3. Accept if ρ_k ≥ η₁, or if ρ_k ≥ η₀ and the model is fully linear. With η₀ = 0 this is acceptance on simple decrease.
  4. If ρ_k < η₁, certify or improve the model.
  5. The radius may grow after a successful iteration. It shrinks only if the model is fully linear, and is otherwise left unchanged.

  Kept from derivative-based trust region: the Cauchy decrease, the ratio test and the radius logic (p. 5, p. 14). Changed, and only where the lack of derivatives forces it: the radius shrinks only for a certified model; the criticality step ties Δ to ‖g‖ and so forces Δ → 0 (p. 2, p. 18); model-improving iterations change neither x nor Δ (p. 15). Iterations fall into four types: successful, acceptable, model-improving, unsuccessful (p. 15). The second-order version replaces ‖g‖ by σ^m = max{‖g‖, −λ_min(H)}, requires a fraction of both the Cauchy and the eigenstep decrease, and forces a radius increase only when Δ_k < βσ^m_k (Algorithm 6.1, p. 21–22). The design motive is to stay close to practical codes while keeping the theory (p. 2–3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f is C¹ with ∇f Lipschitz on the enlarged level set L_enl(x₀) = ∪_{x∈L(x₀)} B(x; Δ_max). The enlargement is needed because sampling can leave the level set (Assumption 3.1, p. 7).
  - Second order: f is C² with ∇²f Lipschitz on L_enl (Assumption 3.2, p. 9). f is bounded below (Assumption 5.1, p. 16).
  - Model Hessians are uniformly bounded (Assumption 5.2), assumed "For simplicity of the presentation" (p. 16): an admitted convenience.
  - Steps: fraction of Cauchy decrease (Assumption 2.1, p. 5); Cauchy plus eigenstep decrease (Assumption 2.2, p. 6).
  - Constants are enlarged without loss of generality so that a model fully linear on a small ball is fully linear on every larger ball (Lemmas 3.2 and 3.4, p. 8–10).
  - Admitted looseness of the abstraction: it admits impractical classes such as M = {f}, or finite-difference Taylor models rebuilt from scratch at every x and Δ (p. 8).
  - Scope: global convergence only, no local rates. Fully linear models with n+1, 2n+1 or (n+1)(n+2)/2−1 points are therefore treated the same (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The proof skeleton, in order:
  1. **Lemma 3.4 (monotonicity in Δ).** Fully quadratic on B(x; Δ̄) implies fully quadratic on B(x; Δ) for all Δ ≥ Δ̄ with the same constants, proved with the integral mean-value theorem along the ray (p. 9–10).
  2. **Lemma 5.1.** The criticality loop is finite unless ∇f(x_k) = 0, because ‖∇f‖ ≤ (κ_eg + 1/μ) α^{i−1} Δ_k^icb for all i (p. 15–16). It repeats [3, Lemma 5.iii].
  3. **Lemma 5.2 (key lemma).** If the model is fully linear and Δ_k ≤ min{1/κ_bhm, κ_fcd(1−η₁)/(4κ_ef)}·‖g_k‖, the iteration is successful, since |ρ_k − 1| ≤ 4κ_ef Δ_k² / (κ_fcd ‖g_k‖ Δ_k) ≤ 1 − η₁ (p. 16–17).
  4. **Lemma 5.3.** If ‖g_k‖ ≥ κ₁, then Δ_k ≥ κ₂, because model-improving iterations never shrink Δ (p. 17).
  5. **Lemma 5.4.** With finitely many successes, lim ∇f = 0, because at most N consecutive model-improving iterations can occur (p. 17–18).
  6. **Lemma 5.5.** Δ_k → 0. The criticality step gives ‖g_k‖ ≥ min{c, μ⁻¹Δ_k}. The decreases on successful iterations are summable, and every other Δ is at most γ_inc times the last successful one (p. 18). The acknowledgement says this lemma was inserted at Powell's suggestion (p. 28).
  7. **Lemmas 5.6–5.7.** liminf ‖g_k‖ = 0, and model stationarity transfers to true stationarity through the criticality step: ‖∇f‖ ≤ (κ_eg μ + 1)‖g‖ (p. 18–19).
  8. **Theorem 5.9 (lim-type).** A Thomas-style argument over subsequences {k_i}, {ℓ_i}: the path length is bounded by the function decrease (p. 19–21). Remark 5.1 states that x_{k+1} = x_k at model-improving iterations is needed only for this lim-type result (p. 21).

  Second order: an eigenvalue-perturbation bound |λ_min(∇²f) − λ_min(∇²m)| ≤ κ_eh Δ (Proposition 7.1, p. 23), then |σ − σ^m| ≤ κ_σ Δ (Lemma 7.2, p. 24). The lim-type proof bounds the path by a geometric sum γ_inc/(γ_inc−1)·Δ_{j*}, using the forced increases on S₊ (Theorem 7.11, p. 27–28). There is no complexity bound.
- **D5 Experiments**: none. Practicality is argued by cost accounting instead (p. 12–13):
  - pivot-based model improvement can cost O(n⁶) linear algebra per iteration;
  - Powell's Lagrange updates cost O(n⁴) plus a global maximization of |ℓ_i|;
  - replacing at most one point per iteration saves function evaluations;
  - in practice "it is rarely necessary to replace points because of bad pivot values" (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Conn–Gould–Toint had said that second-order DFO analysis can simply be derived from the derivative-based case. The paper answers that the details are "not as trivial as one might expect" (p. 3).
  - Powell's two trust regions are replaced by one radius kept comparable to a stationarity measure (p. 2). Powell is credited with an analogue of the criticality step (p. 15).
  - The acknowledgement says Powell's comments as a reviewer led to the improved Step 1, Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 5.5 (p. 28).
  - Thomas [13] and Yuan's non-convergence example [14] frame the lim-type issue (p. 2).
  - Own prior work is the base: [3] for the criticality step (p. 15); [4] and [5] (S025) for building fully linear or quadratic models (p. 2, p. 11–13).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The introduction presents the contribution as a list of features that make the algorithm "closer to a practical one" (p. 2–3). The priority claim is hedged: "As far as we are aware, we provide the first comprehensive analysis" of second-order convergence (p. 3). The scope is explicit: "We provide no analysis for local rates of convergence" (p. 3). A table of κ constants with mnemonic subscripts comes first (p. 4). A parenthetical admits a cost of the device: in derivative-based methods, forcing Δ → 0 "may jeopardize the fast rates of local convergence" (p. 2). The paper candidly lists impractical members of its own abstraction (p. 8). There is no conclusion section; the paper ends with the second-order theorem (p. 28).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory:
  - first- and second-order global convergence, lim-type, with acceptance on simple decrease;
  - Δ → 0 as a natural stopping criterion (p. 2);
  - an abstraction that covers non-polynomial models (p. 2, p. 13).

  The limits are stated: no rates (p. 3); fully quadratic models may need O(n²) points, and the framework does not remove the need for them for second-order results (p. 3); moving x at model-improving iterations would weaken the result to liminf and make it hard to reach full linearity when the region keeps changing (Remark 5.1, p. 21). There is no explicit future-work list.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (practice; the origin of Step 2). Models need to be *certifiable* only within a finite, uniformly bounded number of steps. They are checked or improved only after a failed ratio test (Step 4) and in the criticality step (Step 1) (p. 3, p. 13–14). This is the paper source for Method 4 Step 2 (SKILL.md wording: *decide which iterations actually need the certificate*).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (deterministic ancestor of the contract). *Fully linear* means model accuracy tied to the radius (κ_eg Δ, κ_ef Δ²), with a finite repair procedure when it fails (Def. 3.1, p. 7–8). The probabilistic contract later replaces *can be certified* with *holds with probability p*.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (deterministic form). The classical trust region is kept (Cauchy decrease, ratio test, radius update). Only the pieces that model inaccuracy breaks are changed: shrink only for a certified model, add the criticality step, freeze x and Δ during improvement. The key lemma is the derivative-based one plus a model-error term (Lemma 5.2, p. 16–17).
- Method 3: no link. The results are purely asymptotic (Theorems 5.8–5.9 and 7.10–7.11). This is useful for the trajectory: complexity for this class came later (S088, S104).
- New-pattern candidate: *framework-then-instantiation*. One theorem is proved for an abstract model class, and polynomial interpolation and regression are then shown to belong to it (p. 11–13). This is the formal version of S004's *abstract algorithm class with pluggable quality predicates* (the predicates "fully linear", "certifiably fully linear" and "model-improvement algorithm", p. 7–8).
- New-pattern candidate: *relax the analysed algorithm toward practice*. List the features of the practical codes (one radius, simple decrease, no full certification at every iteration, one point replaced per iteration) and prove convergence for an algorithm that allows them (p. 2–3, p. 12). It is the mirror image of S029's *analysable surrogate for a practical heuristic*.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2/D3] Define your model or oracle class by what the proof needs: (i) error bounds scaled by the radius and (ii) a finite, uniformly bounded repair procedure. Prove convergence once, then show that concrete constructions belong to the class (Defs. 3.1 and 3.3, p. 7–13).
- [D4] Enlarge the constants without loss of generality so that being good on a small ball implies being good on every larger ball with the same constants. You then never re-certify when the radius grows (Lemma 3.4, p. 9–10).
- [D2] Criticality step: when the model gradient is small, force Δ ≤ μ‖g‖ by geometric shrinking. This transfers model stationarity to true stationarity (Lemma 5.7) and makes Δ → 0 a stopping test (p. 13–15, p. 18–19).
- [D4] Key-lemma template: accurate model and Δ ≤ c‖g‖ imply a successful iteration, via |ρ − 1| ≤ (model error)/(predicted decrease) (Lemma 5.2, p. 16–17). This is the lemma that BSV and STORM later make probabilistic.

### S023 · A derivative-free algorithm for least-squares minimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2010, DOI 10.1137/09075531x)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: H. Zhang, A. R. Conn · **Position in trajectory**: The first paper in this batch with Scheinberg at Lehigh (affiliation p. 1). It carries the CSV framework (S007, S025) into a structured solver with software and benchmarks (DFLS). It is the paper that SKILL.md Heuristic 3 and Method 6 cite, and it announces a bound-constrained sequel (DFBOLS, p. 21). **Read**: full text; the reference list was skimmed. The data-profile figures are extracted as axis residue, but their captions and the discussion are readable (p. 18–20).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is structure plus a cost model. Least-squares problems min ½‖F(x)‖² with black-box residuals arise from simulations, experiments and legacy codes, and the goal is "the least possible number of function evaluations" (p. 1). The three DFO classes are reviewed:
  - direct search: many evaluations (p. 1–2);
  - finite differences with quasi-Newton: not robust under noise (p. 2);
  - model-based trust region: "frequently superior" according to Moré–Wild [20] (p. 2).

  The structural idea is borrowed from the authors' constrained DFO work [4], which models each constraint separately. The argument is that function evaluations dominate the cost, so maintaining several models costs more work and storage but no extra evaluations (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Build an interpolation model m_i for **each residual** f_i on one common sample set Y. The paper uses 2n+1 points with Powell's least-Frobenius-norm update, so the model coefficients are updated in O(mn) and models are built in O(mn²) per iteration, O(mn³) when the base point is shifted (p. 2, p. 16–17). The quadratic model of Φ is φ = c + gᵀs + ½sᵀHs with c = ½‖m‖² and g = Jᵀm, and a regime-switching Hessian (p. 5):
  - H = JᵀJ (Gauss–Newton) if ‖g‖ ≥ κ¹_H;
  - H = JᵀJ + κ³_H‖m‖I (Levenberg–Marquardt-like) if ‖g‖ < κ¹_H and c < κ²_H‖g‖;
  - the full JᵀJ + Σ m_i∇²m_i otherwise.

  The typical constants are 1, 1 and 0.01 (p. 5). The regularization protects local quadratic convergence for zero-residual problems with a singular Jacobian; that analysis is in a separate paper [29] (p. 5–6). The algorithm (p. 6–8) is "close in spirit" to the CSV global-convergence framework and to Powell's NEWUOA (p. 6). Its parts:
  - a criticality step that makes Y Λ-poised in a ball of radius ≤ β‖g‖;
  - a Powell-style safety step that skips the function evaluation when ‖d_k‖ < ½ρ_k;
  - acceptance on simple decrease (r_k > 0);
  - a radius update based on ‖d_k‖;
  - a second radius ρ_k, a lower bound on the sampling radius.

  The introduction says a single adaptively updated radius can serve both purposes (p. 3). Later, "in some sense", Δ_k and ρ_k identify Powell's two regions (p. 8). The design reasoning is written out: shrink the radius only when failure is not due to poor geometry (p. 9); skip the poisedness check after a successful step (p. 9); do not move the base point at every iteration, to save O(mn³) (p. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - F is C² with a bounded level set (Assumption 3.1, p. 10).
  - Model Hessians are uniformly bounded (Assumption 3.2, p. 10). In the code this is enforced by a safeguard, and Lemma 3.4 shows that minimum-Frobenius-norm models satisfy it automatically (p. 11).
  - At most |Y|−1 point substitutions make Y Λ-poised (Assumption 2.1, p. 4).
  - Λ-poisedness for underdetermined sets is used in two equivalent forms (Defs. 2.1 and 2.2); the paper notes that "unfortunately" these definitions depend on the polynomial space or the basis (p. 4).
  - The text cites [6] (the Math. Program. geometry paper) for the global-convergence framework, while the lemmas it borrows come from [5], the S007 paper (p. 6, p. 12). This looks like a citation slip.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Standard trust-region technique, with one structural lemma:
  - **Lemma 4.3 (model accuracy via the residuals).** Φ(s) − φ ≤ F(y)ᵀ[∇F(y) − J(y)](s − y) + c₁‖s − y‖² ≤ c₂Δ‖s − y‖ + c₁‖s − y‖². So |r_k − 1| ≤ ε once Δ_k ≤ c‖g_φk‖ (p. 12–13). The residual-model gradient error (3.1) is propagated through the outer ½‖·‖².
  - **Lemma 4.4.** ‖g_φ‖ ≥ δ₁ implies Δ_k ≥ δ₂. The proof is by contradiction along a subsequence where ρ_k drops. The authors flag the "additional complication introduced by the interconnected updates" of ρ (p. 13–14).
  - **Lemma 4.5.** g_φ → 0 implies ∇Φ → 0, through the criticality step (p. 14–15).
  - **Theorem 4.6.** liminf ‖∇Φ(x_k)‖ = 0 (p. 15–16). Only the liminf result is proved, not lim.
  - Borrowed pieces: Powell's Cauchy-decrease bound (Lemma 4.1, p. 12); finiteness of the criticality step from [5] (Lemma 4.2, p. 12); the Hessian bound for minimum-Frobenius models from the book (Lemma 3.4, p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: Baselines are LMDIF (MINPACK Levenberg–Marquardt with forward differences) and NEWUOA (p. 16). The two are chosen as representatives of two different derivative-free classes, and each serves a stated purpose: NEWUOA shows the gain from structure, LMDIF the gain from interpolation over finite differences (p. 16).
  - DFLS is implemented inside NEWUOA's framework, partly so that "the comparison between it and DFLS more meaningful" (p. 16).
  - All codes are Fortran with default parameters except the finite-difference step. Both NEWUOA and DFLS use 2n+1 points and an initial radius of 1.0 (p. 17).
  - Test set: the Moré–Wild benchmark of 53 problems (22 CUTEr nonlinear least-squares types) (p. 17). Convergence test Φ(x₀) − Φ(x_k) ≥ (1−τ)(Φ(x₀) − Φ_L). Data profiles d_s(α) with a budget of 50 simplex gradients, i.e. 50(n_p+1) evaluations. τ ∈ {10⁻¹, 10⁻³, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁷} (p. 17–18).
  - Noisy set P_N: deterministic noise (1 + ε_N π(x))Φ(x) with a Chebyshev-based π and ε_N = 10⁻³, chosen for reproducibility. The final radius and the finite-difference step are set "comparable to the noise level" (p. 19).
  - Results: DFLS ranks first everywhere. It solves about 88% of the smooth problems at τ = 10⁻⁷ within about 22 simplex gradients (p. 19). On noisy problems it is the least affected. The interpolation methods degrade less than finite differences, explained as noise filtering by accumulated models (p. 19–20).
  - Unsolved problems enter the profiles as t = ∞ (p. 18). There is no separate failure analysis.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds explicitly on the authors' CSV framework and on Powell's NEWUOA and least-Frobenius updating (p. 2, p. 6). Its treatment of NEWUOA is respectful: NEWUOA's details "are carefully and intelligently chosen" (p. 16), and its lower ranking is attributed to the missing structure, not to a flaw in the code (p. 19). The Levenberg–Marquardt local-convergence literature (Fan–Yuan, Yamashita–Fukushima, Dan et al.) is cited for the error-bound local analysis, deferred to [29] (p. 3). Powell's two-radius idea is first presented as avoidable (p. 3), then partly adopted (ρ_k, p. 8).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract promises "Promising numerical results" (p. 1). After the algorithm comes a long prose section justifying each step (p. 8–9); this is where the design reasoning lives. Cost accounting is explicit in O-notation for work and memory (p. 2, p. 16–17). Interpretive claims about results are hedged: "This result is perhaps not surprising" (p. 18); "it is reasonable to believe" (p. 21). The conclusion turns the experiments into a recommendation to combine interpolation with structure exploitation (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Three layers: an algorithm class; a global-convergence theorem (liminf); an implementation with benchmark evidence (p. 1, p. 20). Limits:
  - the local convergence is deferred to [29] (p. 3, p. 20);
  - only a liminf result is proved (p. 15–16);
  - the method needs O(mn²) more storage and arithmetic than NEWUOA, called "the essential cost of exploiting the structure" (p. 17);
  - results "can vary considerably" with noise and accuracy, to the point where the preferred algorithm changes (p. 20).

  Future: a bound-constrained version (DFBOLS), already developed (p. 21); an ℓ₁ regularization that would suit bound constraints (p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Structured black box: model each output component on one shared sample set. You pay in linear algebra and memory, not in function evaluations. State the O(·) costs explicitly (p. 2, p. 16–17).

### S011 · Efficient block-coordinate descent algorithms for the group lasso (Mathematical Programming Computation 2013, DOI 10.1007/s12532-013-0051-x)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Z. (Tony) Qin, D. Goldfarb · **Position in trajectory**: A Lehigh-era paper (affiliation p. 1) with Goldfarb's Columbia student. It belongs to the structure-exploiting sparse ML-optimization line (cards S010, S005, S100) and carries DFO/trust-region machinery (the trust-region subproblem) into an ML subproblem. **Read**: full text, including Appendix A (data generation). The file is the preprint ("Noname manuscript"). Figures 1–6 are not in the text file (captions only), so plotted results were read only through the text and Table 6 (p. 16–20).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an assumption in earlier solvers. Block coordinate descent (BCD) for the Group Lasso min ½‖Ax − b‖² + λΣ‖x_j‖ (p. 1–2) had been derived under the "restrictive assumption" that A_jᵀA_j = I, which gives closed-form block solutions (Yuan–Lin [33], Meier et al. [17]) (p. 2–3). Friedman–Hastie–Tibshirani dropped the assumption but solved the subproblems inexactly (p. 3). Gradient methods (SLEP's FISTA) have closed-form subproblems but, the authors claim, lose to BCD in practice (p. 2). The motivation is applied: sparsity in high-dimensional ML, group selection in gene data (p. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Two algorithms and a hybrid.
  - **BCD-GL.** The block subproblem min ½xᵀM_jx + p_jᵀx + λ‖x‖ is solved **exactly**. x_j = 0 if and only if ‖p_j‖ ≤ λ (p. 3). Otherwise the subproblem equals a trust-region subproblem with an unknown radius Δ. Then x_j = Δ·y_j(Δ) with y_j(Δ) = −(ΔM_j + λI)⁻¹p_j, and Δ solves ‖y_j(Δ)‖ = 1 (p. 3). Newton's method is applied to the secular equation φ(Δ) = 1 − 1/‖y_j(Δ)‖, using an eigen-decomposition of M_j computed once per group. The "hard case" cannot occur (p. 4). Newton usually needs fewer than five iterations (p. 4).
  - **ISTA-BC.** For large groups: ISTA with a separate backtracking step length t_j per block, applied Gauss–Seidel style (Algorithm 4.1, p. 5–6). **ISTA-MS** scales a fixed vector of block step lengths t̄_j = 1/‖A_j‖ by one line-searched θ, and stays parallelizable (p. 6).
  - **BCD-HYB.** Switches to ISTA-BC for groups with more than N_BC = 200 variables, a rule decided a priori (p. 7, p. 13).
  - **MMV special case.** The block Hessians are ‖A_j‖²I, so BCD and ISTA-BC coincide and need no eigen-decomposition and no line search (BCD-MMV, Algorithm 5.1, p. 12).
  - Randomized block selection (Nesterov) is also implemented and tested (p. 7).

  Kept: the prox-gradient/ISTA structure and backtracking. Changed: the step length is per block, and the subproblem is solved exactly with classical trust-region subproblem machinery (Moré–Sorensen, Nocedal–Wright) (p. 3–5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Convex composite problem. Groups are given and non-overlapping; overlapping gene groups are handled by the augmentation of Jacob et al., giving 28459 features (p. 2, p. 14). M_j ⪰ 0; if a block is singular, M_j + δI is used to meet the convergence framework's condition (p. 10). λ = 0.2 λ_max (p. 13). The convergence rate analysis covers only ISTA-MS. The rates of ISTA/FISTA "do not readily extend" to ISTA-BC or BCD-GL (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Theorem 1 (ISTA-MS, O(1/k)).** The Beck–Teboulle argument with a vector of Lipschitz constants L = [L₁,…,L_J] instead of a scalar. Lemma 1 generalizes Beck–Teboulle's Lemma 2.3 through the block optimality condition (23). A lower bound t_min on the step comes from the block Lipschitz constants (p. 7–9). The constant picks up √R_L, the ratio of the largest to the smallest block Lipschitz constant (p. 8). The constant C in the theorem statement lacks a factor β that appears at the end of the proof (p. 8–9); this looks like a typo.
  - **Theorem 2 (global convergence of BCD-HYB) by embedding.** Each step type is shown to be an instance of Tseng–Yun's block coordinate gradient descent (BCGD) framework, which is already known to converge. ISTA-BC is BCGD with H_jj = I/t_j and Armijo parameters α = 1, σ = 1, γ = 0.5. BCD-GL is BCGD with H_jj = M_j (+δI) (p. 9–10). The bounds θI ⪯ H ⪯ θ̄I follow from the backtracking bounds on t_j (p. 10). There is no rate for the hybrid.
  - The FISTA-type acceleration in the block setting has no theory: "It is also unclear" whether it gives any theoretical advantage (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Environment and measures. Matlab on a laptop. CPU time, Aprods (matrix–vector products; fractional for block products) and iterations are recorded (p. 13). Aprods is the headline measure because SLEP and SPOR-SPG have C kernels (p. 13).
  - Baselines: BCGD (own Gauss–Seidel implementation), SpaRSA, SLEP, SPG / SPOR-SPG (p. 13).
  - Data:
    - synthetic sets from Yuan–Lin and Meier et al.;
    - nemirovski1 to nemirovski4, built to be hard for first-order methods "in the spirit of the worst case complexity examples" (p. 13);
    - large-group synthetic sets (Table 3, p. 15; generation in Appendix A, p. 22–23);
    - a breast-cancer gene-expression regression (8141 genes, 295 tumours, 878 MSigDB pathway groups) (p. 14);
    - MMV sets and scalability sweeps (Tables 4–5, p. 15).
  - Fairness. A reference solution x* is precomputed (ISTA-BC to a relative change below 10⁻¹⁰), and every solver stops at F(x) − F(x*) ≤ 10⁻⁵. SPG is given τ = ‖x*‖₁,₂ (p. 14–15). The MMV scalability tests use relative accuracy 10⁻⁵ instead (p. 16).
  - Reporting: Dolan–Moré performance profiles (p. 16).
  - Losses and null results reported:
    - on the Nemirovski sets ISTA-BC, ISTA-MS and BCGD do "much worse" (p. 16);
    - the large-data results are "less clear-cut" (p. 16);
    - uniform-random block selection gives no advantage, while Lipschitz-proportional sampling (RBCD-GL) helps in iterations (Table 6, p. 17, p. 20);
    - FISTA-BC gives no gain, and FISTA even shows a lower empirical rate on the breast-cancer data (p. 7, p. 16–17);
    - a Jacobi-parallel ISTA-BC was inferior (p. 6);
    - SpaRSA is excluded from the MMV figure because it stopped at suboptimal solutions (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Earlier BCD work is corrected by removing its orthonormality assumption; the assumption is called "restrictive" (p. 3). The paper claims to beat SLEP's FISTA (p. 2) and Tseng–Yun's BCGD in practice while using BCGD's theory (p. 1, p. 10). Nesterov [19] and Richtárik–Takáč [22] are cited for randomized coordinate selection (p. 7). A footnote concedes that BCD-MMV coincides with Rakotomamonjy's M-BCD, published while the first version of this paper was in preparation (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A labelled contribution paragraph: "Our contribution in this paper is the following" (p. 2). The abstract claims are mildly hedged ("very competitive and often outperforms", p. 1). Metric choices are justified in the text (Aprods vs CPU, p. 13). Weak spots are admitted in the results (Nemirovski sets, "less clear-cut", p. 16). The conclusion is a short summary with no future directions (p. 21). The theory is presented as a supporting role; most of the paper is algorithm and implementation.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Algorithmic and computational: an exact BCD for general blocks, per-block step lengths, a hybrid, and the MMV specialization. Theory is limited to an O(1/k) rate for ISTA-MS and to global convergence by embedding (p. 7–10). Limits:
  - no rates for BCD-GL, ISTA-BC or BCD-HYB (p. 7);
  - sequential and not parallelizable (p. 6);
  - eigen-decomposition cost O(m_j³) per group and O(Σm_j²) storage for large groups (p. 4);
  - poor results on adversarial instances (p. 16).

  Open questions stated in the body: whether acceleration helps in the block framework (p. 7); applying ISTA-BC to Group Lasso logistic regression (p. 2). Compare S054, where acceleration is found not to help proximal quasi-Newton.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (practice). Block structure is exploited to make subproblems exact and cheap: a one-time eigen-decomposition plus a scalar Newton root-find (p. 3–4). **Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] A regularized block subproblem min ½xᵀMx + pᵀx + λ‖x‖ is a trust-region subproblem with an unknown radius. Test the zero solution by ‖p‖ ≤ λ, then solve 1 − 1/‖y(Δ)‖ = 0 by Newton's method with a one-time eigen-decomposition (p. 3–4).

- [D4] Get global convergence for free: show that each new step is an instance of an existing convergent framework, give the explicit parameter mapping (H_jj = I/t_j, α = 1, σ = 1, γ = 0.5), and patch the degenerate case (M_j + δI) (p. 9–10).

- [D5] Fair-comparison protocol: precompute a high-accuracy reference solution, stop every solver at the same F − F* ≤ 10⁻⁵, count matrix–vector products (fractional for block products) rather than CPU time when competitors use compiled kernels, and include adversarial (Nemirovski-type) instances (p. 13–15).
