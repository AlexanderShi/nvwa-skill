# Batch c05 · paper cards (core)

**Batch intent**: See how Scheinberg's 2020–21 adaptive-stochastic programme is put together. S047 is a survey that states the general framework. S026 and S043 are two line-search analyses that change the noise model: bounded noise with an expected-complexity bound (S026), then biased, light-tailed oracles with a high-probability tail bound (S043). Also check what the one paper outside this programme (S017, exact MILP training of decision trees) shows about how far her methods reach.
**Focus dimensions**: D1–D8, all four papers being core. Emphasis on D3 (oracle and noise model), D4 (proof skeleton and devices) and D5 (comparison protocol).
**Material roles**: S047 core · S017 core · S026 core · S043 core. Version caveats: every text file is an arXiv version, and S043's file is the extended 2023 version (see that card).

### S047 · Adaptive stochastic optimization: a framework for analyzing stochastic optimization algorithms (IEEE Signal Processing Magazine 2020, DOI 10.1109/msp.2020.3003539; arXiv:2001.06699)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: F. E. Curtis · **Read level**: full, including the 2-page appendix (pp. 15–16). The text is arXiv v1 (18 Jan 2020); the published magazine version was not seen. · **Position in research trajectory**: a magazine-level synthesis written between the expected-complexity papers (Cartis–Scheinberg 2018 [8], Blanchet et al. 2019 [2], Paquette–Scheinberg [23]) and the later high-probability work. It first re-derives the deterministic analyses in the format the stochastic proofs need, then generalises.

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The entry is a practice cost, not a theorem. SG-type methods are "non-adaptive": step sizes and mini-batch sizes are prescribed and must be tuned for each application, and that tuning can take "weeks or months" on a supercomputer (p. 1; §2 p. 2). Variance-reduced methods (SVRG, SAGA, SARAH) are put in the same non-adaptive group (p. 2). The research question is whether the adaptive deterministic methods (trust region, line search, regularized Newton) can get convergence-rate guarantees when function and derivative information is stochastic (p. 1 §1; p. 2 §4).
  - Transferable: open with a measured practice cost (here, tuning cost) and one small experiment that shows it (§3, p. 2) before any theorem.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (deterministic adaptive framework, p. 4) and Algorithm 2 (stochastic, p. 7) share one skeleton: build a model, compute a step, test sufficient reduction, then expand or contract α. The only differences are that the stochastic model must be accurate with probability ≥ 1−δ1 and the function estimates with probability ≥ 1−δ2 (p. 7). The analysis is organised around a progress measure Φ_k that decreases on *every* iteration (Condition 4.1, p. 3), which the authors call new for the deterministic literature (p. 3). The stochastic counterpart is Condition 5.1 (pp. 7–8): below a threshold α_ε, success has probability 1−δ > 1/2, and the conditional expected decrease of Φ is ≥ Θh_ε(α_k). The illustrative adaptive method adapts the sample size as well as α: on failure it cuts the step and enlarges the batch, on success the reverse (p. 2).
  - Transferable: write the deterministic and the stochastic method as the same algorithm box, with two probability knobs (δ1 for models, δ2 for function estimates) (pp. 4, 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is C¹ with L-Lipschitz gradient (p. 3), with a Lipschitz Hessian added for cubic regularization (pp. 5–6), and f* > −∞ (p. 3). Deterministic models match f to first order (p. 3). The TR condition α_k ≤ τ‖∇f(x_k)‖ is "not necessary for the deterministic setting" but is imposed for consistency with the stochastic case (p. 4). Stochastic accuracy uses Taylor-like bounds (5a–c): κ_fΔ², κ_gΔ, κ_H, each holding with a probability conditioned on F_k (pp. 8–9). κ_f "cannot be too large", while κ_g and κ_H may be arbitrarily large (p. 9). The Paquette–Scheinberg line search needs a variance bound on the function estimates. That condition is stronger than probabilistic (5a), which is "more tolerant to outliers" (p. 11). The stochastic framework is presented as a simplification that leaves out some technical details, with [8, 2] cited for the full version (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: (i) Deterministic template: T ≤ Φ_0/(Θh_ε(α_ε)) (eq. 1, p. 3). (ii) Potentials by method: TR Φ = ν(f−f*) + (1−ν)α² (p. 4); LS Φ = ν(f−f*) + (1−ν)α‖∇f‖² (p. 5); cubic regularization Φ = ν(f−f*) + (1−ν)α‖∇f‖^{3/2}, with the stopping time defined on ∇f(x_{k+1}) (p. 6); second-order stationarity uses α³; convex uses ν(1/ε − 1/(f−f*)) + (1−ν)α; strongly convex uses a log version (p. 6). ν is chosen close to 1 so that the step-increase term on successes is dominated (pp. 4–5). (iii) Stochastic engine: Theorem 5.2, E[T_ε] ≤ (1−δ)/(1−2δ) · Φ_0/(Θh_ε(α_ε)) + 1, citing Blanchet et al. Thm 2.2 (p. 8). (iv) A four-case picture, model good/bad × estimates good/bad (Fig. 3, p. 10). Only the bad–bad case (probability ≤ δ1δ2) can increase Φ, and the key step is that the "possible decrease of this measure is proportional to any possible increase" (p. 10). (v) Paquette–Scheinberg LS: a separate control sequence Δ_k approximates α_k‖∇f‖², so that function-accuracy requirements do not swing with ‖g_k‖. Δ_k grows or shrinks according to a reliability test (eq. 7, p. 11). Orders: O(ε⁻²) for TR and LS, O(ε^{-3/2}) for deterministic cubic regularization, O(ε⁻³) for second-order stationarity, O(ε⁻¹) convex and O(log ε⁻¹) strongly convex (pp. 4–6, 10).
  - Transferable: potential = ν·(objective gap) + (1−ν)·(step-parameter term shaped like the per-iteration decrease), with ν near 1, so that successful and unsuccessful iterations both decrease Φ (pp. 4–6).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: A single illustrative experiment (§3, p. 2; Fig. 1, p. 3): MNIST binary logistic regression (digit 5 vs all other digits) with ℓ2 regularization λ = 10⁻⁴. SG with mini-batch 64 and fixed step sizes: α = 1 reaches ≈98% test accuracy, while α = 0.01 stagnates below about 90%. It is compared with an adaptive method that starts from the same batch size and several initial α. The metric is test accuracy over 10 epochs. The increase/decrease factors are not reported (p. 2). The claim is that the adaptive method, from every initialization, competes with the best SG run (p. 2). No repeated seeds or failure cases are reported.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The survey builds on [8] Cartis–Scheinberg and [2] Blanchet et al. (p. 7) and summarises STORM [10] (p. 9) and Paquette–Scheinberg [23] (p. 11). Its criticism is direct and specific. Exponentially growing batches [15, 24] are judged not adaptive, because their improvement follows prescribed parameters that still need tuning (p. 8). Methods that substitute ε for ‖∇f‖ [28, 30] are judged too conservative to compete with truly stochastic algorithms (p. 9); they reduce to sample average approximation (p. 12). The interpolation assumption of [29] is "unreasonably strong" (p. 11). The probabilistic Wolfe line search [18] has "no known theoretical guarantees" (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Magazine register. Intuition comes first: the tuning cost is dramatised with a CPU-days vs Toyota Camrys comparison (p. 1), and one figure precedes the math (pp. 2–3). Each method gets one short subsection with the same three moves: define T_ε, define Φ_k, verify Condition 4.1 or 5.1 (§4.1–§4.4, §5.2–§5.3). Algebra is pushed to the appendix (pp. 15–16). Open problems are stated with the exact technical blocker (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: The contribution is a unifying analysis template (deterministic Condition 4.1 plus stochastic Condition 5.1) and a map of which methods fit it (TR, LS) and which do not yet (cubic regularization, TRACE) (pp. 3, 7, 12). The limits are concrete. Stochastic cubic regularization is blocked because a T_ε defined through x_{k+1} is not a stopping time with respect to F_k (p. 12). Stochastic TRACE needs the exact Lagrange multiplier of the TR subproblem (p. 12). Future directions: error bounds tailored to the problem, which improve work complexity rather than iteration complexity (p. 12); separate expansion and contraction factors to loosen the bounds on δ1 and δ2 (p. 12); practical batch-size and step-size rules (p. 12). Only expected-complexity bounds are covered (Theorem 5.2, p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (stated): SG is non-adaptive and must be tuned for each application (pp. 1–2). Algorithm 2 "has the same structure as Algorithm 1" but uses stochastic models and estimates (p. 7).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (stated + template): Condition 5.1 with the Theorem 5.2 stopping-time bound (pp. 7–8). STORM's bound "matches the complexity of the deterministic version of the algorithm up to the factor dependent on (1 − δ1)(1 − δ2)" (p. 10).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: accuracy is tied to Δ_k or α_k‖g_k‖ and holds with probability 1−δ conditioned on F_k. Substituting ε is criticised as over-conservative (pp. 8–9, 12).
- new-pattern candidate: *Deterministic framework first*: restate the classical deterministic analyses in exactly the format the stochastic proof needs (a potential that decreases on every iteration), then generalise (pp. 3, 7).
- new-pattern candidate: *Name the measurability obstacle*: the open problem is stated precisely (T_ε, defined through x_{k+1}, is not a stopping time with respect to F_k), not left vague (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- All-iteration potential Φ_k = ν(f(x_k) − f*) + (1−ν)·(α-term shaped like the per-step decrease), with ν chosen near 1 (pp. 4–6).
- A four-outcome table (model good/bad × estimates good/bad) to see which case can increase the potential and to size δ1δ2 (Fig. 3, p. 10).
- Decouple the function-accuracy target from the fluctuating ‖g_k‖ through a separate control sequence Δ_k with its own expand/contract rule (eq. 7, p. 11).
- When an accuracy condition needs the unknowable ‖∇f(x_k)‖, set Δ_k = α_k‖g(x_k)‖ and use a guess-and-increase sampling loop, rather than substituting ε (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "This article summarizes recent research and motivates future work on adaptive stochastic optimization methods, which have the potential to offer significant computational savings when training large-scale systems." (p. 1)
- "By contrast, in the framework presented here, the analysis is structured around a measure in which progress is made in all iterations." (p. 3)

### S017 · Optimal decision trees for categorical data via integer programming (Journal of Global Optimization 2021, DOI 10.1007/s10898-021-01009-y; arXiv:1612.03225)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: O. Günlük, J. Kalagnanam, M. Li, M. Menickelly · **Read level**: full. The text is arXiv v3 (13 Aug 2019); the journal version was not seen. · **Position in research trajectory**: outside the DFO and stochastic-oracle line. It is an interpretable-ML paper from an IBM collaboration, partly written while Scheinberg was on sabbatical at IBM Research, Google and Oxford (p. 1 footnote). It belongs to her ML-optimization strand.

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The entry is the gap between heuristics and optimality. CART and C4.5 grow a tree greedily and then prune it. They can miss a small accurate tree, and "it is usually impossible to establish a bound" on the gap to the best tree (p. 2). The applied pull is interpretability in medicine, criminal justice and business (p. 1) and the FICO explainable-ML challenge (p. 2). There is also a structural entry: one-hot encoding lets numerical classifiers ignore that exactly one feature per group can be 1 (p. 2). The problem is to find, for a fixed topology, the tree with maximum training accuracy, branching at each node on a subset of the values of one categorical feature (pp. 2–3, 6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: MILP (8) uses three sets of variables: group selection v, feature selection z and leaf routing c (pp. 7–9). Leaf labels are fixed in advance by symmetry (p. 6). The topology is not modelled. Four candidate topologies are solved and one is chosen by cross-validation (pp. 10–11). The formulation is then engineered in four ways: aggregated routing inequalities (11)–(12) replace (5)–(6) (p. 12); anchor features break symmetry (eq. 13, pp. 12–13); c variables for wrong classifications are dropped and the rest relaxed to [0,1] (p. 13); and Proposition 3 shows that v and the leaf-adjacent z can be relaxed, so the number of integer variables does not depend on N (pp. 13–14). Extensions: ordinal constraints for binned numerical features (p. 15), a max.card cap on the branching subset size against overfitting (pp. 15–16), and a sensitivity/specificity objective (p. 16). Compared with Bertsimas–Dunn [3], the model is more specialised: binary only, no hyperplane splits. In exchange it exploits categorical structure, stays interpretable and has no regularisation parameter to tune (pp. 4, 26).
  - Transferable: fix the combinatorial shape (topology, leaf labels) outside the MILP and choose among a few shapes by cross-validation, instead of modelling the shape (pp. 6, 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Binary classification only (p. 3). The input is a binary vector with groups, one-hot within each group (pp. 5, 7). Numerical features are binned into deciles (p. 15). Topologies are fixed with at most four levels of decisions (p. 3; Figs. 2–3, pp. 8, 10). "Optimal" means maximum training accuracy, while generalisation is the real goal (p. 6). A weight C handles class imbalance (pp. 9–10). Constraints (9)–(10), which would exclude splits that send every sample the same way, are deliberately not used (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: (a) Correctness: Propositions 1–2 show that the encoding routes each sample to exactly one leaf (pp. 8–9). (b) Weakness of the LP relaxation, shown by an explicit fractional solution (z = 1/2, c = 1/2) that attains the trivial bound |I+| + C|I−| for any tree with at least two levels (pp. 11–12). (c) Valid inequalities obtained by aggregating the routing constraints over all leaves below a node (p. 12). (d) Integrality of extreme points by perturbation: a fractional extreme point is written as a convex combination of two feasible perturbed points, a contradiction (Proposition 3, pp. 13–14). (e) Monotonicity: the optimum over a larger topology is at least as good on training data as over any of its minors (pp. 7, 11). No statistical or generalisation analysis.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 10 binary datasets (2 from LIBSVM, FICO heloc, 7 from UCI), chosen because most of their features are categorical (p. 16; Table 1, p. 17); CPLEX 12.6.1 from Python 2.7 on 8 cores (pp. 16–17).
  - Ablation of the formulation enhancements (Nothing / No Anchor / No Relax / No Strength / All), reporting seconds and number of LPs with a 3-hour limit. It admits a counterexample: a1a solves faster without strengthening (Tables 2–3, pp. 17–18).
  - Scaling by topology and sample size (Table 4, p. 18), and again with CART-based feature selection (Table 5, p. 19).
  - Effect of combinatorial branching (Table 6, pp. 19–20) and of the numerical-feature constraints (Table 7, pp. 20–21).
  - Accuracy vs depth-3 CART (rpart), 5 random splits. CART is trained on 90% of the data, the MILP trees on min{90%, 600} points (p. 21). Runs with 30-minute and 5-minute limits (Tables 8–11, pp. 21–23); standard deviation about 0.2–0.3% (p. 21).
  - Topology chosen by cross-validation vs CART at its default depth (Table 13, p. 24).
  - Negative results are reported. On ttt and krkp, CART is more accurate but uses deeper trees (ttt: 93.1 vs 81.0 with 20.2 leaves, Table 13, pp. 24–25). heloc depth-3 and imbalanced trees collapse under the 5-minute limit without feature selection (66.2/65.0 and 58.2/57.6, Table 10, p. 22).
  - heloc case study: a depth-2 tree reaches 71.6 test accuracy in 405 s, vs 71.7 for the winning entry [7] and 71.0 for CART (p. 25).
  - Sensitivity/specificity trade-off on the breast-cancer data, averaged over 30 runs (pp. 25–26).
  - Transferable: an ablation grid that switches off one enhancement at a time, reports time and number of LPs, and keeps the case where switching off helps (pp. 17–18).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper positions itself against four groups: greedy induction, including Breiman et al. (1984) quoted as calling an overall optimal tree infeasible (p. 4); 1990s relaxations (Bennett–Blue) that do not guarantee optimality (p. 4); SGD on a continuous upper bound [15] (p. 4); and mainly Bertsimas–Dunn [3] (pp. 4, 26). The critique of [3] is direct but specific: categorical structure is ignored, and the regularisation parameter must be tuned although its effect on the topology "cannot be known a priori" (p. 4). DNF and rule learning by IP [18, 13, 4, 7] is discussed (pp. 4–5). The coauthor's own column-generation paper, Dash–Günlük–Wei [7], serves as the strong benchmark (pp. 2, 25).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution is stated as a conditional claim: if a small accurate tree exists, the model finds it in reasonable time (first quote below, p. 3). Each formulation device gets a one-paragraph motivation followed by a proposition or valid inequality (pp. 8–14). Hedging is uneven: "We believe this might be due to" (p. 19) sits beside "undoubtedly because" (p. 25). The reporting rule is explicit: an accuracy is bolded only if it is more than 1% better (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: The contribution is a formulation (modelling plus strengthening) and empirical evidence. No software release is mentioned. The stated limit is concrete: the number of continuous variables, and the difficulty, grow with the training-set size (p. 26). Larger topologies hit the time limit (Table 4, p. 18). The claim is restricted to cases "when classification can be achieved by a small interpretable tree" (p. 25). Two caveats from my reading:
  - Constraint (15) for a guaranteed specificity of 0.95 is printed as "≥ ⌈(1 − 0.95)|I−|⌉" (p. 16). Read literally, it requires only 5% of negatives to be correct. This looks like a typo in the arXiv text; the journal version was not checked.
  - The CART comparison does not use equal data: CART trains on 90% of the data, the MILP trees on at most 600 points, and their feature selection comes from a CART trained on 90% (pp. 21, 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the categorical group structure is modelled directly (one group per node, subset branching). This is what makes the integer-variable count independent of N (pp. 3, 7–8, 14).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: a head-to-head comparison against the standard heuristic under time budgets, with ablations and reported losses (ttt, krkp). But the data budgets differ, and the metric is test accuracy and tree size, not evaluation counts (pp. 21–25).
- Methods 1–4: no link (no oracle, no stochastic process, no step parameter).
- new-pattern candidate: *Formulation engineering for exact MILP training*: exhibit the weak LP point, tighten by aggregation, break symmetry with a canonical representative, and prove which variables may be relaxed (pp. 11–14).
- Scope note on the SKILL's *Weak spots* line: integer programming does occur in Scheinberg's work, but as an exact model-training method, not as an integer black box (pp. 7–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before strengthening an IP, exhibit an explicit fractional point that attains the trivial LP bound. It shows which constraints need aggregating (pp. 11–12).
- Prove extreme-point integrality for part of the variables with a perturb-both-ways convex-combination argument, and declare only the rest integer (Proposition 3, pp. 13–14).
- Break symmetry with a canonical representative (samples that have the anchor feature always follow the left branch) that removes no distinct tree (pp. 12–13).
- Use the cheap heuristic (CART) to pre-select features for the exact model, and report results with and without it (pp. 19, 21–23).
- For an imbalanced, safety-critical task, move one metric into a constraint and optimise the other, then plot training vs test values of the constrained metric (pp. 16, 25–26, Fig. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "The purpose of this paper is to show that if an accurate small (interpretable) tree exists for a given data set, it can be obtained in a reasonable time by our proposed model, while popular heuristic methods such as C4.5 [16] and random forests [6] tend to produce less accurate and less interpretable trees." (p. 3)
- "Thus we have a formulation for training optimal decision trees, where the number of integer variables is independent of the number of samples." (p. 14)

### S026 · Global convergence rate analysis of a generic line search algorithm with noise (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2021, DOI 10.1137/19m1291832; arXiv:1910.04055)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. S. Berahas, L. Cao · **Read level**: full. The text is arXiv v2 (3 Mar 2021); there is no appendix. · **Position in research trajectory**: the bounded-noise extension of the Cartis–Scheinberg 2018 line-search analysis [6]. It is the companion of the FoCM gradient-approximation paper [2] and the direct predecessor of S043, which cites it as [3].

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The goal is to minimise φ when only f(x,ξ) = φ(x) + e(x,ξ) can be computed. The error e may be stochastic, deterministic or adversarial, but |e| ≤ ε_f (p. 1). The entry point is the limits of prior analyses. [6] assumes exact function values; [3] and [18] assume the noise can be reduced to any accuracy. Here the noise is not controllable (pp. 2–3). The motivation is smoothing gradient estimators (Gaussian, sphere) in RL and online learning, which had been used only inside fixed-step schemes and in limited settings such as convex functions (pp. 2–3). The authors concede that bounded noise is "a strong assumption" but justify it for computer codes with discretisation error [13, 14] (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithms 2.1–2.2 (pp. 4–5) form a generic line search with an estimated gradient g_k, a direction d_k, and a modified Armijo test f(x_k + αd_k) ≤ f(x_k) + c1 α d_kᵀg_k + 2ε_f (eq. 2.1). On success α is divided by τ, on failure multiplied by τ, and τ = 1 gives a fixed step (p. 5). The single change relative to [6] is the +2ε_f slack. It lets accepted steps increase φ, which breaks [6]'s proof, since that proof relies on monotone f (p. 2). Two gradient conditions are analysed:
  - the norm condition ‖g − ∇φ‖ ≤ θ‖∇φ‖ with probability 1−δ (eqs. 1.2, 4.1), realizable for finite differences, interpolation and smoothing (pp. 3, 14);
  - a modified [6]-type condition ‖g − ∇φ‖ ≤ max{ζε_g, κα_k‖g_k‖} for biased stochastic gradients (eq. 4.3, p. 14).
  
  General descent directions such as quasi-Newton are covered through angle and length conditions (4.14)–(4.15) (p. 24).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: φ ∈ C¹ with L-Lipschitz gradient and bounded below (Assumptions 1.1–1.2, p. 3). |e| ≤ ε_f, where ε_f is known but not assumed controllable (Assumption 1.3, p. 3). Gradient accuracy holds with a probability conditioned on the past (Definitions 3.1 and 4.1, pp. 6, 14–15), with θ < (1−c1)/(2−c1) (p. 14). The convex case needs bounded iterates (Assumption 4.5). The authors admit this is strong because noisy acceptance lets iterates wander; they call the fix simple and omit it (p. 17). Biased gradient estimates must have a known bias bound ε_g (Assumption 4.25, p. 26). The target accuracy ε is tied to ε_f:
  - nonconvex: ε² > 2ε_f L/(γc1(1−θ)(1−2θ−c1(1−θ))), i.e. ε of order √ε_f (Assumption 4.19, p. 23);
  - convex: ε² > max{8ε_f LD²/(…), 16ε_f²} (Assumption 4.6, p. 17);
  - strongly convex: Assumption 4.13 (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The proof generalises [6]'s counting argument. Assumption 3.3 (p. 7) has four parts: a true successful iteration increases z by ≥ h(α_k) − r(ε_f); a true iteration with small α is successful; any successful iteration decreases z by ≤ r(ε_f); and r(ε_f)/h(ᾱ) ≤ γ < 1. The lemmas:
  - counters split iterations into true/false × successful/unsuccessful × large/small step (p. 9);
  - Lemma 3.4, a Wald-type bound E[Σ W_k I_k] ≥ (1−δ) E[Σ W_k] (p. 8);
  - Lemma 3.6, counting step-size moves up and down (pp. 9–10);
  - Lemma 3.9, the key step: total gain minus total loss ≤ Z (p. 11);
  - finally a linear inequality in E[N] is solved (p. 13).
  
  Theorem 3.13: for δ < 1/2 − √γ/2, E[N] ≤ 2(1−δ)/((1−2δ)² − γ) · (2Z/h(ᾱ) + (1−γ) log_τ(ᾱ/α0)) (p. 13). Progress measures by function class are in Table 3.1 (p. 7): 1/Δ_k − 1/Δ_0 (convex), a log ratio (strongly convex), φ(X_0) − φ(X_k) (nonconvex). The resulting rates are O(1/ε) convex (Thm 4.10, p. 19), O(log 1/ε) strongly convex (Thm 4.17, p. 22) and O(1/ε²) nonconvex (Thm 4.21, p. 23). Each reduces to the fixed-step gradient-descent bound at c1 = 1/2 when δ = θ = ε_f = 0 (pp. 20, 22, 23). The Section 4.3 results (Thms 4.29, 4.30, 4.32) are stated "without proof" (pp. 26–28).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none; the paper has no numerical section.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper is an explicit continuation of [6] Cartis–Scheinberg (pp. 1–2, 5). It contrasts itself with [3] Blanchet et al. and [18] Paquette–Scheinberg, which allow unbounded but reducible noise, use a "more complicated" algorithm and have worse constants (p. 2). [1] Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal used the same modified Armijo test, but with bounded gradient error and for strongly convex functions only (p. 5). The norm condition is credited to Carter [5] (p. 13). The smoothing literature cited is Nesterov–Spokoiny [16], Flaxman et al. [9], Fazel et al. [8] and Salimans et al. [20] (pp. 2–3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A summary of results comes early, in the introduction (p. 3). Nearly every theorem is followed by a remark that sets δ = θ = ε_f = 0 and recovers the deterministic bound, a consistency check written into the text (pp. 13, 16, 20, 22, 23, 28). The hedging is candid ("While this is a strong assumption", p. 1; "may seem strong", p. 17). There are cross-reference slips: §4.3 says "under (4.2)" and Remark 4.34 refers to conditions 4.1 or 4.2 where (4.3) is meant (pp. 26, 28).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: A theory-only contribution: one general mechanism (Theorem 3.13) plus explicit neighbourhood-of-convergence formulas for three function classes (pp. 13, 17–23). Limits:
  - the bounds hold in expectation, not with high probability (p. 13);
  - the bound is on a hitting time: "there are no guarantees that the algorithm will remain in the near-optimal neighborhood, once it reaches it". Behaviour near optimality is left to a separate study (p. 29);
  - linking the noise level to the convergence neighbourhood in the setting of [3, 18] is left as future work (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the contract is ‖g − ∇φ‖ ≤ θ‖∇φ‖ with probability 1−δ plus a known noise bound ε_f. The irreducible part fixes the reachable neighbourhood (pp. 3, 14, 17, 23).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the only change to classical Armijo backtracking is the 2ε_f slack, and every result reduces to the deterministic method when the noise is zero (pp. 4–5, 16).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (expected-complexity form): a counting/renewal argument with explicit dependence on δ = 1 − p and γ (p. 13), and deterministic parity (pp. 20, 22, 23).
- Research Heuristic 12 ✅: this paper is its source (eq. 2.1, p. 4; Thm 4.21, p. 23).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the estimators (finite differences, interpolation, smoothing) enter only through the norm condition, and there is no numerical comparison. This is a counterexample to Taste mark 4, *A guarantee comes with numbers* (pp. 3, 28).
- new-pattern candidate: *Analysis-only trade-off knob*: γ "is not an algorithmic parameter". It trades the allowed failure probability δ against the size of the convergence neighbourhood (pp. 8, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Relax a sufficient-decrease test by exactly twice the noise bound (+2ε_f). Every accepted step's true decrease is then the model decrease minus 4ε_f (Lemmas 4.3–4.4, pp. 15–16).
- Put all noise damage into one function r(ε_f) and require r(ε_f)/h(ᾱ) ≤ γ < 1. The neighbourhood-of-convergence condition on ε then follows mechanically for each function class (pp. 7, 17, 20, 23).
- For convex rates under a non-monotone f, bound 1/Δ_{k+1} − 1/(Δ_k + 4ε_f), and use Δ_k ≥ ε > 4ε_f to keep the ratio Δ_k/(Δ_k + 4ε_f) ≥ 1/2 (Lemma 4.8, pp. 18–19).
- After each theorem, add a remark that zeroes the noise and probability parameters and checks that the deterministic bound comes back (pp. 20, 22, 23).

**Quotes**:
- "This modification causes significant changes in the analysis of the expected complexity rates, as the analysis in [6] heavily relies on the fact that objective function can never be increased by the algorithm." (p. 2)
- "In other words, γ is not an algorithmic parameter, it is simply a parameter whose value implies a particular bound on the neighborhood of convergence." (p. 8)

### S043 · High probability complexity bounds for line search based on stochastic oracles (Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 2021, arXiv:2106.06454)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: B. Jin, M. Xie · **Read level**: full, with a **version caveat**. The text file is arXiv v5 (2 Nov 2023), titled *High probability complexity bounds for adaptive step search based on stochastic oracles* (p. 1, printed in capitals). This is the extended version, and it cites the NeurIPS paper as [9] (p. 4; reference list, p. 29). All page references and claims below refer to v5, not to the NeurIPS 2021 proceedings. The proofs of the oracle constructions (Props. 7–9) are deferred to "[9, Appendix A]" and are not in this text: not read. · **Position in research trajectory**: it turns the expected-complexity line ([5] Cartis–Scheinberg 2018, [16] Paquette–Scheinberg, [3] = S026) into high-probability tail bounds, and widens the zeroth-order noise model from bounded to one-sided subexponential.

- **D1 Problem & entry point**: The entry is three limits of the earlier step-search papers (pp. 2–3). (1) The zeroth-order oracle was exact [5], bounded [3], or unbounded with small variance but at the price of a complicated method [16]. (2) The first-order oracle had ε_g = 0 and τ = ∞, which forced an upper bound on the step size. (3) Only expected complexity was available. A competitor from ML adds pressure: SLS ("SGD + Armijo") [20] needs per-sample smoothness, g = ∇f(x,ξ), a strong growth condition and a step-size cap, which make it no better than fixed-step SGD (pp. 3–4, 28–29). The goals are listed in five bullets (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: SASS (Algorithm 2.1, p. 5). Each iteration draws a fresh gradient estimate, so the direction changes during backtracking; hence "step search" rather than line search (footnotes 1 and 3, pp. 1–2; p. 4). It tests the modified Armijo condition f(x⁺) ≤ f(x) − αθ‖g‖² + 2ε'_f, then expands α by 1/γ on success and shrinks it by γ on failure. The algorithm is the same as in [3]; the novelty lies in the oracles and the analysis (p. 4). The oracles (p. 1):
  - SZO(ε_f, ν, b): mean error ≤ ε_f, and the error is one-sided subexponential;
  - SFO(ε_g, τ, κ, δ): ‖g − ∇φ‖ ≤ max{ε_g, min{τ, κα}‖g‖} with probability ≥ 1−δ.
  
  The oracle constants are intrinsic and unknown to the algorithm; the only noise-related input is ε'_f ≥ ε_f (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: ∇φ is L-Lipschitz and φ is bounded below; the algorithm does not know L (pp. 1, 4). Only the noise of the difference f(x) − f(x⁺) needs to be subexponential (p. 5). Light tails are justified by Hoeffding (bounded loss) or by the CLT (averaging) (p. 2). The convex case assumes bounded iterates (Assumption 5, p. 22). Neighbourhood conditions:
  - nonconvex: ε > max{ε_g/η, c·√(4ε'_f/(θ(p − ½)))} (Inequality 1, p. 15);
  - strongly convex / PL: Inequality 2 (p. 18);
  - convex: needs both a gap tolerance ε0 and a gradient tolerance ε1, because near small gradients a biased oracle can point the wrong way (pp. 6, 22).
  
  There is an internal tension on independence. p. 4 says the NeurIPS version's "strong assumption on the independence of the errors in the function estimates" is removed. Yet p. 10 still says the unbounded case "will need an additional assumption that the noise on different iterations is independent", and p. 17 mentions "i.i.d. subexponential noises". The proof of Proposition 1 (p. 12) uses only subexponentiality conditional on F'_{k−1}.
- **D4 Analysis & proof techniques**: The skeleton (pp. 7–14):
  - Assumption 3 (i)–(v) on the process {Z_k, A_k, I_k, Θ_k};
  - Lemma 3.1: Azuma–Hoeffding on Σ I_k − pt;
  - a large/small-step indicator U_k (Definition 4, p. 7);
  - Lemma 3.2 / Cor. 3.3: large successful steps ≥ ½(large steps − d), by counting step-size moves (p. 8);
  - Lemma 3.4: small true steps ≤ small false steps (p. 8);
  - Lemma 3.5, the key lemma: on {T_ε > t, Σ I_k ≥ p̂t}, the number of good iterations is ≥ (p̂ − ½)t − d/2. This is a deterministic implication; its complement has probability 0 (p. 9).
  
  With bounded noise this gives Theorem 3.6, P(T_ε ≤ t) ≥ 1 − exp(−(p − p̂)²t/(2p²)) (p. 10). With subexponential noise, the *sum* of the damages r(ε'_f, E_k + E_k⁺) is bounded by a Bernstein-type inequality (Proposition 1, pp. 11–12), giving Theorem 3.8 (pp. 12–13). Proposition 2 (sums and affine maps of subexponentials, p. 14) and Proposition 5 (the log of a subexponential variable ≥ 1 is subexponential, p. 21) give the parameters of r for each function class. Rates:
  - nonconvex: of order [1/(p − ½ − (4ε'_f + s)/(Cε²))] · (φ(x0) − φ*)/(Cε²) with overwhelming probability, with O(L) dependence vs O(L³) in [16] (Remark after Thm 4.1, p. 17);
  - strongly convex / PL: log form (Thm 4.2, p. 21);
  - convex: Thm 4.3 (pp. 23–24).
  
  Oracle realisations: sample sizes for mini-batch oracles (Prop. 8, p. 24); randomized finite differences with bias ε_g = 2(√n Lσ + √n ε_f/σ), minimised at σ = O(√ε_f) (Prop. 9 and Remark, p. 25).
- **D5 Numerical experiments** (pp. 26–28):
  - Convex tests: RBF-kernel logistic regression on all 64 binary PMLB datasets with 150–5000 points. Competitors: SASS, SLS [20] with its suggested parameters (after the authors "tried various parameter combinations") and ADAM with five learning rates. 5 trials from Gaussian random starts.
  - Equal work budget, counted in inner products: ADAM runs 100 epochs, SASS 50 (pp. 26–27). The metric is the median over trials of the best test loss.
  - Results: SASS beats SLS on 32/64 datasets and beats ADAM at every learning rate except 10⁻¹, where ADAM wins on 47/64 (p. 27).
  - Nonconvex tests: MLP and CNN on MNIST, ResNet18 on FashionMNIST. The x-axis counts forward plus backward passes, and SASS uses 3 per iteration (p. 27).
  - Sensitivity to ε'_f: 0, 1/5, 1/2 and 1 × the empirical standard deviation of 30 zeroth-order calls, re-estimated every epoch. The method is robust unless ε'_f = 0 (Fig. 1, p. 26).
  - Honest caveat: the oracles use a fixed mini-batch of 128, so the theory's oracle properties are not checked (p. 26).
  - Losses are reported: ADAM with lr 10⁻¹ wins on the convex set. The explanation for SLS on the CNN (its step size collapses) is labelled a hypothesis (pp. 27–28).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper extends [3] (= S026) and [5] Cartis–Scheinberg. It improves on [16] Paquette–Scheinberg in constants (p. 17) and simplicity (p. 3). The idea for the tail bound comes from Gratton et al. [7] (p. 3). The critique of SLS [20] is direct: its per-sample smoothness assumption is called very strong and often false in practice, and its nonconvex analysis makes the line search "essentially impractical" (pp. 3–4, 28–29). The paper introduces the name "step search" to separate these methods from classical line search (footnotes 1 and 3, pp. 1–2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The oracle definitions come on page 1, before the algorithm. Each oracle parameter is justified by a practical reason: ε_g from the largest allowable mini-batch, τ < ∞ for realism (p. 2). The contributions are a bullet list (p. 3). The proof engine is described in words before the lemmas (first quote below, p. 7; "The engine of the analysis is a key lemma", p. 8). A remark after Theorem 4.1 turns the tail bound into an order statement and recovers the deterministic case (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: Contributions:
  - theory: high-probability bounds for three function classes under biased, light-tailed oracles;
  - a simpler algorithm, with no step-size cap;
  - oracle constructions for mini-batch and finite-difference settings (pp. 3–4, 24–25).
  
  Limits:
  - the experiments do not implement adaptive oracles;
  - practical variants (adaptive mini-batch, choice of γ and ε'_f) are left to future work (p. 26);
  - ε'_f is fixed in the analysis but re-estimated in practice, and values smaller than the theory dictates work (p. 29);
  - the convex case still needs bounded iterates (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (the strongest in this batch): SZO/SFO definitions with intrinsic parameters, bias allowed, and accuracy adapting with α (pp. 1–2, 4). Oracles are constructed from mini-batches and from randomized finite differences (pp. 24–25).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the Armijo-type step search is kept, and the only change is the 2ε'_f relaxation of the acceptance test. The algorithm is identical to that of [3] (pp. 4–5).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a high-probability tail bound via Azuma–Hoeffding, a deterministic counting lemma and a Bernstein bound on accumulated damage (pp. 7–13). Matches Research Heuristic 6 (expected → high probability).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: an equal-work comparison (inner products, network passes) against ADAM and SLS. But the fixed-batch oracles do not meet the theory, and ADAM gets a learning-rate sweep (pp. 26–27).
- Research Heuristic 7 ✅ (relaxed acceptance, pp. 4–5). Warning sign 1, the hand-tuned step schedule ✅: stated in the abstract (p. 1) and visible in the experiments, where ADAM's best learning rate changes between the convex and nonconvex tests (p. 28).
- new-pattern candidate: *Oracle parameters unknown to the algorithm*: the theory is stated in oracle constants the method never uses. Only one user-supplied upper bound, ε'_f, enters the algorithm (p. 4).
- new-pattern candidate: *Calibrate the noise slack empirically*: ε'_f is set from the standard deviation of repeated oracle calls at the current point, re-estimated every epoch, with a sensitivity plot (p. 26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- High-probability template in three steps: (1) Azuma–Hoeffding on Σ I_k; (2) a *deterministic* lemma: if the method has not stopped and enough iterations were true, then enough iterations were good; (3) a union with a concentration bound on the accumulated damage (pp. 7–13).
- Classify each iteration as a large or small step relative to ᾱ, and count step-size moves up and down to relate successful and unsuccessful iterations (pp. 7–8).
- State oracle accuracy as max{ε_g, min{τ, κα}‖g‖}. ε_g is the floor the oracle cannot beat. τ caps looseness when α is large and removes the need for an α_max (pp. 1–3).
- Give every method the same budget in the dominant computational unit (inner products, forward/backward passes), not in iterations or epochs (p. 27).
- For zeroth-order oracles, write the finite-difference bias ε_g(σ) and choose σ = O(√ε_f) to minimise the convergence neighbourhood (p. 25).
