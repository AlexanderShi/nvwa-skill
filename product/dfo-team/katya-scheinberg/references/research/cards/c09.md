# Batch c09 · Katya Scheinberg · paper cards

**Batch intent**: Three 2025–2026 papers push the programme in three directions. (1) An oracle weaker than zeroth order: comparisons only, and no objective function at all (S087). (2) The group's probabilistic-oracle, high-probability machinery transplanted onto a second-order adaptive method, cubic regularization (S093). (3) The Powell-style geometry programme closed off with noise, quadratic models and a full benchmark against Powell's own code (S104). The question is which SKILL.md methods survive when function values disappear, when the method is second order, and when theory has to meet practice on CUTEst.
**Focus dimensions**: D1–D8, all filled because every paper is core. The emphasis is on D2 (what was kept from the classical method and what was changed), D4 (proof skeleton and which framework is reused) and D5 (S104 is the only paper here with experiments).
**Material roles**: S087 core · S093 core · S104 core.

### S087 · Function-free optimization via comparison oracles (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2604.26867)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Zikai Xiong (Georgia Tech) · **Read**: full text, pp. 1–46. The file is arXiv v2, 18 May 2026 (p. 1). The paper has no appendix and no numerical section. References (pp. 44–46) were skimmed. · **Position in trajectory**: a new direction. The oracle is weaker than the zeroth-order oracles of the stochastic-oracle line, and the objective function itself is removed. None of Scheinberg's own earlier papers are cited (reference list, pp. 44–46). The only DFO-group tool borrowed is the Haar cosine-measure lemma of Gratton–Royer–Vicente–Zhang [11] (p. 19).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a modelling objection, not a missing rate. Earlier comparison-oracle work treats comparisons "as a weak interface to a latent scalar objective function". Its guarantees are stated through function-value gaps, regret or gradient norms (p. 2). Preference applications (search and ranking, A/B testing, clinical tuning, RLHF; pp. 1–2) may have no application-given objective, or only a non-identifiable one. Figure 1 is the argument: three functions (convex, nonconvex and discontinuous) share the same level sets. So they induce the same preference, and every comparison-based algorithm follows identical trajectories on them (pp. 2–3). Three questions structure the paper (Question 1, p. 2; Questions 2–3, p. 4):
  1. how to formulate optimisation from a preference relation alone;
  2. whether normal directions can be estimated optimally and adaptively;
  3. whether optimal and adaptive methods can be built on those estimates.

  The oracle is noiseless. Fixed-advantage noisy oracles are reduced to it by repetition via [35]. Noise that depends on function differences is excluded as "no longer function-free" (pp. 5, 11).
  - Transferable technique: argue for a new problem formulation through an invariance: exhibit objects the oracle cannot tell apart, and require guarantees stated only in invariant quantities (Figure 1, Prop. 2.9, pp. 2–3, 10–11).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Every smooth-optimisation object is replaced by a geometric object of the preference relation (pp. 3–4, 9, 12):
  - the gradient by the outward normal n_x to the preference sublevel set S_x;
  - f − f* by the level-set optimality gap Δ_LS(x) = dist(X*, L_x);
  - stationarity by the regularity radius r_x, the largest radius of two tangent balls.

  Normal-direction estimation uses an "orthogonal-complement construction" (p. 4). It does not search for n_x. Instead it builds d−1 approximately tangential orthonormal directions and reads n_x off the remaining direction, with one comparison for the sign (Alg. 2, p. 16). Each tangential direction comes from bisection on the unit circle in a plane span{v_i, q_{i+1}} of a Haar frame, with a random in-plane rotation (Alg. 1, p. 15). A rotation step keeps orthonormality, so no linear system is solved (Lemma 3.4, pp. 15–16).

  The adaptive variant (Alg. 3, p. 19) halves the comparison radius h while both x+hu and x−hu are worse than x. Under convexity this yields an exact one-sided certificate (Lemma 3.2, p. 13). The optimisation layer keeps two classical schemes:
  - NDD (Alg. 5, p. 29) is projected normalized gradient descent with estimated normals. It uses step η = D1/√K and keeps a best-so-far point at the cost of one comparison per iteration (pp. 28–30).
  - adaNDD (Alg. 6, p. 36) uses KT coin-betting (Orabona–Pál) in anchored form, with a projection correction g_k = n̂_k + max{0, −⟨n̂_k, s_k⟩}s_k. Each normal-estimation call gets a comparison budget B_k (eq. (56), p. 35).

  Cutting-plane methods are considered and set aside. They would need Õ(d² log(dR/ε)) comparisons and at least quadratic per-iteration cost in d (§3.5, pp. 26–27).
  - Transferable technique: to estimate a unit direction from sign-only feedback, estimate its orthogonal complement by d−1 one-dimensional bisections (O(log(1/ε)) each), then fix the sign with one query (Algs. 1–2, pp. 14–16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Everything is imposed on the preference relation, never on a representative function:
  - completeness and transitivity (Def. 2.1, p. 7);
  - plateau-freeness (ties only on the boundary) and convexity of the sublevel sets (Def. 2.5, p. 8), with convexity invoked only for global guarantees (p. 8);
  - r-regularity via two-sided tangent balls (Defs. 2.6–2.7, p. 9);
  - Euclidean projections onto C are available (p. 7).

  Lemma 2.10 is the bridge to smooth theory: if an L-smooth f realises the relation, then r_x ≥ ‖∇f(x)‖/L (p. 11). A small r_x is a stronger certificate than a small gradient; f = t³ gives r_0 = ∞ (p. 12). The local growth condition r_x ≥ min{γ1Δ_LS(x), γ2} (Condition 4.7, p. 32) is verified for five cases (Examples 4.8–4.13, pp. 32–34):
  - convex quadratics;
  - the distance to a convex set;
  - L-smooth functions with local strong convexity;
  - PL plus quadratic growth, possibly nonconvex;
  - bounded C with a nonvanishing gradient.

  It is also verified for McKinnon's Nelder–Mead counterexample. The conclusion expects, without proof, that the theory carries over to Hilbert spaces (p. 44).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  1. Halfspace certificate: a comparison of x with x+hu gives ⟨n_x,u⟩ ≥ −τ or ≤ τ, with τ = h/(2r_x), from tangent-ball geometry (Lemma 3.1, p. 13). Under convexity the certificate is exact (Lemma 3.2, p. 13).
  2. Planar bisection keeps a bracket invariant, giving |⟨n_x,y⟩| ≤ τ + π/2^{T+2} (Lemma 3.3, pp. 14–15).
  3. An orthonormal-basis decomposition gives ‖n_x − n̂‖ ≤ 2√(d−1)(h/r_x + π/2^{T+1}). The bound separates a geometry bias from the bisection error (Thm 3.5, pp. 16–17). With h ≤ εr_x/(8√(d−1)) the count is O(d log(d/ε)) comparisons (Cor. 3.6, p. 17).
  4. Adaptive radius:
     - halvings are bounded by log2(h_in/(r_x|⟨n_x,u⟩|)) (Lemma 3.8, pp. 18–19);
     - Haar anti-concentration gives P(|⟨v,q⟩| ≤ ρ) ≤ √(2d/π)ρ (Lemma 3.9, p. 19);
     - the random rotation makes the offset of the dyadic grid uniform (eqs. (18)–(25), pp. 21–22).

     Together these give a count with an additive 4⌈log2(1/δ)⌉ term, i.e. an exponentially decaying tail (Thm 3.11, pp. 20–23).
  5. Lower bound: an adversary keeps the larger half-cell of the sphere. Spherical-cap volume and Wendel's inequality then give Ω(d log(1/ε)) comparisons (Thm 3.14, Cor. 3.15, pp. 24–25).
  6. NDD needs two facts: Δ_LS(x) ≤ ⟨n_x, x−x*⟩ from the supporting halfspace (Lemma 4.2, p. 29), and monotonicity of Δ_LS under preference (Lemma 4.3, p. 29). The proof then telescopes the distance recursion of Shor's normalized-subgradient proof plus an accumulated estimation-error term. The result is a two-case guarantee: either Δ_LS(x̂) ≤ ε̂, or some iterate has a small r_x and x̂ is preferred to it (Thm 4.4, pp. 29–31). Under Condition 4.7 this becomes Õ(dD²/ε²) comparisons over O(D²/ε²) normal steps (Cor. 4.14, pp. 34–35).
  7. adaNDD combines four pieces (Lemmas 4.15–4.18, Thm 4.19, pp. 36–40):
     - the KT regret bound;
     - a projection-correction inequality ⟨n̂_k, x_k−x*⟩ ≤ ⟨g_k, z_k−x*⟩;
     - boundedness of the iterates;
     - a budget-exhaustion certificate, with a union bound over δ_k = 6δ/(π²k²) showing that early stopping implies r_{x_τ} < r* with probability ≥ 1−δ.
  8. Span-method lower bound: a zero-chain instance max_i x_i, smoothed by convolution with a kernel supported on the ordered simplex so that the preference is regular, gives Δ_LS ≥ 3D/(4√(K+1)) (Thm 4.23, pp. 41–43).
  - Transferable technique: give an adaptive subroutine a budget equal to its high-probability cost in the good regime. If the budget runs out, that is itself a certificate, with probability 1−δ, that you are in the near-stationary regime (eq. (56), Thm 4.19, pp. 35, 40).
  - Transferable technique: to make a nonsmooth zero-chain lower-bound instance satisfy a regularity assumption, convolve it with a kernel whose support preserves the coordinate ordering (eqs. (74)–(75), Lemma 4.22, pp. 41–42).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. There is no numerical section; the paper runs §1–§5 and §5 is the summary (pp. 7, 43–44).
- **D6 Positioning**: The literature is reframed, not attacked. Classical comparison methods (Hooke–Jeeves, Nelder–Mead) are said to have poor or no guarantees. Direct search with complexity needs sufficient decrease, which requires function differences (p. 5). Jamieson et al. [14] are credited as the first to name a "comparison oracle" (p. 2). Golovin et al. [9] are credited for noticing the invariance under monotone transformations (pp. 2, 5). Competing normalized-gradient estimators are translated into this paper's notation and compared on two axes, the admissible comparison radius and the comparison count (pp. 17–18):
  - Zhang–Li [45] need h ≲ εr_x/d^{3/2}, against εr_x/√d here;
  - the concurrent Tao et al. [39] loosen the radius, but only with constant success probability;
  - Karabag et al. [16] cost quadratically in d;
  - sign averaging costs polynomially in d/ε.

  The concurrent lower bound of [39] is acknowledged openly (p. 26).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Three numbered Questions map one-to-one to §§2–4 and to the three contributions (pp. 2, 4, 7).
  - One figure carries the motivation (Figure 1, p. 3).
  - Two named design principles, "optimality and adaptivity", are announced (p. 4) and then checked for every layer.
  - An interpretation paragraph follows each theorem, e.g. "the price of adaptivity", a factor of about two (p. 23), and the split of the error into two sources (p. 17).
  - Hedging is rare and flagged: "we believe that NDD may also apply" to nonconvex relations (p. 32); "we expect" the Hilbert-space extension (p. 44).
  - Small language slips: "suppresse" (p. 6), "incribing" (p. 9), "sometime" (p. 11).
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: Contributions (pp. 3–4, 43):
  - conceptual: a new problem class, the optimality measure Δ_LS and the stationarity measure r_x;
  - algorithmic: a deterministic near-optimal normal estimator and its adaptive version, plus NDD and adaNDD;
  - theoretical: matching lower bounds for normal-direction estimation and for the number of normal steps of span-based methods.

  Limitations are stated specifically: global guarantees need convexity; the oracle is noiseless; the lower bounds cover only direction estimation and the step count, so "the minimax number of pairwise comparisons required for function-free optimization" is open (p. 44). Future work: nonconvex function-free theory, and certifying a point preferred to one with a small regularity radius (p. 44). There are no experiments, and the paper does not say this is a limitation.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the contract is exactness of a sign-valued oracle, not accuracy with probability p (Def. 2.2, pp. 7, 11). The Method 1 move survives in the derived oracle. The normal-estimation accuracy is scheduled against the iterate, ε_k = min{1/2, 1/(√k(1+‖x_k−x_1‖))} (Alg. 6, p. 36). The comparison radius must shrink with the local geometry, h ≲ εr_x/√d (Cor. 3.6, p. 17), the analogue of a sampling radius tied to Δ_k.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the outer methods are kept, namely Shor's normalized subgradient proof (p. 32) and KT coin-betting (p. 36). Only the direction oracle is new. Backtracking reappears on the comparison radius h instead of the step (Alg. 3, p. 19).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: randomness is internal to the estimator, not in the oracle. High probability is claimed only for the comparison count, with a log(1/δ) tail (Thm 3.11, p. 23). The yardstick is not "deterministic parity" but matching minimax lower bounds at each layer (pp. 4, 24–26, 41–43).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: estimators are compared head-to-head on admissible radius and comparison count, but only in theory (pp. 17–18).
- New-pattern candidate: invariance-first formulation, where guarantees are stated only in quantities the oracle can distinguish (pp. 2–3, 10–11).
- New-pattern candidate: lower bound for every layer, i.e. estimator and outer method each matched to a minimax bound (pp. 23–26, 41–43).
- New-pattern candidate: a two-case guarantee, near-optimal or certified near-stationary (Thm 4.4, Thm 4.19, pp. 29–31, 38–40).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Invariance test before choosing an optimality measure: list the transformations the oracle cannot see, and discard any measure that is not invariant under them (Prop. 2.9, p. 10).
- Error split: bound estimator error as (geometry-induced bias) + (algorithmic truncation error), then set each parameter to make its term ε/2 (Thm 3.5, Cor. 3.6, pp. 16–17).
- A random rotation of a dyadic query grid turns a worst-case smallest inner product into a high-probability logarithmic cost (eqs. (18)–(25), pp. 21–22).
- Budget-exhaustion certificate for adaptive inner loops (Thm 4.19, pp. 35, 40).
- A smoothed zero-chain hard instance for lower bounds under regularity assumptions (Lemma 4.22, Thm 4.23, pp. 41–43).

**Quotes**:
- "In summary, prior work aims to optimize a scalar function through weaker oracles. In contrast, our central goal is to formulate an optimization problem when the preference relation itself is the basic object defining optimality." (p. 2)
- "Optimality matters because we seek worst-case complexity guarantees that cannot be substantially improved in general." (p. 4)

### S093 · First-and Second-Order Stochastic Adaptive Regularization with Cubics: High-Probability Iteration and Sample Complexity (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2026, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2025.0123; arXiv:2308.13161)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Miaolan Xie (Purdue) · **Read**: full text, pp. 1–21. The file is arXiv v2, 21 Apr 2025 (header date April 23, 2025, p. 1), not the journal version. There is no appendix and no numerical section. References (pp. 19–21) were skimmed. · **Position in trajectory**: a journal extension of the WSC 2023 paper [SX23], which had only first-order iteration complexity (footnote, p. 1). It moves the cubic-regularization method of Cartis–Scheinberg 2018 [CS18] into the high-probability framework of JSX24, and it gets its sample complexity from JSX25 (pp. 3, 8, 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: ARC is the deterministic winner: O(ε^{-3/2}) against O(ε^{-2}) for line search and trust region [CGT11] (p. 2). The stochastic ARC literature falls short in two ways (p. 2):
  - analyses with only expected bounds, which need an exact or deterministically bounded zeroth-order oracle [CS18, BG22, BGMT…];
  - a non-adaptive method [TSJ+18], which needs known Lipschitz constants and accurate estimates at every iteration. Its bound holds only on that event, so "No expected complexity bound can thus be derived".

  The gap is high-probability iteration and sample complexity for ARC under the stochastic zeroth-, first- and second-order oracles already used for the group's trust-region and line-search methods (pp. 2–3). The stated settings are expected-risk minimisation and simulation optimisation (abstract, p. 1; p. 16).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (p. 4) is ARC with three stochastic ingredients:
  1. the oracle accuracy inputs are tied to the regularisation parameter: µ/σ_k for the gradient and √(µ/σ_k) for the Hessian;
  2. the acceptance ratio carries a +2ε'_f "error correction" (eq. (8));
  3. σ_k is decreased by γ on success and increased by 1/γ otherwise (eq. (10)).

  The step conditions (6)–(7) are the deterministic ones of CGT11 (pp. 3–4). The slack is justified in one sentence (quoted below, p. 4). The second-order Algorithm 2 (p. 11) changes two things:
  - tighter oracle inputs, min{µ/σ_k, µ/σ_k²} and the matching square roots;
  - an extra subproblem condition ‖s_k‖ ≥ η2·(−2λ_min(H_k))/σ_k, which relaxes by η2 ∈ (0,1) Nesterov–Polyak's property of exact subproblem solutions (Prop. 1, eqs. (29)–(30), pp. 11–12).

  Setting α_k = 1/σ_k makes SARC formally an adaptive step-size method, so it fits JSX24 (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: scale each oracle's accuracy by the method's own adaptive parameter, with the power that the Taylor model needs for that derivative order: gradient ∝ 1/σ, Hessian ∝ 1/√σ (Alg. 1, p. 4; Remark 1, p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Assumption 1: bounded below, C², with L-Lipschitz gradient and L_H-Lipschitz Hessian (p. 1).
  - SZO(ε_f, λ, a): the error is sub-exponential with mean ≤ ε_f (eqs. (1)–(2), pp. 1–2).
  - SFO(κ_g): ‖∇ϕ − g‖ ≤ κ_gµ1 with probability ≥ 1−δ1 (eq. (3), p. 2).
  - SSO(κ_H): the operator-norm error is ≤ κ_Hµ2 with probability ≥ 1−δ2 (eq. (4), p. 2).

  The authors admit this is stronger than JSX24/CBS22: SFO and SSO must be implementable for arbitrarily small µ1 and µ2, "this happens only with small probability" (p. 3). A true iteration requires the gradient error and the Hessian error along the step to be ≤ κ·max{µ/σ_k, ‖s_k‖²}, and e_k + e_k⁺ ≤ 2ε'_f (Def. 1, p. 4). Assumption 2 (p. 9) requires ε'_f > ε_f and p = 1 − δ1 − δ2 − exp(−min{u²/2K², u/2K}) > 1/2, and puts a floor on ε. Remark 2 reads that floor as O(ε_f^{2/3}), "better dependency" than the O(√ε_f) of SASS and stochastic trust region (p. 9). Remark 1 notes that the relaxed inputs max{µ/σ_k, ‖s_k‖²} would suffice, but they depend on s_k and so are left open (p. 5). For sample complexity the paper assumes unbiased minibatch estimates with variances v_f², v_g², v_h² (eqs. (39)–(42), p. 16), and the dimension is treated as a constant (footnote, p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The proof has two layers.
  1. Deterministic, per-realisation lemmas (pp. 5–8), announced by a roadmap paragraph (p. 5):
     - model decrease ≥ σ‖s‖³/6 (Lemma 1);
     - σ ≥ σ̄ gives success or a tiny step (Lemma 2);
     - the step norm is bounded below by ‖∇ϕ(x⁺)‖ (Lemma 3);
     - the step is not tiny until ε-stationarity (Lemma 4);
     - a true and successful iteration decreases ϕ by ∝ ε^{3/2} − 4ε'_f (Lemma 5).
  2. Theorem 2 (p. 10) checks, item by item, the five properties of JSX24's generic process, each with a plain-English gloss:
     - (i) P(I_k | F) ≥ p;
     - (ii) a small α and a true iteration give success;
     - (iii) a true, successful iteration gives Z_{k+1} ≤ Z_k − h(A_k) + 4ε'_f;
     - (iv) h(ᾱ) > 4ε'_f/(p − 1/2);
     - (v) the damage per iteration is bounded.

  The ARC order enters only through h(α) ∝ ε^{3/2} (p. 10). Theorem 3 then imports the JSX24 tail: P(T_ε ≤ t+1) ≥ 1 − exp(−(p−p̂)²t/(2p²)) − exp(−min{s²t/8K², st/4K}) (p. 10). One change was needed: because ARC measures progress at the trial point, the stopping time is shifted and the paper bounds T_ε ≤ t+1 rather than T_ε ≤ t (Def. 2, p. 9; p. 10). The expected bound and almost-sure finiteness follow from the exponential tail and Borel–Cantelli (Remark 3, p. 11).

  The second-order case needs a new step-length bound. Lemma 9 uses Hessian Lipschitz continuity, the SSO accuracy and condition (30) to show ‖s_k‖² ≥ ε/(σ_k/η2 + 2L_H)² whenever λ_min(∇²ϕ(x⁺)) < −√ε (pp. 13–14). Then h becomes a minimum of two terms (eq. (38), Thm 4, p. 15), and Theorem 5 repeats the tail bound (p. 15).

  Sample complexity comes from three steps:
  - σ_k is dominated by a one-sided geometric random walk;
  - JSX25 Theorem 3 (restated as Theorem 6) gives α*(n) ≥ βᾱ with high probability when γ is chosen as a function of n (p. 17);
  - Chebyshev gives per-iteration minibatch sizes O(v_g²/ε²), O(v_h²/ε) and O(v_f²/ε³) (pp. 17–18).

  Multiplying by O(ε^{-3/2}) iterations gives O(ε^{-3.5}) gradient samples, O(ε^{-2.5}) Hessian samples and O(ε^{-4.5}) function samples (Theorem 7, eqs. (43)–(44), p. 18).
  - Transferable technique: when a new method must enter an existing stochastic-process framework, first prove realisation-wise lemmas. Then state one theorem whose items are exactly the framework's hypotheses, each with a one-line gloss, and import the framework's tail bound unchanged (Theorem 2 → Theorem 3, p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: none, and the paper says why: "We do not discuss the numerical performance of our method in this paper" (p. 3). The reason given is that ARC's efficiency depends heavily on the subproblem solver, and "We expect similar behavior in the stochastic case" (p. 3).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper positions itself as the extension of JSX24 and CBS22 to the CS18 framework, which "requires careful modification of most of the elements of the existing analysis" (p. 3). The related-work paragraph presents SZO/SFO/SSO as a shared standard. They appear in the group's CS18, PS20, JSX24, BSV14, CMS18, BCMS19 and CBS22, and in others' MWX23 and BXZ23 (p. 2). The main foil is TSJ+18. The critique is concrete: non-adaptive, known Lipschitz constants, no expected bound (p. 2). Remark 5 gives an even-handed comparison (p. 19):
  - gradient samples: O(ε^{-3.5}), matching TSJ+18;
  - Hessian samples: O(ε^{-2.5}) against O(ε^{-3.5}) Hessian-vector products in TSJ+18;
  - function samples: O(ε^{-4.5}), which TSJ+18 does not need at all.

  The trade is justified by adaptivity and by weaker, possibly biased or corrupted, derivative oracles (p. 19). Subspace and variance-reduced cubic methods are set aside as different settings (pp. 2–3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract closes with a claim from complexity alone: "as in the deterministic case, they outperform other stochastic adaptive methods" (p. 1).
  - Contributions (p. 3): "the first comprehensive analysis", two numbered allowances on the oracles, then a claim to be the first to establish first- and second-order iteration complexity bounds matching the deterministic O(ε^{-3/2}).
  - The stronger assumption is disclosed in the contributions paragraph itself, together with an argument that it rarely binds (p. 3).
  - A roadmap paragraph explains the role of each lemma before any proof (p. 5).
  - Remarks interpret constants against competitors (Remark 2, p. 9; Remark 5, p. 19).
  - Hedging is low, apart from the unverified numerical expectation (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: The contribution is theory only: the first high-probability and expected iteration complexity for first- and second-order stochastic ARC, O(ε^{-3/2}) matching the deterministic order, plus high-probability sample complexity and a smaller noise floor O(ε_f^{2/3}) (pp. 3, 9, 11, 15, 18). Limitations are stated concretely:
  - the SFO/SSO assumption is stronger (p. 3);
  - the relaxed oracle inputs are left open (Remark 1, p. 5);
  - there are no numerics (p. 3);
  - dimension is ignored in sample counts (p. 16);
  - function-value sample complexity is worse than methods that avoid function values (p. 19).

  Future work: implementable relaxed inputs (p. 5) and a numerical study (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: three explicit contracts (SZO, SFO, SSO). Accuracy is tied to the adaptive parameter (µ/σ_k, √(µ/σ_k)). The irreducible ε_f fixes the neighbourhood, now O(ε_f^{2/3}) (pp. 1–2, 4, 9).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: ARC is kept with CGT11's step conditions. What changed is the oracle inputs and the 2ε'_f slack. For second order, only a relaxed exact-solution property (30) is added (pp. 3–4, 11–12).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the stopping time has a sub-exponential tail. Expected and high-probability O(ε^{-3/2}) match the deterministic ARC order (pp. 3, 10–11, 15).
- Heuristic 10 ✅ evidence: the high-probability lower bound on the step parameter (JSX25) is converted into total sample complexity (pp. 17–18).
- Research Taste mark 4 ✗ contradiction: no numbers accompany the guarantee, and the abstract still claims SARC outperforms other stochastic adaptive methods (pp. 1, 3).
- New-pattern candidate: framework-then-instantiation (already in the pool from c04). A new method is certified by checking a published abstract process's hypotheses (i)–(v) (Theorem 2, p. 10).
- New-pattern candidate: noise-floor exponent as a comparison axis. A higher-order method is sold partly on reaching a smaller neighbourhood, ε_f^{2/3} versus √ε_f (Remark 2, p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A hypothesis-checklist theorem that plugs a new algorithm into an existing tail-bound framework (Theorem 2, p. 10).
- Accuracy inputs tied to the adaptive parameter with derivative-order-specific powers (Alg. 1, p. 4; Alg. 2, p. 11).
- A +2ε'_f acceptance slack, where ε'_f is any upper bound on the mean zeroth-order error, not necessarily the tightest (eq. (8), p. 4).
- A shift of the stopping time by one when the deterministic proof measures progress at the trial point (Def. 2, p. 9; p. 10).
- Relaxing an exact-subproblem property by a factor η2 ∈ (0,1) to get an implementable termination test (eq. (30), p. 12).
- Iteration → sample complexity: per-iteration minibatch size from Chebyshev at the high-probability smallest step parameter, times the iteration bound (pp. 17–18).

**Quotes**:
- "This is because function value estimates have an irreducible error, so without this correction term, the algorithm may always reject improvement steps." (p. 4)
- "We do not discuss the numerical performance of our method in this paper." (p. 3)

### S104 · Powell-Style Model-Based Derivative-Free Optimization with Complexity Guarantees (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2609.09441)

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a practice–theory gap. Powell's model-based trust-region methods (UOBYQA, NEWUOA) are very effective in practice (p. 1). Z. Zhang's PDFO/PRIMA have made them widely used again, "This raises the interest in providing solid theory" (p. 2). But the existing complexity results [15] cover simplified methods that "require occasional complete model reconstruction" and "essentially abandon Powell’s careful geometry correcting approach" (pp. 1–2). Even [11] departs from Powell, because it discards potentially useful sample points (p. 2). Five extensions are promised (p. 2):
  1. keep those points;
  2. quadratic models at no extra complexity cost;
  3. deterministic noise and the noise floor it implies;
  4. noisy random subspaces;
  5. a careful implementation that matches Powell's codes.

  The complexity measure is function evaluations. Because every such method is O(ε^{-2}) in ε, the paper declares the n-dependence of Ψ(n,ε) and the noise floor ψ(ε_f) to be the real targets (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: when every competitor has the same ε-order, say so, and move the contribution to the constants that still differ: dimension dependence and noise floor (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The framework (Alg. 1, p. 4) is a standard trust region with two DFO-specific changes:
  - success also requires ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2Δ_k, a test taken from the probabilistic-model paper [3] (BSV14). It replaces the criticality step, which the paper calls "much less practical and cumbersome" (p. 4);
  - model-improving iterations leave Δ unchanged when the model is not fully linear (p. 4).

  Corollary 2.10 shows that letting η2 = √n, instead of a constant, improves the finite-difference bound from O(n²ε^{-2}) to O(n^{3/2}ε^{-2}) (pp. 7–8). The central design (Alg. 2, pp. 11–12) splits the sample set in two:
  - Y: n points whose geometry is certified with linear Lagrange polynomials;
  - Z: up to n(n−1)/2 points monitored only by distance.

  The quadratic model interpolates Y exactly, fits Z by least squares and keeps ‖H‖ ≤ K (eqs. (4.1)–(4.3), p. 11). Model improvement has four ordered sub-steps: (i) replace a far point of Y; (ii) self-correction, i.e. swap in the trial point where |ℓ_j(s_k)| > Λ_sc; (iii) Powell/IDFO geometry correction by argmax |ℓ_j(s)|; (iv) refresh Z (p. 12). The rejected trial point is reused because "the reason the step is rejected, to begin with, is due to disagreement of the model and the function at the trial step" (p. 10).

  In random subspaces with noise, two repairs are added: a relaxed ratio with +2ε_f, and an explicit floor Δ_min. They are needed because a poor subspace can cause unsuccessful iterations at small Δ, so the radius no longer has an automatic lower bound (Alg. 3, pp. 17–18). The geometry-correcting subspace method restarts Y0, Z0 whenever the subspace is redrawn (Alg. 4, pp. 22–23). The practical GC-YZ-LIN (Alg. 5, p. 26) adds Powell's resolution floor σ_k, a small-step gate and a small-model-gradient gate (p. 24). It also adds a new "hedge" model that switches between minimum-Frobenius-norm and minimum-change models by an exponentially weighted average of relative prediction error (p. 25). GC-YZ-V swaps in NEWUOA-style quadratic Lagrange self-correction and has no theory (p. 25).
  - Transferable technique: certify a minimal core and reuse the rest. Only n points carry the poisedness certificate the proof needs, and every other evaluated point improves the model through a constrained least-squares fit (Alg. 2, pp. 10–12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - ϕ is bounded below and has an L-Lipschitz gradient (Assumptions 1.1–1.2, p. 2).
  - The oracle is deterministic with bounded noise, |f − ϕ| ≤ ε_f (p. 2).
  - The subproblem gives a fraction of Cauchy decrease, and the model Hessian is bounded by κ_bhm (Assumption 2.2, p. 4). κ_bhm should be thought of as O(L) (p. 7), and the algorithm's K is distinct from the true κ_bhm (footnote 3, p. 11).
  - Subspaces must be well aligned with probability θ > 1/2 (Assumption 5.7, p. 20). Uniform subspaces give θ ≥ 243/443 by Lemma 6.7 of [11] (p. 20).
  - The gates need ‖s_k‖ ≥ κ_step min{Δ_k, ‖g_k‖/κ_bhm} (Assumption 6.1, p. 24).

  The theory uses linear Lagrange polynomials only. Quadratic terms enter through the bounded-Hessian least-squares fit (Thms 3.3, 3.5, pp. 8–10). The reachable accuracy is ε ≥ Ω(√(n·ε_f)), i.e. of order √n·√ε_f (Cor. 4.4, p. 16: ε > √(4ε_f/(γ²·min{C₂, L + κ_bhm}·C₁²)) with C₁⁻¹ = Θ(√n); Thm 5.13 with Δ_min = Θ(√ε_f), p. 22). The extracted text "Ω(√nϵf)" hides where the radical ends; the square root covers ε_f.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  1. Trust-region counting (Lemmas 2.3–2.7, Thm 2.8, pp. 5–6): a small Δ with a fully linear model gives success; success gives a decrease of C2Δ² − 2ε_f; the radius is bounded below; |U| ≤ |S| + log. Footnote 2 corrects [11], which had 4ε_f instead of C0 and "The error is inconsequential" (p. 5).
  2. Constants are tracked with explicit n-dependence, which yields the η2 = √n gain (pp. 6–8).
  3. Error bound: a Λ-poised Y gives ‖∇ϕ − g‖ ≤ √(n(Λ²−1)+2)(½(L+κ_bhm)√nΔ + √n·2ε_fΛ/Δ), so κ_eg = O(√n) for Λ = 1 + O(1/n) (Thms 3.3, 3.5, Cor. 3.6, pp. 8–10). The noise term uses the linearity of Lagrange polynomials: ℓ_i(Δy_i/‖y_i‖) = Δ/‖y_i‖ ≤ Λ (p. 10).
  4. Counting geometry steps uses −log|det Y| as a potential (Lemma 4.2, pp. 13–14):
     - Hadamard's inequality gives |det Y| ≥ Λ0^{-n};
     - Cramer's rule gives |det Y⁺| = |det Y|·|ℓ_i(s)|;
     - AM–GM on the singular values gives max_i‖(Y^{-T})_i‖ ≥ |det Y|^{-1/n}.

     Hence log|det Y⁺| ≥ (1−1/n) log|det Y|. A separate induction on subspace dimension (claims 2Λ and 7Λ) shows that Y is 14ⁿ-poised after 2n steps (Lemma 4.3, pp. 14–15). Together: at most 4n log n + 8n + 2n|log log Λ| oracle calls per run of model-improving iterations (Thm 4.1, pp. 13, 15). The result is O(n^{3/2} log n ε^{-2}) evaluations with η2 = √n (Cor. 4.4, p. 16).
  5. Noisy subspaces (pp. 19–21) use indicator processes I_t (well aligned), A_t (successful) and B_t (large radius), with the key inequality A_t ≥ I_t(1−B_t) (p. 20). Increases of ϕ, at most 4ε_f each, are paid for by the large successful iterations (Lemma 5.8). The Cartis–Scheinberg [10] renewal lemmas (5.10–5.11) then give E[T_ε] ≤ 4θ/(2θ−1)²·(…) (Thm 5.12, p. 21). With finite-difference models: E[K_ε] = O(n/ε²·(L+ε_f/Δ_min²)²), optimised at Δ_min = Θ(√ε_f), giving E[C_ε] = O(nq/ε²) (Thm 5.13, pp. 21–22). With geometry correction: O(nq log q/ε²) (Cor. 5.14, p. 22).
  - Transferable technique: when randomisation destroys an automatic lower bound on the radius, impose it as a parameter (Δ_min), then choose it to balance the two terms of the noise floor (pp. 18, 22).
- **D5 Experiments** (§6, pp. 23–30): The test problems are unconstrained CUTEst via S2MPJ [17]: 174 low-dimensional problems (2 ≤ n ≤ 5) and 95, 101 and 92 problems at n = 30, 100 and 200 (p. 27). Solvers compared (pp. 24–25, 27):
  - GC-YZ-LIN, backed by theory;
  - GC-YZ-V, without theory;
  - NEWUOA through PRIMA;
  - DFOTR [2], Bandeira–Scheinberg–Vicente 2012;
  - GC-sub, random subspaces with q = 5.

  Parameters are in Table 6.1 (p. 28): η1 = 0.01, η2 = 5×10⁻⁹, γ_inc = 1.3, γ_dec = 0.8, Λ = 1000, Λ_sc = 2 and K = 10¹⁰⁰. Protocol (p. 27):
  - Y0 = {Δ0u_i} with randomly rotated coordinate vectors, Δ0 = 0.5, and Z0 = {−Δ0u_i};
  - termination tolerances of 10⁻¹² so that the budget ends every run;
  - exact subproblems;
  - data profiles at τ ∈ {10⁻², 10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁶}, with the area under the curve in the legend, averaged over the random rotations.

  The x-axis unit of the profiles is not stated in the extracted text. For fairness, the new hedge model is first shown to improve every solver (Figure 6.2) and is then given to all of them (p. 27). Findings are listed before the plots, including the negative one (pp. 26–27):
  1. the hedge model helps every solver;
  2. geometry-correcting methods beat DFOTR in high dimension;
  3. GC-YZ-LIN roughly matches NEWUOA, and GC-YZ-V beats it;
  4. a competitive practical random-subspace method "remains difficult".

  Two failures are diagnosed: DFOTR's radius collapse (p. 29), and why subspaces underperform: O(q) points per redraw, the worst case actually attained, and the loss of full-space curvature (pp. 29–30). The practical parameters deliberately depart from theory: tiny η2, Λ = 1000 and huge K. The paper explains each departure, since the analysis is worst case (p. 27).
  - Transferable technique: give every competitor your new generic component after showing it helps each of them. Then average profiles over random rotations of the initial set, and let the budget, not a tolerance, stop the run (p. 27).

- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: Contributions:
  - theory: complexity of a Powell-style geometry-correcting method that reuses points and fits quadratic models, O(n^{3/2} log n ε^{-2}) (Cor. 4.4, p. 16), and noisy random-subspace bounds O(nq/ε²) and O(nq log q/ε²) (pp. 22);
  - algorithm and software: GC-YZ-LIN and the hedge model (pp. 24–26);
  - empirical: parity with NEWUOA (p. 29).

  Limitations are specific:
  - the theory covers linear Lagrange polynomials only, and the best performer, GC-YZ-V, is not covered (pp. 24–25, 29);
  - practical random subspaces are not competitive (pp. 27, 29–30);
  - practical parameters sit far from theoretical values (p. 27);
  - restarting subspace sample sets is expensive (p. 22).

  Future work:
  - quadratic Lagrange theory (p. 24);
  - adaptive η2 and Λ (p. 27);
  - theory for patience parameters (p. 25);
  - subspace sets started from two points, and reuse of points across subspaces (p. 30).
