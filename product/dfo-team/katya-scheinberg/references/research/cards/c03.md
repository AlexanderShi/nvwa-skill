### S031 · Optimization methods for supervised machine learning: From linear models to deep learning (Leading developments from INFORMS communities 2017, DOI 10.1287/educ.2017.0168)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: F. E. Curtis · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh ISE period (both authors at Lehigh, p. 1). Dated July 3, 2017 (arXiv v1, 30 Jun 2017, p. 1). STORM was on arXiv and the complexity papers were technical reports at the time (refs [8], [15], [16], pp. 22–23). The last subsection (§3.4, pp. 21–22) is where the tutorial places her group's random-model work, and SARAH [61], her co-authored variance-reduced method, is listed among the VR methods (p. 12). The text does not attribute sections to authors. **Read**: full text. Reference list skimmed. Figure 5 is an image and is absent from the text file.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap between communities, not a technical gap. ML has "a mathematical optimization engine at its core", yet barriers of "terminology, goals, and avenues for collaboration" between statistics, CS and optimization cause duplicated effort (p. 1). The target reader knows optimization but not ML (p. 1). The problem is derived rather than posited, as a chain (pp. 3–7):
  1. maximize the probability of a correct prediction (1);
  2. restrict to a parameterized class (2);
  3. for binary classification, minimize the 01-loss (4);
  4. replace it by the logistic surrogate (7);
  5. replace the unknown distribution P by the empirical risk (8);
  6. add a regularizer (11).

  Each step names what it gives up: the 01-loss is discontinuous and unstable (p. 4), and (7) cannot be evaluated because P is unknown (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: No new algorithm. The organizing move is the same throughout: take the deterministic method, name the ML cost that breaks it, and show the stochastic repair.
  - GD: a line search is known to work well, but each F evaluation is a pass over the data (p. 8). Fixed-step rates O(κ log(1/ε)) and O(1/ε) and the O(nd) gradient cost (pp. 8–9).
  - SGD: O(d) per iteration, Robbins–Monro steps, O(1/ε) and O(1/ε²) rates (pp. 9–10). SGD with momentum (Alg. 1) has no known guarantee, "to the best of our knowledge" (p. 11).
  - Variance reduction exploits the finite-sum structure: SAG/SAGA, SVRG (Alg. 2), SARAH; complexity Tables 2–3 (pp. 11–13).
  - Second order: sampled Hessians, BFGS (Alg. 3), and four stochastic quasi-Newton repairs of the displacement vector y_k: same mini-batch, overlapping batches, damping, and a sampled-Hessian product (pp. 13–15).
  - Deep learning: SGD with back-propagation, then Hessian-free Newton-CG, Gauss–Newton and trust-region CG, then subsampled-Hessian methods (pp. 16–21).
  - The endpoint is the random-model framework. Trust-region, line-search and cubic-regularization methods with random gradient and Hessian estimates keep their rates if the estimates are accurate enough with some positive probability, and |S_k| is tied to the step length or the trust-region radius (p. 21).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: ℓ and r convex in §2 (p. 7). ℓ2-regularized logistic regression is the running example because the regularizer guarantees that a minimizer exists (p. 8). Rates are quoted under µ-strong convexity and L-Lipschitz gradients (p. 8). The SGD-versus-GD argument assumes one can "sample indefinitely" (p. 10). The text then flags that in ML the sample set is fixed, so that argument "should only be used as a theoretical guideline" (p. 11). For stochastic quasi-Newton, SGD's rates are kept if two moment inequalities (constants µ1, µ2) hold for E[B_k⁻¹∇_{S_k}F]. The authors warn that the gradient estimates may be correlated with the Hessian approximations (pp. 15–16).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No proofs. One worked argument, the Bousquet–Bottou trade-off (p. 10):
  1. split E[f(ŵ̂) − f(w*)] into estimation and optimization error (17);
  2. require each to be ≤ ε/2 (18), so n ≥ O(1/ε²);
  3. any method that touches the full sample then costs at least O(1/ε²) per iteration;
  4. SGD reaches ε/2 in O(1/ε²) iterations at a cost independent of n.

  Complexity is summarized per method in Tables 2–3 as iteration bounds in n, κ and ε (p. 13). The generalization bound (10) is stated without proof (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The paper points to a numerical comparison of Hessian-free trust-region subproblem solvers in a Lehigh PhD thesis [88]: 20–30 Hessian-vector products per iteration, and the methods converge to different solutions (p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: Leans on Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal [11] (pp. 3, 9, 10, 12, 19) and Bousquet–Bottou [12] (p. 10). SGD is treated fairly. The paper concludes that "at least in theory, SGD is a superior algorithm for large-scale" ML, then gives the practical caveats (pp. 10–11). Her group's papers [3] and [15] are grouped with Gratton–Royer–Vicente–Zhang [34] as "a very general framework of random models" (p. 21). STORM [16] and Blanchet et al. [8] are presented as the extension to inexact F (pp. 21–22). Reference [16] lists STORM's first author as "C. Chen" (p. 23). The STORM paper itself gives R. Chen (S012 p. 1).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Tutorial voice. A glossary table opens the paper (Table 1, p. 2), and a business toy example (Product A, p. 2) comes before any formula. Algorithms are boxed pseudocode (Algs. 1–3, pp. 11, 12, 14). Hedging is explicit and frequent: "to the best of our knowledge" (p. 11); the practical gains of DNNs remain "somewhat of a mystery" (p. 19); "In truth, we cannot be sure." (p. 19). There are no theorems, only intuition and cited rates. The evaluative thesis comes late. SGD wins in theory (p. 10) but not necessarily in practice (p. 10), and there is "yet to be a practical second-order method that provably achieves such improvements in theory" (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is expository: a bridge from OR to ML. There is no conclusion section; the paper ends inside §3.4 (p. 22). Open questions are stated concretely in the body:
  - stochastic momentum lacks guarantees (p. 11);
  - no practical second-order method has a provable improvement (p. 15);
  - how to make SG-type methods find good local minimizers and avoid saddle points (p. 20);
  - learning bounds like (10) are useless for DNNs because C ≫ n (p. 20);
  - more experiments on negative-curvature directions are needed (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (stated, 2017). The random-model results "simply require that |Sk| has to be chosen sufficiently large with respect to the length of the step taken by the algorithm", with the sample size tied to the trust-region radius in [3] and [34] (p. 21). This states the oracle contract in a tutorial two years before the SIAM News essay. The same passage orders the oracles by the accuracy they need: sampled Hessians need much looser sample sizes than sampled gradients, and F estimates need even stronger ones (p. 22).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (stated). A line search is "well known" to give a well-performing algorithm (p. 8). SGD "is very sensitive to parameter choices" (p. 11). For nonconvex problems "evaluating F is essential for ensuring progress", as in trust-region accept/reject (p. 21). This is an earlier stated instance than the 2020 IEEE SPM article that SKILL.md cites.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (stated). Standard methods with random estimates "retain their convergence rates" (p. 21). Subsampled Newton methods recover rates "similar to their deterministic counterparts" (p. 21). Deterministic parity is the success criterion.
- Research Taste, mark 6 ⚠ variant. Bridging communities is the explicit aim (p. 1), but here the bridge is ML ↔ OR for first- and second-order methods, not DFO ↔ zeroth-order.
- New-pattern candidate: *accuracy hierarchy across oracles*. Function-value estimates need the tightest accuracy, gradients less, Hessians the least (p. 22; also S012 p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1/D7] When writing for another community, open with a glossary table that maps their terms to yours, with an "a.k.a." column (Table 1, p. 2).
- [D1] Derive the optimization problem as a chain of named relaxations from the true goal, and say at each step what is lost (pp. 3–7).
- [D4] Error-budget argument: split the target ε between statistical and optimization error, then compare algorithms by the total cost of meeting both (17)–(18) (p. 10).
- [D3] Show an ingredient is necessary with a one-line degenerate case. On separable data the unregularized logistic loss tends to 0 along an unbounded ray, so no minimizer exists (p. 8).
- [D6] Summarize competitors in a complexity table with one common measure: iterations to ε, in n, κ and ε (Tables 2–3, p. 13).
- [D4] Check any new preconditioned stochastic step against the generic two-inequality condition (µ1, µ2) for keeping SGD's rates, and look for correlation between B_k and the gradient sample (pp. 15–16).

**Quotes**:
- "In practice, however, standard SGD is not necessarily the most effective approach to solve optimization problems in ML." (p. 10)
- "the results simply require that |Sk| has to be chosen sufficiently large with respect to the length of the step taken by the algorithm." (p. 21)

### S009 · SGD and Hogwild! convergence without the bounded gradients assumption (International Conference on Machine Learning 2018, arXiv:1802.03801)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: L. M. Nguyen (lead, corresponding), P. H. Nguyen, M. van Dijk, P. Richtárik, M. Takáč (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The Lehigh ML-optimization line with Nguyen and Takáč, the SARAH group (SARAH is cited on p. 1). Scheinberg is fifth of six authors, and the paper does not say who did what. Unlike S012, the algorithm has neither an adaptive step nor a model. **Read**: main paper (pp. 1–9) in full, and supplement A–C (pp. 10–13) in full. Supplement D (Hogwild! analysis, pp. 13–24) was read through Lemma 8 (p. 18), then skimmed (Lemma 9 onward, D.3, D.4).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an assumption that contradicts the setting.
  - Classical SGD analysis assumes E‖∇f(w;ξ)‖² ≤ σ² for all w, (5) (p. 2).
  - Under strong convexity this gives F(w) ≤ σ²/(2µ) + F(w*) for all w. That contradicts F(w) ≥ µ‖w − w*‖² + F(w*) far from w* (p. 2).
  - A 1-D example (f1 = ½w², f2 = w) shows that E‖∇f(w_t;ξ)‖² can be arbitrarily large along SGD's own iterates (p. 2).
  - The alternative in Bottou et al. 2016, E‖∇f‖² ≤ M‖∇F‖² + N, is consistent. But M and N are unknown, and M sets the learning rate (p. 2).

  The opening observation is that in expected-risk minimization each realization f(w;ξ) is itself convex and L-smooth, which classical analysis ignores (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Nothing in SGD is changed; only the analysis is. Lemma 1: under per-realization L-smoothness and convexity, E‖∇f(w;ξ)‖² ≤ 4L[F(w) − F(w*)] + N, with N = 2E‖∇f(w*;ξ)‖² (p. 4). Without convexity of the realizations, 4L becomes 4Lκ (Lemma 2, p. 4). For Hogwild! the only change is a diminishing step size. A general recursion (10) with random diagonal "filter" matrices S^ξ_u, satisfying d_ξE[S^ξ_u|ξ] = D_ξ, covers both Hogwild! (D = 1, (13)) and single-coordinate updates (D = Δ̄, (12)) (pp. 5–6). Algorithm 2 is the lock-free pseudocode (p. 6). The explicit leading constants rank the variants: D = 1 is better, and SGD gains a further factor 2 (Remark 2, p. 6). A practical remark turns the step size into constants over doubling periods so that the cores can synchronize (Remark 5, p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - F is µ-strongly convex (Assumption 1, p. 2).
  - Each f(·;ξ) is L-smooth (Assumption 2, p. 3).
  - Each f(·;ξ) is convex (Assumption 3, p. 4). This is later dropped at the cost of a factor κ (pp. 4, 7).
  - The ξ_t are i.i.d. (p. 3).
  - Hogwild!: memory is consistent with delay τ, and entry reads and writes are atomic; inconsistent vector reads are allowed (Assumption 4, p. 6).

  The bounded-gradient assumption is dropped entirely (p. 3). The authors present Assumption 2 as the price of the larger step: "We are able to achieve this improvement by introducing Assumption 2, which holds for many ML problems" (Remark 1, p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  1. Lemma 1 (pp. 4, 10). Uses ‖a‖² ≤ 2‖a − b‖² + 2‖b‖² with b = ∇f(w*;ξ), plus a generalization of Johnson–Zhang: E‖∇f(w;ξ) − ∇f(w*;ξ)‖² ≤ 2L[F(w) − F(w*)] (Lemma 3, p. 10).
  2. Theorem 1, almost-sure convergence (pp. 4, 11–12). With η_t ≤ 1/(2L) the F − F* term cancels, leaving E[‖w_{t+1} − w*‖² | F_t] ≤ (1 − µη_t)‖w_t − w*‖² + η_t²N (22). The Robbins–Siegmund supermartingale theorem (Lemma 4, from Bertsekas) plus Ση_t = ∞ then gives ‖w_t − w*‖² → 0 by contradiction (p. 12).
  3. Theorem 2, O(1/t) in expectation (pp. 4, 12–13). Induction on E‖w_t − w*‖² ≤ NG/(µ²(t + E)) with G = max{I, J}; "this trick is based on the idea from (Bottou et al., 2016)" (p. 12). α = 2 is chosen so that the step size depends only on the known µ and L (p. 13). A restart from w_T removes the dependence on ‖w0 − w*‖ (p. 13).
  4. Remark 1 (p. 4). The admissible initial step becomes 1/(2Lκ) instead of 1/(2Lκ²) in Bottou et al., larger by a factor κ = L/µ.
  5. Hogwild!, Theorem 5 (p. 6; proofs pp. 13–24).
     - Inconsistent reads are modelled with 0/1 masks Σ_{t,j}, (28)–(30) (p. 15).
     - Lemma 6 bounds E‖w_t − ŵ_t‖² using a collision probability Δ for the cross terms (pp. 14, 16).
     - Lemma 7 gives a one-step recursion (p. 17), and Lemma 8 unrolls Y_{t+1} ≤ β_tY_t + γ_t (p. 18).
     - The product Π(1 − 2/(j + E)) telescopes (p. 19).
     - A bootstrap induction on a decreasing envelope Z_t closes the loop between w and ŵ (pp. 20–21).
     - The leading term 4α²DN/(µ²t) does not depend on τ, and τ(t) may grow like √(t·L(t)) (pp. 6–7, 22–24).
- **D5 Experiments**: ℓ2-regularized logistic regression with λ = 1/n on ijcnn1 (n = 91,701) and covtype (n = 406,709) from LIBSVM (p. 7). Algorithm 2 is simulated on a single core. This is justified because the claim concerns convergence per iteration or per coordinate update, not wall-clock time (p. 7). Results average 10 runs, with the step size taken from Theorem 5 (p. 8). Two sweeps (p. 8):
  - filter fraction v ∈ {1, 3/4, 2/3, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4} with τ = 10;
  - τ ∈ {1, 10, 100} with v = 1.

  The x-axes are coordinate updates t′ and epochs (p. 8). There are no baselines. The experiments only confirm the O(1/t′) shape and the insensitivity to τ (p. 8).
- **D6 Positioning**: Direct and specific.
  - The bounded-gradient papers (Shalev-Shwartz et al., Nemirovski et al., Recht et al., Hazan–Kale, Rakhlin et al.) rest on an assumption that "is clearly false" under strong convexity (p. 2).
  - Bottou et al. 2016 is corrected: M and N are unknown, and the step bound is improved by L/µ (pp. 2, 4).
  - Moulines–Bach 2011 is credited with studying SGD without bounded gradients (p. 2).
  - Recht et al., Mania et al. and De Sa et al. analyse Hogwild! with bounded gradients and a step that depends on the unknown σ (p. 5). Mania et al.'s claim to remove the assumption "was only mentioned as a remark without proof" (p. 5).
  - Leblond et al. 2018 (fixed step, finite sum, convex components) is the closest work, and the differences are listed point by point (pp. 2–3, 5).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A contribution section with nested bullets (§1.1, p. 3). The key move is announced with "Here we make an important observation" (p. 2). Theorems come with remarks that compare constants with the closest paper (Remark 1, p. 4) and explain surprising features: Remark 3 opens "Surprisingly" and uses a thought experiment, (14)–(15) (pp. 6–7). "Our experiments confirm our analysis" appears twice (pp. 3, 7). One loose claim: to contrast with Leblond et al., the introduction says the diminishing-step analysis shows "convergence to the optimal solution with probability one" (p. 3). The almost-sure theorem is proved only for SGD (Theorem 1, p. 4); the Hogwild! result holds in expectation (Theorem 5, p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: A theory contribution: weaker assumptions, a larger step bound, and the "first convergence results" for Hogwild! with a diminishing learning rate (pp. 1, 3). Limits are not discussed as such. The conclusion only summarizes and gives no future directions (p. 8). Limits visible in the text:
  - F must be strongly convex throughout (p. 2);
  - the step schedule needs µ (p. 4);
  - the delay τ must be bounded (Assumption 4, p. 6);
  - there are no multi-core timing experiments (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The oracle property, a second-moment bound on the stochastic gradient, is derived from per-sample structure instead of assumed (Lemma 1, p. 4). But the contract holds in expectation, not *accurate with probability p* as in Method 1.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. The structure of ML expected risk (each realization smooth and convex) replaces a generic noise assumption (pp. 2, 4).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. The practitioner's method (SGD, Hogwild!) is kept unchanged and only the analysis changes (pp. 3, 5). But the method is non-adaptive, with a pre-specified diminishing step.
- Research Taste, warning sign 1 ⚠ variant. The step schedule is pre-specified but not hand-tuned: it is computed from µ and L (p. 13). The paper sells this as the advantage over the unknown M or σ of earlier analyses (pp. 2, 5).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant. Supermartingale machinery (Robbins–Siegmund) gives almost-sure convergence and an induction gives O(1/t) (pp. 11–13). There is no adaptive step parameter and no stopping-time argument.
- New-pattern candidate: *assumption-contradiction audit*. Prove in three lines that a standard assumption contradicts the setting, add a 1-D counterexample, then replace the assumption with a derived bound (p. 2). Similar small-counterexample moves: S031 p. 8 (separable data) and S004 pp. 7–8 (batch c01).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Before relaxing a standard assumption, prove it contradicts your setting (bounded gradients vs strong convexity, three lines), and add a 1-D counterexample along the algorithm's actual iterates (p. 2).
- [D3/D4] Derive the noise bound from per-sample smoothness: E‖∇f(w;ξ)‖² ≤ 4L[F(w) − F*] + 2E‖∇f(w*;ξ)‖² (pp. 4, 10). The constants are known, so the step size becomes computable (p. 13).
- [D4] Prove an O(1/(t + E)) rate by induction with G = max{initial-gap term, α²/(α − 1)}, then restart from w_T to separate the transient and asymptotic regimes (pp. 12–13).
- [D2] Write the variants as one recursion with a random filter satisfying an unbiasedness identity, read off each variant as an extreme value of one parameter, and keep leading constants explicit so the variants can be ranked (pp. 5–6).
- [D5] Test an asynchrony theorem with a single-core simulation that injects the delay τ explicitly, and plot against both coordinate updates and epochs (pp. 7–8).
- [D7] Pair a counter-intuitive feature of a theorem ("independent of delay τ") with a thought experiment that explains it (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "However, this assumption is clearly false if F is strongly convex." (p. 2)
- "Here we make an important observation: if we view (1) not as a general stochastic problem but as the expected risk minimization problem, where ξ corresponds to a random data sample pulled from a distribution, then (1) has an additional key property" (p. 2)

### S012 · Stochastic optimization using a trust-region method and random models (Mathematical Programming 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10107-017-1141-8; arXiv:1504.04231)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: R. Chen, M. Menickelly (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The STORM paper. It extends BSV 2014 (ref [2], exact function values) to random function estimates (p. 8). It is the almost-sure stage before the complexity analysis of Blanchet et al.; rates are explicitly deferred (p. 25). Chen and Menickelly were at Lehigh (Chen's thesis is ref [6], p. 35; Menickelly's Lehigh affiliation, p. 1). **Read**: full text of arXiv v2 (23 Sep 2016, p. 1), including the appendix algorithms (pp. 37–40). The published Math. Program. version was not read. The figures are images, but their axis labels survive in the text (pp. 29, 31–34).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap between two fields. DFO methods were developed for deterministic functions, although noise is where DFO matters most; stochastic optimization mostly relies on stochastic derivatives (p. 1). Both standard classes are criticized for concrete reasons (p. 2):
  - SG methods are slow and "strongly dependent on the choice of algorithmic parameters";
  - SA methods need tuned sampling, cannot handle biased noise, and "suffer significantly in the presence of outliers".

  Two applied examples motivate biased noise (pp. 3–4, 25–26):
  - a randomized numerical process, e.g. hyperparameter tuning where training may fail;
  - black-box equation solvers whose failure probability rises as accuracy is tightened.

  The problem is min f(x), with f smooth and bounded below, where only f̃(x) = f(x, ε) can be computed (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The stated goal is that "a standard efficient unconstrained optimization method, such as a trust region method, can be applied, with very small modifications" (p. 2). Algorithm 1 (p. 7):
  - **Kept**: a quadratic model on B(x_k, δ_k), fraction of Cauchy decrease (Assumption 2.1), the ratio test ρ_k ≥ η1, and radius update by ×γ or ÷γ (p. 7). The parameters are "chosen almost identically to the standard practices" of trust-region methods (p. 5).
  - **Changed**: ρ_k is computed from fresh estimates f0_k and fs_k of f(x_k) and f(x_k + s_k), not from f (step 4, p. 7). Acceptance also requires ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2δ_k (step 5, p. 7).
  - **Consequence**: a successful iteration can be "false", meaning f may increase. The analysis must show that "true" successes happen often enough (p. 7).
  - The sampling rate can go down as well as up with δ_k, increasing "only when necessary" (p. 5). First- and second-order models fit without changing the algorithm (p. 5).

  Section 5 shows how to meet the contract:
  - unbiased noise: Chebyshev sample averages at n+1 well-poised points (p. 24), or averaged stochastic gradients (p. 25);
  - computation failures: plain interpolation with no averaging, because averaging optimizes the wrong function (pp. 26, 30).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - **Oracle contract** (pp. 8–9). Models are α-probabilistically κ-fully linear: ‖∇f − ∇m‖ ≤ κ_eg δ_k and |f − m| ≤ κ_ef δ_k², conditioned on the past σ-algebra (Def. 3.4). Estimates are β-probabilistically ε_F-accurate: |f0_k − f(x_k)| ≤ ε_F δ_k² (Defs. 3.3, 3.5), conditioned on a σ-algebra that already includes the current model. The estimate accuracy is "somewhat tighter" than the model accuracy (p. 9).
  - Probabilities are constant, not increasing to 1, and failures can be arbitrarily bad (p. 3).
  - f is bounded below, and f and ∇f are L-Lipschitz on an enlarged region containing all iterates (Assumption 4.1, p. 10). Remark 4.2 admits that this depends on the algorithm's behaviour, because iterates may leave the level set, and leaves weaker versions "for future work" (p. 10).
  - ‖H_k‖ ≤ κ_bhm (Assumption 4.3, p. 10).
  - The restriction ‖g_k‖ ≥ η2δ_k is used in the proof (p. 6). η2 ≥ Θ(L) may force small steps, and the experiments do not use η2 (p. 21).
  - Hidden constraints (f fails deterministically at some x) are excluded (Remark 5.1, pp. 26–27).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  1. **Deterministic lemmas under good events** (pp. 11–13).
     - A fully linear model and a small δ_k give decrease ∝ ‖g_k‖δ_k (Lemma 4.5) and ∝ ‖∇f‖δ_k (Lemma 4.6).
     - A good model, good estimates and a small δ_k give a successful iteration (Lemma 4.7), via a five-term decomposition of ρ_k − 1.
     - Good estimates alone make any accepted step decrease f by C2δ_k² (Lemma 4.8).
  2. **Theorem 4.11, ΣΔ_k² < ∞ almost surely** (pp. 14–19).
     - Potential Φ_k = νf(X_k) + (1 − ν)Δ_k², with E[Φ_{k+1} − Φ_k | F] ≤ −σΔ_k².
     - Split on ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≥ ζδ_k versus < ζδ_k, then on the four outcomes of (I_k, J_k).
     - Choose ν close to 1 so that every outcome except "both bad" is no worse than an unsuccessful step (b1 in (28)).
     - Bound the "both bad" increase by Taylor plus Lipschitz (C3 = 1 + 3L/(2ζ)), then make its probability (1 − α)(1 − β) small (pp. 16, 19).
  3. **Probability conditions** (Corollary 4.12, p. 20): (αβ − ½)/((1 − α)(1 − β)) ≥ C3/C1, plus an upper bound on (1 − α)(1 − β). Remark 4.13 plugs in textbook values (κ_ef = κ_eg = κ_bhm = 10L, κ_fcd = ½, γ = 2, η1 = ½). This gives η2 ≥ 32L, ζ ≥ 42L, C1 = 2/17 and (1 − α)(1 − β) ≤ 1/440 (pp. 20–21).
  4. **Theorem 4.16, liminf** (pp. 21–22). Needs αβ ≥ ½.
     - R_k = log_γ(δ_k/b) is a submartingale.
     - It is bounded below by W_k = Σ(2·1_{I_k}1_{J_k} − 1), a ±1 walk whose lim sup is ∞ by the submartingale dichotomy (Theorem 4.4, from Durrett, p. 10).
     - So the radius cannot stay below b, which contradicts ‖∇f‖ ≥ ε′ for all k.
  5. **Lemma 4.17 and Theorem 4.18, lim-type** (p. 23). Σ_{k: ‖∇f‖>ε} Δ_k < ∞ almost surely, from bound (34). The rest follows BSV [2] and is not reproduced (p. 23).
  6. **Construction side** (p. 24). Chebyshev gives p ≥ V/((κ′_ef)²(1 − α′)δ⁴) samples per point. Independence over the n+1 points gives α = (α′)^{n+1}.
- **D5 Experiments** (Matlab on a laptop, p. 27):
  - **Unbiased noise**: 53 CUTEr sum-of-squares problems with uniform noise, either multiplicative (1 + ω_i)f_i or additive f_i + ω_i, with σ ∈ {0.1, 10⁻³} (pp. 27–29).
    - Baselines are the authors' own TR-SAA, a simplified sample-average trust region "similar to" Deng–Ferris, and TR-SAA-resample. The resample variant was built to remove one violation of STORM's assumptions, the reuse of samples across iterations; it still correlates f0_k with the model (p. 28).
    - Performance profiles count function evaluations until the reduction test (52) holds with τ = 10⁻³. Runs are averaged over 10 repetitions, with a budget of 1000(n+1) and identical parameters for all methods (pp. 28–29).
    - The theoretical 1/δ_k⁴ sample rate is ignored. A rate of about 1/δ_k "was chosen after testing various other rates" (pp. 28, 30).
  - **Computation failures** (p. 30): a garbage value V = −10000 is returned with probability σ when |f_i(x)| < ε, with σ = 1 − p_s^{1/m}. 30 runs, budget 10000(n+1). There is no comparison, because no other method targets this noise.
  - **Theory-versus-practice sweep** on f = Σ(x_i − 1)² (pp. 30–32). The theory needs (1 − σ) > 0.9592 for n = 2 and > 0.9990 for n = 10. Empirically, at (1 − σ) = 0.998 and n = 10, 100% of runs succeed even though α ≈ 0.27.
  - **ML** (pp. 31–35): logistic regression on a9a, covtype-scale, mnist and shuttle against Adagrad, with one pass over the data as budget. Training and test loss are plotted against log10 samples observed, for STORM with and without sampled Hessians.
  - A comparison with SPSA and Kiefer–Wolfowitz is deferred to Chen's thesis (p. 27).
  - Reading note: appendix Algorithm 4, used for the failure experiments (p. 30), is titled "STORM for unbiased noise" (p. 39).
- **D6 Positioning**: Three related methods are compared by their assumptions, not by performance (pp. 4–5):
  - Deng–Ferris: the probability of accuracy must go to 1, with summable failures;
  - SPSA: decaying step and finite-difference parameters that need tuning;
  - SCSR: increasing probabilities and a unique minimizer.

  STORM ties its accuracy only to the radius, while Deng–Ferris also tie it to the true model gradient, which is "harder to ensure" (p. 5). Billups–Larson [3] is acknowledged as "a very similar method" (pp. 5–6):
  - the assumption differences are listed (unbiased noise; probabilities tending to 1);
  - their advantage is conceded (no restriction on the step);
  - the empirical comparison is skipped because "we view them as more or less the same method".

  The estimate definition is credited as a modification of Larson–Billups [17] (p. 9), and the Φ function as similar to one used in [17] with a different analysis (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The novelty sentence is about assumptions: "The main novelty of this work is the analysis of the framework and the resulting weaker, more general, conditions compared to prior work" (p. 3). Two bullets then say what is *not* assumed (p. 3). The proof of Theorem 4.11 is preceded by a plain-language outline of its key ideas (p. 15). Remarks say which parameter choices are proof artefacts (the case 2(b) remark, pp. 18–19; Remark 4.14, p. 21). The introduction hedges honestly on tightness ("far from tight", p. 3), and the experiments then test it (pp. 30–31). There is no conclusion section; the paper ends after the ML experiments (p. 35).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Theory (almost-sure first-order convergence under a fixed-probability contract), plus a catalogue of noise models and experiments (p. 1). The limits are stated specifically:
  - no convergence rates, "a subject for future research" (p. 25);
  - the required probabilities are hard to know in practice (p. 3) and far from tight (pp. 3, 31);
  - η2 forces small steps; step size and accuracy control could be decoupled, at the cost of a "much more complex" analysis (pp. 18–19, 21);
  - the Lipschitz assumption holds on an algorithm-dependent region (p. 10);
  - hidden constraints are out of scope (p. 27).

The experiments work far below this (α ≈ 0.27, p. 31).

- Method 3 ✅ evidence for the stochastic-process half: the expected decrease of Φ_k and the log-radius submartingale (pp. 14–22). ⚠ variant for the complexity half: only almost-sure convergence is proved, and rates are explicitly deferred (p. 25).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. Fully linear models come from well-poised sets (p. 24). Algorithm 4 changes the interpolation set minimally but recomputes all values afresh, so that a garbage value cannot persist (p. 30).

The paper says "It is assumed in [2] that the function values at the current iterate and the trial point can be computed exactly" (p. 8) and "Some of the assumptions in this paper were inspired by an early version of [3]" (p. 5). **Transferable techniques**:
- [D3] Write the oracle contract as two events (model good, estimates good) with fixed conditional probabilities and no assumption about failures. Condition the estimate event on a σ-algebra that already includes the current model (pp. 8–9).
- [D2] Draw fresh estimates of f at both the current and trial points every iteration, independent of the model data, so that a lucky low value cannot lock in (p. 7; independence discussion p. 28).
- [D4] Use the potential Φ_k = νf(X_k) + (1 − ν)Δ_k² with a 2×4 case split. Choose ν so that every case but the worst does no worse than an unsuccessful step, then make the worst case rare (pp. 14–19).
- [D4] For liminf, show that log_γ(δ_k/b) is a submartingale bounded below by a ±1 walk that counts good iterations (pp. 21–22).
- [D4] Plug textbook parameter values into your probability conditions so readers see how large the constants are (Remark 4.13, pp. 20–21).
- [D5] Theory-threshold sweep: plot empirical success against the probability of success, with the theoretical threshold drawn as a line (pp. 30–32).
- [D5] When a baseline violates your assumptions, build a variant that removes one violation (TR-SAA-resample) to isolate its effect (p. 28).
- [D6] If a concurrent paper has a near-identical method, say so, list the assumption differences, concede its advantage, and explain why you do not compare (pp. 5–6).
