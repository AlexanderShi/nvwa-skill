### S017 · An efficient primal-dual interior-point method for minimizing a sum of Euclidean norms (SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing 2000, DOI 10.1137/s1064827598343954)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K. D. Andersen (Lindo Systems; the work was done at Odense), E. Christiansen (Odense), M. L. Overton (Courant) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Conn is at IBM Watson with DARPA support (p. 1). The paper returns to a problem Conn first worked on with Calamai (Calamai–Conn 1980, 1987: active-set Newton methods for location problems; p. 2, p. 19). It is a continuous, nonsmooth, derivative-based problem, not DFO. **Read**: full text, including all tables and the reference list.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper enters through the problem's history and a failure shared by the existing algorithms.
  - History: the problem goes back to Fermat and may be "the earliest example of duality" (abstract p. 1; Kuhn, p. 2). It arises in many modern applications (p. 1, pp. 3–4).
  - The failure (p. 2): Weiszfeld's iteratively reweighted least squares and the smoothing ‖z_i‖ → (‖z_i‖² + µ²)^{1/2} are robust but slow. Neither uses duality, and both slow down because in most interesting applications some norms are zero at the solution.
  - The active-set Newton methods of Calamai–Conn and Overton were the first to use duality, but they need many iterations when many norms are zero, because the active set changes at every step (p. 2).
  - Andersen's primal method [And96b] was the first to solve large instances with many zero norms quickly (p. 3).

  Problem statement: derive a primal-dual interior-point method for the pair D (min Σ‖z_i‖ subject to A_iᵀy + z_i = c_i) and P (max Σ c_iᵀx_i subject to ‖x_i‖ ≤ 1, Σ A_i x_i = 0), then make it work at large sparse scale (pp. 1–3). The unifying observation across all the applications is that "some, and perhaps many" norms are zero at the optimum (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: organise the related-work section around one shared difficulty (here, zero norms at the solution) and classify every earlier method by how it copes with it (pp. 2–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Apply Newton's method to three conditions: primal feasibility, dual feasibility and a perturbed complementarity condition (p. 3, p. 5).
  - Complementarity. The duality gap is Σ(‖z_i‖ − x_iᵀz_i) ≥ 0. It vanishes iff z_i − ‖z_i‖x_i = 0 for each i (4) (p. 4). This is not differentiable at z_i = 0, so it is replaced by the centering condition z_i − (‖z_i‖² + µ²)^{1/2} x_i = 0 (7) (p. 5). Theorem 1 (from [And96b]) shows that (7) is exactly the optimality condition of a smoothed dual D_µ paired with a penalized primal P_µ. The result is "a sort of central path", although not one derived from a logarithmic barrier (p. 5).
  - Linear algebra. Eliminating variables leaves the Schur complement system A H_µ Aᵀ Δy = A E_µ⁻¹ z (15) (p. 6). Unlike in LP, H_µ = E_µ⁻¹F_µ is not symmetric, and E_µ, F_µ depend explicitly on µ (p. 6–7).
  - Symmetrization. F_µ is positive definite on the primal feasible set, and it is symmetric on the central path. So A H_µ Aᵀ is nearly symmetric near the path (p. 7). This justifies replacing H_µ by ½E_µ⁻¹(F_µ + F_µᵀ), so that sparse Cholesky can be used, and compensating with a skew correction term h⁽³⁾ on the right-hand side (30) (p. 7, p. 10).
  - Step rules (pp. 7–8). The dual step Δz is a descent direction for the smoothed dual, so y and z are updated by a line search on it (18)–(19). The primal step Δx is not an ascent direction for P_µ, so a line search is inappropriate there. Two alternatives are given: a new *primal scaling rule* x̃ = γ̄(x + Δx), possible only because the primal equality constraints are homogeneous and without analogue in standard IPMs (p. 8); and a fraction-to-boundary steplength rule. Taking τ = 1 "works quite well" but is excluded because rounding could leave x just outside the feasible set (p. 8).
  - Mehrotra predictor-corrector, transplanted from LP (pp. 8–10). A second-order expansion of the centering condition gives the corrector right-hand side (24). The centering estimate is µ̃ = gap(x+Δx, z+Δz)³ / (n·gap(x,z)²) (25), described as "a natural generalization of Mehrotra’s formula for LP" (p. 9). If the corrector is not a descent direction for the smoothed dual, both corrector steps are discarded and the predictor steps are used (p. 10).
  - The full algorithm has 10 steps (p. 11). It stops when the gap and the residuals are below ε, or on breakdown signals: factorization failure, line-search failure, or ‖Ax‖ > gap(x,z), which "cannot happen in exact arithmetic" (p. 11).
  - Kept from LP practice: the three-block Newton system, Mehrotra's predictor-corrector and modified Cholesky. Changed: the complementarity condition, the nonsymmetric Schur complement with a skew correction, the primal scaling rule, and (in the large-scale code) one centering parameter per norm (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: when the Newton matrix is only nearly symmetric (symmetric on the central path), symmetrize it to get Cholesky and move the skew part to the right-hand side as a correction term (p. 7, p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: guard a heuristic step (the corrector) with a cheap descent test, and fall back to the step that has a descent guarantee (the predictor) (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: add stopping tests for events that are impossible in exact arithmetic, such as primal infeasibility growing above the duality gap, to detect when rounding dominates (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - A (m × dn) has full rank and m < dn, since otherwise x = 0, z = 0 solves both problems (p. 5).
  - d is usually 2 or 3. For d = 1 the problem is an LP (p. 1).
  - The problem is convex but nondifferentiable wherever some z_i = 0 (p. 1).
  - Strict complementarity may fail, unlike in LP (p. 5). The experiments separate the strictly complementary (SC) and non-SC cases (p. 13).
  - rd = 0 is maintained exactly. rp is not, "since primal feasibility cannot be maintained exactly in the presence of rounding errors" (p. 6).
  - No convergence or complexity assumptions are stated, because no convergence theorem is proved (see D4).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There is no convergence or complexity analysis of the new method. The analytic content is:
  - The duality derivation is a four-step chain: Cauchy–Schwarz, min-max exchange [Roc70, Cor. 37.3.2], a trivial step, and an unboundedness argument (p. 2).
  - Theorem 1 is imported from [And96b], and its proof is "a simple modification" (p. 5).
  - Descent of Δz for the smoothed dual: (Δz)ᵀE_µ⁻¹z = −(Δy)ᵀ A H_µ Aᵀ Δy < 0, because the symmetric part of A H_µ Aᵀ is positive definite (p. 7).
  - Structural facts about F_µ: it is positive definite on the feasible set and symmetric on the central path. In the limit µ → 0, either a block of E_µ goes to zero or a block of F_µ becomes singular (p. 7).
  - The complexity theory for the problem class is delegated to Xue–Ye [XY97], who use the Nesterov–Todd self-scaled cone theory (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: prove only the local facts each design choice needs (descent of the dual step, positive definiteness, near-symmetry on the path), and state plainly where the global theory lives (p. 3, p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two tiers.
  - **Tier 1 (§4, pp. 11–14)**: a Matlab implementation on small, topologically constrained Steiner-tree problems [DO98] with random data. Sparsity is not exploited, d = 2, the machine is a Sparc Ultra, and the tolerance is 10⁻¹⁰. The tables report *medians* of iterations, final gap and infeasibility over many random instances per class (p. 11).
    - Design: five variants (nonsymmetric; symmetrized; with skew correction; with modified Cholesky; with both) crossed with two primal rules (Tables 1–2, p. 12). Omitting the h⁽²⁾ correction "had essentially no effect in any case" (p. 12).
    - Stratification: a second class, the Chung–Graham ladder (n = 85, m = 84), is split into the 20 SC instances and the 180 non-SC instances out of 200 generated (Tables 3–6, pp. 13–14).
    - Findings: robust convergence in every case tested, rapid local convergence, and high accuracy despite ill-conditioning (p. 12). The nonsymmetric version is the most accurate. Symmetrization without modified Cholesky loses accuracy, and the skew correction "substantially improves" the symmetrized version (p. 12). Non-SC instances reach the same accuracy but with more iterations and a slower rate, with an explanation of why (p. 13).
    - The authors recommend the symmetrized version with skew correction and modified Cholesky, because it allows Cholesky instead of LU (p. 13).
  - **Tier 2 (§5, pp. 13–18)**: large sparse plastic-collapse problems. For comparability they are run on the same Convex 3240 vector machine and use the same discrete problems as the earlier primal barrier study [And96b] (p. 14). The large-scale code differs from the §3 algorithm in two disclosed ways: a different Mehrotra generalization with no skew term, and individual centering parameters µ̃_i (p. 14). A footnote adds that µ = 0 was used in the predictor right-hand side in these runs (p. 10).
    - sspN, smooth problems with no zero norms (up to n = 160,801 and 31 million nonzeros in L): no CPU gain over the primal barrier method, only a small iteration reduction (Table 7, p. 15).
    - lNa13, 25–87% zero norms: at N = 120, 34 vs 176 iterations and 44,144 vs 284,378 s. The method also finds more zero norms, which the authors believe is physically correct (Tables 8–9, p. 16).
    - lNa20, 62–96% zero norms: CPU reduced "by a factor 4 or more", and larger instances become solvable (Table 10, p. 17).
    - clN13, with extra linear equality constraints handled by an ℓ1 penalty [AC98]: 11–35 iterations vs 30–201 for the barrier method. At N = 201, CPU falls from 36,371 s to 6,179 s, and to 4,293 s with a dense-column technique (Table 11, pp. 17–18).
  - Cost unit: iterations and CPU seconds (p. 15). Accuracy: relative gap and infeasibility (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: when you compare against an earlier method, rerun on the *same machine and the same instances* as the earlier paper, and say so (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: split a random test class by the structural property your theory says matters (strict complementarity), and report the two strata separately (pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable technique: report the tier where your method gives no gain (smooth sspN) next to the tiers where it wins, and explain why (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself between general conic theory and specialized practice. It acknowledges that sum of norms is a special case of QCQP, covered by Nesterov–Todd and Adler–Alizadeh, but argues that the problem is "sufficiently important that a specialized approach is justified", a view it shares with Xue–Ye (p. 3).
  - Earlier methods are classified by whether and how they use duality (p. 2).
  - Conn's own Calamai–Conn methods are described as the first to exploit duality but inefficient when many norms are zero (p. 2), so the new method supersedes them.
  - The main baseline is coauthor Andersen's primal method. The new method's advantage is framed as matching general LP experience that primal-dual methods beat primal interior-point methods [Wri97] (p. 3).
  - Applications are credited to others: Alpert et al. applied "a variant of our algorithm" to VLSI placement; Chan–Golub–Mulet to image reconstruction; JPL used Weiszfeld's method for spacecraft fly-bys (pp. 3–4).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Structure: a historical hook (Fermat, "earliest example of duality", p. 1), a prior-method taxonomy (pp. 2–3), a roadmap (p. 3), and a list of applications ending with "We believe there is great opportunity" (p. 4).
  - The abstract states a concrete result: accurate solutions in fewer than 50 iterations and "physically meaningful solutions previously unobtainable" (p. 1).
  - Hedging is attached to observations: "apparently because" (p. 12), "presumably because such a solution does not exist" (p. 13), "From our physical understanding of the solution we believe this is correct" (p. 16).
  - The physics is used as an interpretation: complementarity is the material's "flow rule". z_i is strain and x_i is stress, so any deformation forces the stress onto its bound (pp. 16–17).
  - The conclusion is two sentences and restricts the claim to nonsmooth problems (p. 18).
  - Transferable technique: when the application has a physical meaning, map each optimality condition to it (here complementarity = flow rule). Then use the physics as an extra accuracy check (the number of zero norms) (pp. 16–17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contributions: an algorithm with design justifications, a small-scale study of the options, and a large-scale sparse code applied to plastic collapse (pp. 1, 3, 13–18).
  - Limits stated by the authors: no gain on smooth instances (p. 15); slower convergence without strict complementarity (p. 13); the scaling rule fails if nonhomogeneous linear constraints are added (p. 12); symmetrization without modified Cholesky loses accuracy (p. 12).
  - Future directions: other applications (p. 4).
  - Not stated by the authors: there is no convergence proof for either the §3 algorithm or the large-scale variant, and the large-scale code tested in §5 is not the algorithm presented in §3 (differences disclosed on p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (outside DFO): the paper exploits structure before reaching for a generic method. It uses the duality and complementarity pair instead of generic smoothing (pp. 2–5). It rejects a general QCQP treatment in favour of a specialized one (p. 3). It exploits the homogeneity of the primal constraints to create the scaling rule (p. 8), and sparsity through symmetrization so that Cholesky can be used (p. 7, p. 13).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: proven LP machinery is transplanted to a new cone. Mehrotra's predictor-corrector and its µ formula are generalized (pp. 8–9), and modified Cholesky is imported as "standard practice in LP" (p. 12). The new device (the skew correction) exists only to make the transplant work (p. 10).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: a two-tier test. Tier 1 is random small instances with per-option tables and medians (pp. 11–14); tier 2 is large application instances on the baseline's own machine (p. 14). Every option setting is reported (Tables 1–6), including the no-gain tier (p. 15).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: code and numbers without the theory leg. The only theorem is imported from [And96b] (p. 5), complexity is delegated to [XY97] (p. 3), and the large-scale code differs from the presented algorithm (p. 14). This is the opposite of Workflow B's checkpoint, although here there is no theorem to mismatch.
- Method 4 ✅ weak evidence: the application partner (Christiansen, plastic collapse) is a coauthor, and the physical meaning of complementarity guides the interpretation (pp. 16–17). Conn's personal role in the application cannot be identified from the text.
- Heuristic 8 ✅ evidence (swap in a better solver, even if the old one is yours): the new method explicitly replaces the Calamai–Conn active-set approach when many norms are zero (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: *supersede your own earlier method openly*. Name your earlier method, say where it fails, and replace it (p. 2). This batch has a second instance in S016 (p. 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Classify the prior methods by how they handle the one feature that makes your problem hard (zero norms at the solution) (pp. 2–4).
- [D2] Replace a nondifferentiable complementarity condition by a smoothed centering condition, and prove it is the exact optimality condition of a smoothed primal-dual pair (Theorem 1, p. 5).
- [D2] Symmetrize a nearly symmetric Newton matrix to use Cholesky, and move the skew part to the right-hand side (pp. 7, 10).
- [D2] Guard a corrector step with a descent test and fall back to the predictor (p. 10).
- [D2] Add a stopping test for an event that cannot occur in exact arithmetic (p. 11).
- [D5] Compare on the earlier study's machine and instances (p. 14), and split test classes by the structural property the theory says matters (p. 13).

### S003 · An algorithmic framework for convex mixed integer nonlinear programs (Discrete Optimization 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.disopt.2006.10.011)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: P. Bonami, L. T. Biegler, G. Cornuéjols, I. E. Grossmann, C. D. Laird, J. Lee, A. Lodi, F. Margot, N. Sawaya, A. Wächter (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the first paper of a 2004 IBM–CMU project on MINLP that builds on COIN-OR components. The code named here is what the authors expected to release on COIN-OR in 2006 (p. 1–2). Conn is one of eleven authors, and his individual contribution cannot be identified from the text. **Read**: the whole preprint of 31 October 2005 (p. 1), including the references and Tables 1–5 (pp. 13, 19–22). This version has no conclusion section, and the published version was not seen.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is strategic, not driven by an anomaly (abstract p. 1; p. 1):
  - MINLP is "an important and difficult area" that needs new methods and software for large problems.
  - Its two building blocks, MILP and NLP, have both made steady progress.
  - COIN-OR now supplies reusable components (Cbc for MILP, Ipopt for NLP).

  The problem is the convex MINLP P: min f(x, y) subject to g(x, y) ≤ 0, x ∈ X ∩ ℤⁿ, y ∈ Y, with X and Y polyhedral, X bounded and f, g twice continuously differentiable (p. 2). The method is exact for convex relaxations and usable as a heuristic for nonconvex MINLPs (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when two neighbouring fields have both matured and share an open-source platform, choose the problem that needs both of them at once, and build the team from both fields (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper defines one family of algorithms with the two classical methods at its extremes (abstract p. 1; p. 3).
  - Lower bounds come from outer approximations (OA) and from NLP relaxations of subproblems. Upper bounds come from fixing the integers and solving the NLP P_x̄, all inside one branch-and-cut (p. 3).
  - Using only NLP relaxations gives classical NLP branch-and-bound (BB). Alternating OA and P_x̄ at the root gives classical OA (p. 3).
  - Three implementations are built from COIN-OR parts: I-BB, I-OA and I-Hyb, using Cbc, Cgl, Clp and Ipopt 3.0 (p. 8).
  - **I-Hyb** (§3.3, pp. 9–10; Figure 3, p. 11) extends the Quesada–Grossmann branch-and-cut (Figure 2, p. 7) with three changes:
    1. It solves the NLP relaxation every L = 10 nodes. With L = 1 it reduces to NLP BB (p. 9).
    2. It runs a local OA enumeration at the root with a time limit κ = 30 s. With κ = ∞ it reduces to OA (pp. 9–10).
    3. It reuses Cbc's MILP technology: Gomory, probing, MIR and cover cuts; reliability branching with parameter 8 on up to 20 variables with at most 100 simplex iterations; and an NLP check of any OA-feasible point found during strong branching (p. 10).
  - Implementation choices, each with a stated reason: Ipopt, an interior-point solver, was used because no suitable open-source active-set NLP solver was available (p. 8). Ipopt is warm-started from the parent node's primal-dual solution, with a weaker push away from the bounds on warm starts (p. 8). A heuristic switches to the restoration phase sooner, to detect infeasibility (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: parametrize your algorithm so that its extreme settings recover the two rival classical methods (L = 1 gives BB, κ = ∞ gives OA). Then one code can benchmark the whole spectrum (pp. 3, 9–10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f and g are convex and C², X and Y are polyhedral, and X is bounded (p. 2).
  - For the OA equivalence, a constraint qualification must hold at every optimum of P_x̄ and of the feasibility problem P^F_x̄ (Theorem 1, p. 4).
  - The linearizations (1)–(2) are valid at *any* point, feasible or not, because of convexity (p. 3).
  - Finite convergence of OA and of Quesada–Grossmann is cited from [10] and [24] under the same assumptions (pp. 5–6).
  - Nonconvex use is heuristic only (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The theory is thin and partly corrective.
  - Theorem 1 (p. 4): P and P^OA(T̂) have the same optimal *value*. No proof is given in the text.
  - The paper then points out that Theorem 1 of Fletcher–Leyffer [10] is stated incorrectly: optimal solutions of the OA relaxation need not be optimal for P. The counterexample (Example 1, pp. 4–5) is a ball in ℝ³ with one integer variable. The linearized MILP leaves y unconstrained, so (1, λ, −√3/2) is MILP-optimal for every λ but infeasible for the MINLP whenever λ ≠ 0 (p. 5).
  - The convergence of the three algorithms is not re-proved (pp. 5–6).
  - Transferable technique: when you restate a known theorem, test the converse direction, and if it fails, give a minimal counterexample (here, a free continuous variable that the linearizations cannot see) (pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Test set** (pp. 10–12): 41 convex instances chosen from a library of more than 150 convex problems [27]. They are published on the web in AMPL and GAMS formats, except the Water problems, which exist only in AMPL (p. 12).
    - Each class is described by its application, the source of its nonlinearity and its integrality gap, e.g. BatchS about 15%, CLay 100% by deliberately poor modelling, big-M Syn above 600% (pp. 10–12).
    - Selection rule: instances solved in under 3 hours by an *earlier implementation of the hybrid*, excluding those solved in under 30 s, plus the smallest unsolved instance of each class (p. 12).
  - **Baselines**: the commercial counterpart of each parent method. SBB (NLP BB with CONOPT) and Dicopt (OA with CPLEX 9.0 and CONOPT), with versions stated (p. 14).
  - **Setup**: one IBM IntelliStation (Xeon 3.2 GHz, 2 GB, Fedora Core 3) and a 3-hour limit (p. 14). SBB's reported CPU times exclude file I/O; on Syn40M02M the real time exceeds 31 hours (p. 14).
  - **Summary**: a Dolan–Moré performance profile over time (Figure 4, pp. 14–15), without the Water problems, which cannot run in GAMS (p. 14).
    - I-Hyb dominates I-BB and I-OA, and dominates SBB except at very small ratios (p. 14).
    - Dicopt solves many problems fastest. I-Hyb becomes competitive only at p = 28. The authors re-read the same data with *time differences* instead of ratios: then I-Hyb is best once 45 s beyond the best time are allowed (p. 14).
  - **Failures**: shown as *** and attributed to Ipopt numerics, the LP solver or CONOPT (p. 14; Tables 2–3, pp. 19–20).
  - **Diagnostics** (pp. 14–16):
    - Per-class winners, and a conjecture that the quality of the OA linear relaxation decides whether OA or BB is better (p. 14).
    - Tree sizes and NLP counts (Table 3, p. 20).
    - Time per node: 0.27 s for SBB vs 0.21 s for I-BB, so the warm-start advantage of an active-set solver "does not confer any advantage" (p. 15).
    - Time per MILP: 81.9 s for I-OA vs 2.1 s for Dicopt. Two explanations are offered: Cbc is relatively weak on easy MILPs, and interior-point NLP solutions sit at the centre of optimal faces, which changes the OA cuts. A controlled swap tests the first: I-OA with CPLEX as LP solver on Syn40M03M takes 267 s instead of 1,815 s, still far from Dicopt's 1.2 s (pp. 15–16).
    - Iteration counts correlate with the gap closed by the first MILP (Table 5, p. 22), and CLay's symmetry is offered as the explanation for Dicopt's many iterations (p. 16).
  - Caveat not discussed by the authors: selecting instances with an earlier version of one of the compared methods may favour that method (p. 12).
  - Transferable technique: explain a timing gap by decomposing it into per-unit costs (time per node, time per MILP), then test the leading explanation with a controlled component swap (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable technique: when a ratio-based performance profile looks bad because of instances solved in seconds, also report a difference-based reading and say why it matters (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - It builds on OA (Duran–Grossmann [9]; Fletcher–Leyffer [10]), NLP BB (Gupta–Ravindran [14]; Leyffer [19]), generalized Benders (Geoffrion [11]) and extended cutting planes (Westerlund–Pettersson [36]) (p. 2).
  - The hybrid extends Quesada–Grossmann [24] (pp. 5–6). Grossmann is a coauthor.
  - Fletcher–Leyffer's theorem is corrected with a counterexample (p. 4).
  - The paper claims to combine BB-type and OA-type enhancements "apparently for the first time" (p. 9).
  - Commercial solvers are the baselines, and the comparison with them is diagnosed candidly (pp. 14–16).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Four numbered contributions come first, introduced by "Thus far", which signals an ongoing project (p. 2).
  - Algorithms are given as pseudocode figures (Figures 1–3).
  - Every limiting case of a parameter is named (pp. 9–10).
  - Hedging: "We suspect" for the OA-vs-BB criterion (p. 14), "This value for p might seems quite high" (p. 14), "Geometric considerations lead us to believe" (p. 16).
  - The results section is mostly diagnosis (why a solver wins where it does), not a victory lap (pp. 14–16).
  - Transferable technique: after the headline plot, spend the discussion on per-class explanations and testable hypotheses rather than on restating the win (pp. 14–16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contributions: a public instance library, a hybrid algorithm family, an open-source framework, and computational results (p. 2). The authors call it "the first step in an ongoing and ambitious project" (abstract p. 1).
  - Limits stated by the authors: Dicopt is much faster on many BatchS, RSyn and Syn instances (p. 14, Table 2); there are failures caused by Ipopt numerics (p. 14); which problems suit OA and which suit BB is an open question (p. 14).
  - Future directions: release on COIN-OR (p. 2).

### S016 · Geometry of interpolation sets in derivative free optimization (Mathematical Programming 2008, DOI 10.1007/s10107-006-0073-5)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K. Scheinberg (IBM Watson), L. N. Vicente (Coimbra; "Most of this paper was written while this author was visiting IBM T.J. Watson Research Center") (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the first of the CSV trilogy. It follows CST 1997 ×2 ([7], [8]; p. 26), and it announces the regression/underdetermined sequel (S028) (p. 2). S028 later cites it as Math. Program. 111 (2008) 141–172 (S028 p. 28). **Read**: full text, including the appendix on the cubic case (pp. 24–25) and the references (pp. 25–26).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap in the convergence theory.
  - Model-based DFO methods must satisfy Taylor-like error bounds on value and gradient if they are to inherit derivative-based convergence theory: as the step shrinks, the gradient error must vanish, so descent is eventually found (p. 2).
  - The classical Ciarlet–Raviart bound contains a set-dependent constant Λ_Y, which must stay *uniformly bounded* over all sets the algorithm uses (p. 2).
  - The existing maintenance schemes do not guarantee this. Powell's Lagrange-maximization rule has no proof of a uniform bound (p. 3, p. 5, pp. 18–19). The authors' own CST scheme [7], based on Newton polynomials, needs an "extra step" that replaces all interpolation points, which is "very expensive" (p. 19).

  Purpose: "provide the necessary theoretical foundation for various DFO sampling methods to guarantee such bounds" (p. 2). The paper treats polynomial interpolation only. Regression and underdetermined models are deferred to an "upcoming paper" (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: enter by naming the single property the convergence proof needs from the component you study (a uniformly bounded Λ_Y). Then show that each existing way of maintaining it either lacks a proof or costs too much (pp. 2–3, 18–19).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Reinterpret Lagrange polynomials geometrically, then tie them to a matrix one can factorize. The steps:
  1. **Coefficients view** (Lemma 3.1, p. 6): the Lagrange polynomials are exactly the coefficients λ(x) that express φ(x) in terms of the vectors φ(yⁱ), i.e. M(φ,Y)ᵀλ(x) = φ(x). They do not depend on the basis φ.
  2. **Λ-poisedness** (Definition 3.2, p. 7): Y is Λ-poised in B(1) if every x ∈ B(1) has such a λ(x) with ‖λ(x)‖ ≤ Λ. The quantity 1/Λ measures, in a sense, the distance to linear dependence of the vectors φ(yⁱ) (p. 7).
  3. **Equivalence with a matrix quantity** (Theorem 3.3, p. 8): take the *natural basis* of scaled monomials (1.2)/(3.3) and the set scaled to the unit ball. If ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ Λ, then Y is √p₁Λ-poised. Conversely, if Y is Λ-poised, then ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ θΛ, where θ is independent of Y and Λ. Moreover θ ≤ 1 for d = 1, and θ ≤ 4√p₁ for d = 2 (3.9) (p. 10).
  4. **Error bounds via the scaled matrix** (§4): for linear models, ‖e_g(x)‖ ≤ (5√n γ_L ‖L̂⁻¹‖/2) Δ (Theorem 4.1, p. 13). For quadratic models the Hessian, gradient and value errors are O(Δ), O(Δ²) and O(Δ³), with explicit constants such as α^H_Q = 3√2/2 (Theorem 4.2, p. 16). The bounds are restated in terms of κ(M̂) (§4.3, p. 17) and of Λ (§4.4, p. 18).
  5. **Algorithms** (§5): Algorithm 5.1 uses LU (pp. 20–21) and Algorithm 5.2 uses Gram–Schmidt/QR (pp. 22–23). Both factorize M point by point and enforce a pivot threshold ξ ∈ (0, 1/4). If some remaining point gives a pivot ≥ ξ, it is swapped in. Otherwise a new point is found by maximizing the pivot polynomial over the ball, which is always possible because of Lemma 3.4. The output bound is ‖M⁻¹‖ ≤ c(p₁) ε_growth/ξ (5.1) (p. 19), with c(p₁) = √p₁ for LU (p. 21) and p₁ for QR (p. 23).
  - Compared with Powell: the uniform bound is guaranteed. Compared with CST 1997: only the points that "spoil" poisedness are replaced, not the whole set (p. 19). Unchanged, and admitted as a drawback: the polynomial set may have to be completely recomputed at each update, whereas Powell's scheme updates it (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: when an abstract quality constant (a max over a ball of Lagrange polynomials) cannot be checked directly, prove it equivalent, up to constants independent of the data, to the norm of the inverse of a matrix. Then control that norm through pivot thresholds in a factorization (Theorem 3.3, p. 8; §5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Linear case: f is C¹ with Lipschitz gradient (γ_L). Quadratic case: f is C² with Lipschitz Hessian (γ_Q). Cubic case: third derivatives are Lipschitz (γ_C) (pp. 12, 14, 25).
  - Complete (determined) interpolation, p₁ = binom(n+d, n) (p. 2). The paper calls such models "fully linear" and "fully quadratic" (pp. 13–14). This is not the later class-based definition of fully linear models.
  - Without loss of generality, the smallest enclosing ball is centred at 0 and has radius 1, by shifting and scaling (p. 6).
  - y⁰ = 0, i.e. the current iterate is an interpolation point. If it is not, a shift puts the set inside B(2Δ) (p. 11).
  - The natural basis is required. The condition number of M(φ,Y) is meaningless for an arbitrary basis, since it can be made anything between 1 and ∞ (p. 4, Remark 3.1 p. 10).
  - The paper stresses that the actual size of the bound Λ is unimportant for theory; only its uniform boundedness matters. For efficiency, being able to improve Λ cheaply matters (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Norm-equivalence argument** (Theorem 3.3, pp. 8–9). The easy direction is ‖λ(x)‖ ≤ √p₁‖M⁻ᵀ‖ because ‖φ̄(x)‖_∞ ≤ 1 on B(1) (p. 8). For the converse:
    - Nonsingularity: if M were singular, some v ≠ 0 would satisfy φ̄(x)ᵀv = 0 on the whole ball, so v = 0 (p. 8).
    - The bound: ψ(v) = max_{x∈B(1)} |vᵀφ̄(x)| is a norm, so ψ(v) ≥ σ‖v‖ by equivalence of norms in finite dimension. Take the top right-singular vector v̄ of M⁻ᵀ and a y with |v̄ᵀφ̄(y)| ≥ σ. Orthogonality of the singular directions then gives ‖M⁻ᵀφ̄(y)‖ ≥ σ‖M⁻ᵀv̄‖, so θ = 1/σ (p. 9).
  - **Explicit θ** (Lemma 3.4, pp. 9–10): a quadratic with ‖v̂‖_∞ = 1 attains at least 1/4 in absolute value on the unit ball. The proof fixes every variable not in the largest term to zero and examines three cases, using the points (±1/√2, ±1/√2) for the cross term xᵢxⱼ (p. 10). The same argument later turns the step of Algorithm 5.1 that maximizes over the ball into "a trivial enumeration" (p. 21).
  - **Scale invariance** (Lemma 3.5, p. 11): Lagrange polynomials are scale invariant, but κ(M) is not, since it blows up as Δ → 0. Hence the measure is always taken on the set scaled to B(1) (p. 11).
  - **Error bounds** (pp. 12–17): subtract the interpolation conditions from the error equation, Taylor-expand f around x, and subtract the first equation from the others to cancel e_f(x). This leaves a linear system L e_g(x) = O(Δ²) whose matrix *does not depend on x* (4.7) (pp. 12–13). The right-hand side is bounded with Dennis–Schnabel's Lipschitz lemmas (p. 13, p. 15). In the quadratic case the diagonal scaling diag(D_Δ⁻¹, D_Δ²⁻¹) (4.18) splits the Hessian, gradient and value orders (pp. 15–17).
  - **Sharpness remark**: in terms of M̂, the bound's constant grows linearly in √p₁, whereas the classical bound (2.1) gives a constant linear in p₁ (p. 17). The approach also yields a bound on the derivatives of the Lagrange polynomials, ‖Dλ(x)‖_∞ ≤ 2θΛ (p. 18).
  - **Algorithm guarantees**: Propositions 5.1 and 5.2 give |D_ii| ≥ ξ and |R_ii| ≥ ξ/√p₁ (pp. 21, 23). The resulting ‖M⁻¹‖ bounds involve ‖L⁻¹‖‖U⁻¹‖ or ‖R̄⁻¹‖, i.e. growth factors that are "expected to be of reasonable size" but are not bounded (pp. 21, 23). The LU version costs O(n⁶) flops (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: to bound ‖A⁻¹‖ through a functional property, show that v ↦ max_{x∈B} |vᵀφ(x)| is a norm and use the equivalence of norms in finite dimension. Then make the constant explicit with a small case analysis (pp. 9–10).
  - Transferable technique: in Taylor-error proofs for interpolation, subtract one interpolation equation from the others to eliminate the unknown value error. This leaves a system whose matrix does not depend on the evaluation point (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Experiments**: Minimal, and labelled as such (§5.3, pp. 23–24).
  - Setup: 100 random points in a unit cube centred at zero. Interpolation sets are selected from them by Powell's Lagrange-maximization rule, by the LU algorithm and by the QR algorithm, and ‖M⁻¹‖ is compared.
  - Result: all three give very close results, with ‖M⁻¹‖ mostly between 10 and 100 and never above 1000 (p. 24).
  - Not stated in the text: the dimension n, the number of repetitions, and any optimization run.
  - Authors' caveats: the behaviour "may be the consequence of the random nature of the chosen interpolation points". Without a real DFO package "it is difficult to reproduce a more practical example" (p. 24). Whether the algorithms are useful in practice "will require the implementation of the algorithms within a good DFO package" (p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: when a theory paper can only afford a toy test, state exactly what the test cannot show (random points, no optimizer in the loop) (p. 24).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Ciarlet–Raviart [4] is the source of the classical bounds. Their "stretchedness" split is shown to mislead by a counterexample: six points on a circle are nonpoised for quadratic interpolation, so small perturbations are nearly nonpoised yet have small stretchedness (p. 6).
  - Powell [16, 17]: the rule is called reasonable and "seems to work well in practice", but it has no uniform-bound proof (pp. 18–19). The authors expect their algorithms may not outperform it (p. 23).
  - The authors' own CST 1997 [7] has the same gap as Powell plus an expensive full-replacement step (p. 19). Earlier work by the authors is criticized in the same terms as the competitor's.
  - de Boor [9] used Gaussian elimination for interpolation with *fixed* data. The optimization setting differs because the sample set changes during the run (p. 19).
  - Pattern search is related through its finite sets of positive bases (p. 18). Surveys and implicit filtering are cited (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The thesis is in the abstract: maintaining a bounded constant is the "main task" of such an algorithm (p. 1).
  - Novelty is claimed selectively. The bounds are not claimed as new: the section's goal is their usefulness and simple derivation (p. 17). The novelty claimed is the intuition, the condition-number link and the algorithms (pp. 3, 11).
  - Optimization motivations are addressed explicitly: the paper adjusts the presentation to smallest enclosing balls rather than convex hulls "to our DFO motivation" (p. 6).
  - Candid statements of possible failure: the authors concede that the new algorithms might not perform better than Powell's (p. 23). Powell-type rules may also be provable with this theory, which is left for "a separate study" (p. 24).
  - One practical claim is made without evidence in the paper: maintaining well-poisedness at every iteration is "more efficient" than doing so only when the proof needs it (p. 18).
  - There is no conclusion section; §5.3 plays that role (pp. 23–24).
  - Transferable technique: separate what is new from what is only re-derived for this audience, and say so in the section itself (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contributions: theory. The Λ-poisedness definition, its equivalence with ‖M̂⁻¹‖, Taylor-like bounds for d = 1, 2 (and 3 in the appendix), and two factorization-based algorithms that certify and repair a set (pp. 3, 7–25).
  - Limits stated by the authors: practical usefulness is untested (p. 23); the polynomials may need full recomputation at each update (p. 19); θ for degree > 2 is "beyond the scope" (p. 10); the cubic constants are not given (p. 25).
  - Future directions: prove uniform poisedness for Powell-like rules (p. 24); regression and underdetermined models (p. 2), delivered in S028.
  - Not stated by the authors: the growth factors ε_growth that enter the final bound are not bounded (pp. 21, 23).

**Method links**:

  - Step 1 (Taylor-like bounds): Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 (pp. 13, 16).

### S028 · Geometry of sample sets in derivative-free optimization: polynomial regression and underdetermined interpolation (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2008, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drn046)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K. Scheinberg (IBM Watson), L. N. Vicente (Coimbra) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the sequel announced in S016 (S016 p. 2). It precedes and points to the book *Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization* ([9], "to appear") and the SIOPT convergence paper ([10], "under final review") (p. 28). **Read**: full text, including Figures 4.1, 5.1 and 5.2 and the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper extends S016 in two directions, each motivated by a practical regime (pp. 1–2):
  - more points than basis elements (regression): evaluations are cheap enough to oversample but noisy, so regression may smooth;
  - fewer points than basis elements (underdetermined interpolation): evaluations are too expensive for a full quadratic model at each iteration, yet first-order models are inefficient.

  The authors blame the lack of supporting theory for the rare use of regression in DFO (p. 1). Underdetermined models were already in practical use: the COIN-OR DFO code used minimum-norm models in early iterations, and Powell's method uses least-Frobenius-norm updating throughout (p. 2). The key claim is that "the theory in [8] does not immediately extend" (p. 2).

  The problem is to show that the Taylor-like bounds needed by the SIOPT convergence framework [10] hold for regression and underdetermined models under a suitable Λ-poisedness (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: motivate each generalization by the cost/noise regime in which a practitioner would want it (cheap and noisy → regression; expensive → underdetermined) (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Carry S016's coefficients view (M(φ,Y)ᵀλ(x) = φ(x)) into both non-square cases and pick the canonical solution:
  - **Regression**: the system in λ is underdetermined. Its minimum-norm solution gives the Lagrange regression polynomials (Lemma 2.4, p. 9), and the regression model is still m(x) = Σ f(yⁱ)Lᵢ(x) (Lemma 2.3, p. 8).
  - **Underdetermined interpolation**: the system in λ is overdetermined. Its least-squares solution gives the minimum-norm Lagrange polynomials (Lemma 5.4, p. 22). The authors call this "a kind of duality" (p. 22).
  - **Choice of model in the underdetermined case**:
    - Truncating the basis is arbitrary, because it keeps elements solely by their order in the basis, and it can fail even when a different sub-basis would be well poised (Example 5.1, pp. 18–19).
    - The best sub-basis is not robust. Example 5.2 gives two nearly identical sample sets whose best sub-bases produce very different models, each biased toward one basis component (pp. 19–20).
    - The minimum-norm solution is robust to small perturbations and spreads the gradient over the components (p. 20). It is therefore chosen with the natural basis. Four reasons are given for that basis: it appears in Taylor bounds, it is the obvious implementation choice, it is well scaled, and Λ-poisedness in it transfers to any well-conditioned basis (Remark 5.1, p. 23).
  - **Algorithms**: no new algorithm is given. The S016 algorithms apply to regression, because a Λ-poised subset makes the whole superset Λ-poised (p. 14). Algorithms that target regression sets directly are left open (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: to choose between two ways of making an ill-posed fit unique, build two nearly identical inputs and show that one rule jumps while the other varies continuously (Example 5.2, pp. 19–20).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Smoothness as in S016: Lipschitz gradient for the linear case, Lipschitz Hessian for the quadratic case (pp. 15, 25).
  - The sample set is shifted and scaled so that its smallest enclosing ball is B(1), with y⁰ = 0 (pp. 10, 14).
  - Poisedness means full column rank of M for regression (p. 5) and full row rank for the underdetermined case (p. 21).
  - The natural basis is fixed, and the minimum-norm Lagrange polynomials depend on the basis (pp. 20, 23).
  - Remark 5.2 notes that Powell's definition also requires a subset of n+1 linearly poised points, and the authors write: "We impose this requirement directly on Y when we derive the first-order error bound" (pp. 23–24).
  - Noise enters only informally: the authors say it is "easy to show" that regression on noisy and on true values converge to each other pointwise as the number of points grows, if the noise is i.i.d. with zero mean, and cite the book for this (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Lemma 1.1** (p. 4, maximum-determinant subset): among m > n vectors, the n-subset with the largest |det| expresses every other vector with coefficients of modulus at most 1, by Cramer's rule. So any combination with |λᵢ| ≤ Λ can be rewritten on the subset with coefficients of modulus at most (m − n + 1)Λ. D. Coppersmith is thanked for help with it (p. 27).
  - Uses of Lemma 1.1:
    - regression Λ-poised ⇒ some q₁-subset is (p − q + 1)√q₁ Λ-poised for interpolation; any Λ-poised q₁-subset makes the whole set Λ-poised for regression (Theorem 2.7, p. 10);
    - minimum-norm vs sub-basis poisedness, equivalent up to √(p₁q₁)θ and (q − p + 1) factors (Theorem 5.6, pp. 22–23).
  - **Basis independence of the regression polynomial** (p. 5): change basis ψ = Pᵀφ, so M(ψ,Y) = M(φ,Y)P and α_φ = Pα_ψ.
  - **Reduced-SVD versions of S016's Theorem 3.3** (Theorem 2.8, pp. 11–13; Theorem 5.7, p. 24). The proof is the same norm-equivalence argument, but "there are a few extra steps" (p. 11). θ ≤ 4√q₁ for d = 2 (2.13). The bound will "grow rapidly (most likely, exponentially)" with the degree (p. 13).
  - **Error bounds**:
    - Regression (Theorems 3.1–3.2, pp. 15–16) is stated *without proof* as "straightforward adaptations" of S016 (p. 14). The constants carry √p₁ (linear) or √(p₁q₁) (quadratic).
    - Underdetermined quadratic (Theorem 5.8, pp. 25–26): only the projection Vᵀ of the scaled errors onto the p-dimensional row space is bounded. The authors call this reasonable "since we do not have enough information to do better", and it reduces to S016's bound when p = q (p. 26).
  - **Remark 2.1** (p. 8): the construction fails for ℓ1 and ℓ∞ regression, because only least squares is linear in f_Y.
  - Transferable technique: when moving from square to rectangular systems, use the maximum-determinant subset and Cramer's rule to pass between *some solution with small coefficients* and *a square subsystem with small coefficients*, paying a factor (m − n + 1) (Lemma 1.1, p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: when information is missing (fewer points than unknowns), bound the projection of the error onto the directions that are observed, and show that it collapses to the full bound in the square case (p. 26).
- **D5 Experiments**: Illustrative and two-dimensional, and described by the authors as "very limited" (p. 26).
  - **Regression vs best subset** (§4, pp. 16–17): three test functions f1–f3, of which only f1 is reported "since the results for the others seem to follow the same pattern" (p. 16).
    - Setup: p random points in the unit square plus the origin. The best-subset (BS) model uses the subset with the best condition number and is compared with the least-squares regression (LSR) model on all points. Errors are measured on a 0.1 lattice as max (E1) and summed ℓ2 (E2). There are 200 runs, a win needs a margin above 0.001 in both errors, and p₁ ranges from 7 to 31 (pp. 16–17).
    - Results: both models improve with more points, and BS becomes progressively worse relative to LSR, e.g. with 21 points BS is worse 138 times and LSR 28 times (Figure 4.1, p. 17). The authors nevertheless state that "no model was consistently better" (p. 17). This matches the theoretical trade-off: Λ falls as points are added, but the p₁ factor in the bound grows (p. 16).
    - The supposed advantages of regression under noise and spikes are argued, not tested: the spike argument "has not been verified experimentally" (p. 17).
  - **Minimum-norm vs best basis** (§5.5, pp. 26–27): p₁ = 5, 4, 3, on the unit square and on a square of radius 0.01. The best-basis (BB) model is worse, and on the small square the minimum-norm model is better in every decided run (BB worse 58/193/118, MN worse 0; Figure 5.2, p. 27).
    - Possible inconsistency: in Figure 5.1, the p₁ = 5 row lists a cumulative BB error of 81950 against an MN error of 653000, while BB is worse 133 times and the text says BB was worse (pp. 26–27). This may be a typo in the source; it was not resolved.
  - The authors say that showing the benefit of regression fully would need "a separate computational study" (p. 3). For minimum-norm models they rely on existing performance in the COIN-OR DFO code and in Powell's work (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when comparing two model-building rules, repeat the comparison on a shrunken region (radius 0.01), where Taylor-type theory applies, as well as on a large one (Figures 5.1–5.2, p. 27).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - It continues S016 ([8]) (abstract p. 1).
  - It connects to regression-based simplex gradients in implicit filtering (Kelley et al. [2, 3]) and in Custódio–Vicente [11, 12] (p. 1).
  - It connects to the minimum-norm models of the COIN-OR DFO code [1] and to Powell's least-Frobenius-norm updating [17] (p. 2), whose underdetermined Lagrange polynomials are given as a variant of Definition 5.2 (Remark 5.2, pp. 23–24).
  - Approximation-theory definitions (Ciarlet–Raviart [4], Davis [13]) are called "ill-suited for an optimization audience" and are simplified (p. 2, p. 7).
  - Colson–Toint [5] is cited for exploiting partial separability (p. 18).
  - Deng–Ferris [14] handle noise by repeated sampling at the same points. The authors offer regression at, for instance, random points as an alternative when repeated sampling does not reduce noise (p. 27).
  - Kelley and Toint are thanked for discussions (p. 27).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The abstract states continuity with [8] and the thesis that the "mechanisms and concepts which control the quality of the sample sets" of interpolation models extend to the over- and underdetermined cases (p. 1).
  - Section 4 is framed as a question, "will such interpolation provide consistently worse or better models", and answered 'neither' from the theory before the experiment (p. 16).
  - Unverified claims are marked as such (p. 17). Proofs that are adaptations are omitted and flagged (p. 14).
  - Hedging: "in our opinion" (p. 1), "Perhaps, the main advantage" (p. 27).
  - The conclusion points forward to the book and to noisy problems (pp. 26–27).
  - Transferable technique: pose the practical question your theory raises, say what the theory predicts (here, a trade-off), and only then show the small experiment (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contributions: theory. Definitions of Lagrange polynomials and Λ-poisedness for regression and for minimum-norm interpolation, their relation to complete interpolation and to κ(M̄), and error bounds (pp. 7–26).
  - Limits stated by the authors:
    - "very limited" computations (p. 26);
    - no algorithm that targets regression sets directly (p. 14);
    - θ likely exponential in the degree (p. 13);
    - underdetermined bounds control only a projection (p. 26);
    - no ℓ1/ℓ∞ regression (p. 8);
    - no complete convergence theory until the book (pp. 26–27).
  - Future directions: in the book, algorithms that build Λ-poised regression and minimum-Frobenius sets, and global convergence of a modified UOBYQA-type algorithm; regression for noisy functions (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ core evidence for step 2 (choose the geometry condition for the model type: interpolation, regression or underdetermined). This paper is where the step comes from (pp. 9–10, 22–24). It also supports step 4: the bounds "can be used to show global convergence of various optimization methods based on least-squares regression models as long as the sample sets for regression remain Λ–poised" (p. 16).
- Method 3 limitation (noise) ⚠ variant: the SKILL says Method 3 is weak on strongly noisy blackboxes. This paper proposes regression models as the remedy for noise (pp. 17, 27), but supports it only by a cited asymptotic argument, not by experiments. This supports the Workflow D checkpoint (switch the model type to regression) as a design intention, not as a demonstrated fix.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: theory first. Computation is deferred to "a separate computational study" (p. 3), and algorithms and convergence to the book (p. 27).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the tests are illustrative 2-D random instances with one reported function, not a public test set (pp. 16–17, 26–27). This is acceptable for a theory paper, and the authors label it as limited (p. 26).
- Method 5 ✅ weak evidence: if the sparsity of the derivatives is known, it "can be exploited trivially" by choosing the matching sub-basis (p. 18). Colson–Toint's partial separability is cited (p. 18).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: approximation-theory notions (generalized Lagrange polynomials, Lebesgue constants) are simplified and re-proved for an optimization audience (pp. 2, 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *equivalence up to data-independent constants between an abstract quality measure and a computable matrix quantity* (Theorems 2.8 and 5.7, pp. 11, 24; also S016 Theorem 3.3).
- New-pattern candidate: *theory for a device practitioners already use*: minimum-norm and least-Frobenius-norm underdetermined models were already used extensively in practical implementations before this theory existed (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: *perturbation robustness as the selection criterion between model-building rules* (Example 5.2, pp. 19–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] For a non-square fit, identify the canonical solution (minimum-norm or least-squares) whose Lagrange-type representation m(x) = Σ f(yⁱ)Lᵢ(x) survives (Lemmas 2.3–2.4, 5.3–5.4, pp. 8–9, 21–22).
- [D2] Rule out an alternative with a perturbation counterexample: two nearly equal inputs give very different outputs (Example 5.2, pp. 19–20).
- [D4] Use the maximum-determinant subset (Cramer's rule) to move between rectangular and square poisedness, at a cost of a factor (m − n + 1) (Lemma 1.1, p. 4; Theorems 2.7, 5.6).
- [D4] When data underdetermine the model, bound only the error's projection onto the observed subspace and check that it reduces to the square case (Theorem 5.8, pp. 25–26).
- [D5] Report paired win counts over 200 random runs with a stated tie margin (0.001), on both a large region and a region of radius 0.01 (pp. 16–17, 26–27).
- [D7] Answer the practical question with what the theory predicts ('neither', because Λ falls while the p₁ factor grows) before showing numbers (p. 16).
