### S040 · Numerical experiments with the LANCELOT package (Release A) for large-scale nonlinear optimization (Mathematical Programming 1996, DOI 10.1007/bf02592099)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. Gould (Rutherford Appleton Laboratory), Ph. L. Toint (Namur); Conn at IBM T.J. Watson (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the testing twin of the LANCELOT book (ref [17]). It cites the convergence theory behind the package: CGT 1988 [10], the augmented Lagrangian [13] and the inner-iteration count [18] (pp. 7, 15). The complete per-run data are in a separate report [16], which is S103 (card in s2-03). The MINOS comparison is "reported on separately" [4] (p. 5), which is S102/S120 (cards in s2-05/s2-06). The paper's list of LANCELOT weak points (p. 49) names the fixes that later became S089 (slack structure, [14]) and S007 (Lagrangian barrier, [15]). The work was funded by ARPA/AFOSR (p. 3). **Read**: full text, §1–§8, the acknowledgements and the references (pp. 1–55). The figures could not be read (see Extraction quality).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an engineering question about the authors' own package. Research in large-scale optimization is described as a major subject of interest in the community (p. 5). LANCELOT has produced theory and software, and the purpose here is to report experiments "on a sizeable collection of test problems" and to draw "first conclusions on the respective merits of the algorithmic options available in the package" (p. 5). The problem class is min f(x) subject to c(x) = 0 and simple bounds, with smooth f and c and large n and/or m. Group partial separability is exploited, and convergence to local minimizers is sought "from all starting points" (p. 6). Inequalities become equalities through slacks, which "is extensively used in simplex-like methods" (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: state the aim of a software paper as a ranking of the package's own options, not as a win over rivals. Send the rival comparison to a separate paper (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: The paper has two halves: describe every algorithmic option precisely (§2–§5), then test each option against a fixed default (§6–§7).
  - **Architecture**: three nested iteration levels (Figure 2, p. 8):
    - AUGLG *major* iterations on the augmented Lagrangian;
    - SBMIN *minor* iterations, a trust-region method on the bound-constrained subproblem;
    - *cg-iterations* inside the bound-constrained quadratic subproblem (pp. 7–8).
  - **SBMIN** (pp. 9–14) works in four stages:
    1. test the projected gradient;
    2. find the generalized Cauchy point (GCP) along the projected-gradient arc;
    3. reduce the model further over the variables that are free at the GCP;
    4. apply the ratio test and update the radius.

    The GCP carries convergence, and the step beyond it carries the rate (pp. 10–11). The inner stopping rule (3.23) is taken from Lescrenier [41] because it guarantees an asymptotically superlinear rate (p. 12).
  - **A design choice stated openly**: the acceptance rule may reject the lowest point found so far. Keeping it "has not proved beneficial in our experience" (p. 13).
  - **AUGLG parameters** (pp. 16–17): τ = 0.1, μ₀ = 0.1, ω₀ = 1 and η₀ = 0.1258925, so that η^(0) = 0.01. Initial multipliers are 0 and the scaling is S = I (p. 16). The authors add that "Automatic choices of the initial penalty parameter in penalty function methods are notoriously hard to justify" (p. 17).
  - **The option space** (§5, pp. 17–27):
    - Curtis–Reid automatic scaling of the Jacobian at a typical x (pp. 17–18);
    - direct solvers: mltf (MA27 multifrontal) and semltf (Schnabel–Eskow modified Cholesky) (pp. 18–19);
    - CG preconditioners: none, diagonal, band(m_b), Munksgaard incomplete factorization, full Schnabel–Eskow, full Gill–Murray–Ponceleón–Saunders, and an expanding band whose width is set by a three-level rule on the projected-gradient norm (5.12) (pp. 20–22);
    - derivatives: exact; forward-difference gradients switching to central differences near convergence (fdg); partitioned PSB, SR1, BFGS or DFP updates on the element *internal variables* (pp. 22–26);
    - SBMIN options: approximate GCP, ℓ2-norm trust region, and an accurate BQP solve (pp. 26–27).
  - **Test design**: one factor at a time. Every "basic variant" differs from the default in exactly one option (pp. 32–33). The one exception is fdg, which is finite-difference gradients plus SR1. That gives 21 variants × 943 problems = 19803 runs (pp. 33–34).
  - Transferable technique: fix a documented default configuration (Figure 5 prints the whole specification file, p. 32), then test variants that each change exactly one option. Every difference is then attributable to one option.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and c are twice continuously differentiable (pp. 8, 14). The objective is group partially separable (5.13)–(5.14), which the authors justify by the fact that "any function with a continuous, sparse Hessian matrix may be written in this form" (p. 23). Each equality constraint becomes a single group in the augmented Lagrangian (p. 23). The convergence guarantees are cited, not re-proved (pp. 7, 10, 15–16).
  - Practical constraints are admitted. The options scaling, mltf, semltf, expband, seprc, gmpsprc and munksg need Harwell Subroutine Library code, which requires a licence, so they "could not be selected as default" (p. 34). BFGS and DFP updates "must be skipped" whenever they would lose positive definiteness, which partly explains their poor results (p. 36). Imposing positive definiteness on element Hessians has "little justification" (p. 26).
  - Transferable technique: name the non-mathematical constraints on a default setting, such as licensing and storage, next to the performance data (p. 34).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There are no new proofs. The paper's analysis tools are for experiments:
  - **Failure taxonomy** per variant: succ, stall, infs, mem, iters, cpu, error (p. 34). Stall is not always a failure "from the user's point of view", because some runs stall very near the solution (p. 34).
  - **Same-answer filter**: a run counts in the efficiency comparisons only if its final value matches the best critical value found for that problem to within 0.001% (p. 38).
  - **Two summaries side by side**: averages over the problems solved by all variants, and *rank histograms* over the 826 problems solved by at least one variant, with ranks clustered 1–3, 4–6, … (p. 39). The two summaries disagree for semltf, which has the best ranking but the worst average CPU. The cause is traced to a few large unconstrained problems with indefinite early Hessians (p. 45). munksg shows the reverse (p. 45).
  - **Normalized inner-work metric**: cg-iterations per minor iteration per variable, which lies in [0, 1]. The authors state its two sources of approximation (p. 42).
  - **A posteriori diagnostic experiment**: the overflow failures of gmpsprc are attributed to its eigenvalue-perturbation threshold being set at machine precision. Rerunning with (machine precision)^(3/4) avoids the overflow, which is consistent with Schlick [51] (pp. 36–37).
  - Transferable technique: when averages and rankings disagree, report both and trace the disagreement to the few problems that cause it (p. 45).
  - Transferable technique: when a rival technique fails oddly, form a hypothesis about the cause and test it with one targeted rerun before blaming the method (pp. 36–37).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Test set**: CUTE. The first 624 unconstrained or bound-constrained instances and the first 319 constrained ones, giving 943 instances from 398 problems (pp. 27–28). C17–C19 were excluded because they were expected to exceed the time limit, and C261–C262 because they are nondifferentiable (p. 28). A subset of 45 large instances, each annotated with its application area, is analysed separately (Table 2, pp. 30–31).
  - **Problem characteristics** reported: distributions of size and of m/n (Figures 3–4), and relative nonlinearity of objective and constraints (Table 1) (pp. 28–30).
  - **Protocol** (p. 32): at most 1000 iterations and 18000 CPU seconds; initial radius 1.0; printing disabled. The derivative checker was on but its warnings were ignored, "to reflect what would be a reasonable strategy for a cautious user". All derivatives had been checked beforehand.
  - **Machines**: two DECstations 5000/200 with 48 MB. f77 was used without optimization because of a compiler bug, and CPU times were cross-checked between the two machines (p. 34). The runs took "8 months of nearly uninterrupted computation" (p. 51).
  - **Cost units**: minor iterations, which equal function evaluations because each minor iteration evaluates the functions exactly once (p. 39); cg-iterations; CPU time.
  - **Results**:
    - Reliability (Table 3, p. 35): the default succeeds on 873 of 943 (92.5%) and seprc on 878 (93.1%). dfp is lowest at 621. On the subset, default and band(10) are best with 41 of 45 (pp. 35–36).
    - Coverage: 919 problems are solved by at least one variant and 441 by all. Only 12 subset problems are solved by all variants (p. 38).
    - Efficiency: semltf, seprc, gmpsprc and accBQP use the fewest minor iterations. The default is "least often amongst the worst" (p. 39). Full-matrix and incomplete-factorization preconditioners win on cg-iterations (p. 42). munksg has the best average CPU. sr1 is the most efficient quasi-Newton update (pp. 45–46).
    - Finite differences: fdg solves 83% of the problems, "a quite acceptable score" (p. 38).
  - **Weak points** that the summaries do not show (p. 49):
    1. slacks inflate problems with many inequalities, with a fix given in [14];
    2. there is no special treatment of linear or network constraints;
    3. GCP active-set prediction is called disappointing in practice, so a barrier treatment [15] is suggested.
  - Transferable technique: after the summary statistics, add a short list of weak points seen only in the detailed runs, each paired with a planned fix (p. 49).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper positions LANCELOT against itself, since the MINOS comparison is deferred to [4] (p. 5). Rival linear-algebra techniques are treated on the merits. The Schnabel–Eskow and Gill–Murray–Ponceleón–Saunders modified factorizations are compared head to head. The poorer one's failures are diagnosed and shown to be avoidable, not dismissed (pp. 36–37). The incomplete-factorization result is qualified by the make-up of the test set: discretized PDE problems, where such preconditioners "earned their good reputation", are a minority (p. 37). The references rest on the authors' own theory ([10], [13], [18], [19]) and on standard texts (Dennis–Schnabel [22], Gill–Murray–Wright [31]) (pp. 51–55).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Each metric section ends in a numbered list of conclusions: 13 on reliability (pp. 35–38), 6 on minor iterations (p. 39), 9 on cg-iterations (pp. 42–43) and 12 on CPU (pp. 45–46). Hedging is graded: "perhaps surprisingly" (p. 35), "somewhat surprising" (p. 36), "the difference is admittedly marginal" (p. 39), "is not clear. One possible explanation…" (p. 42). The final conclusions are split into points about the package and points "of more general interest" (p. 50). The paper closes by asking users to report the "(undoubtly many)" aspects that could be improved (p. 50).
  - Transferable technique: separate the conclusions that hold for your package from those you claim hold more generally (p. 50).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is software evaluation plus general computational lessons. For the package: the default is "reliable and acceptably efficient". The ℓ2 trust region, diagonal rescaling and DFP "could probably be removed from future releases". Automatic scaling and accurate BQP solves should not be defaults but "may provide excellent behaviour on some harder problems" (p. 50). General lessons (p. 50):
  - difficulty is linked more to nonlinearity than to size;
  - direct factorizations are most robust when used as preconditioners;
  - use exact second derivatives when available, and partitioned SR1 otherwise;
  - finite-difference gradients with SR1 are acceptable even for large problems;
  - full factorizations may not scale, while banded preconditioners probably will.

  The limits are specific: the three weak points (p. 49); a test set dominated by well-scaled problems (pp. 35, 45); and a CPU unit measured without compiler optimization (p. 34). Future work points to the barrier [15] and structured trust regions [19] (p. 50).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): this is the testing twin of the LANCELOT book, with its theory cited at every algorithmic step (pp. 7, 10, 12, 15–16) and its full data in a companion report [16] (p. 34).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): a public collection (CUTE, 943 instances), per-option results for all 21 variants with failures coded (Method 2 step 5), and a separately analysed large-scale subset (pp. 27–38). The paper also states that minor iterations equal function evaluations (p. 39), so the cost the user pays can be read off.
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): there are no noisy variants. The second tier is a set of large CUTE instances tagged with application areas, not live engineering problems (pp. 30–31). CPU and cg-iterations sit beside evaluations as cost units (pp. 42, 45).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): exploiting structure is built into the package (group partial separability, internal variables, partitioned updates, pp. 22–26). Exact second derivatives are recommended whenever available, with finite differences as a measured fallback (pp. 24, 38, 50).
- new-pattern candidate: **Self-audit of one's own solver starts a research line**. Weak point 1 cites the slack-structure note [14], which is S089, and weak point 3 cites the barrier method [15], which is S007 (p. 49). This is a third paper with the candidate already seen in S089 (s01) and S054 (s2-06).
- new-pattern candidate: **One-factor-at-a-time variants around a documented default** (pp. 32–33).
- new-pattern candidate: **Averages and rank histograms side by side, with outliers traced** (pp. 39, 45).
- new-pattern candidate: **Prune options on evidence**. The authors recommend removing their own options that the data do not support (p. 50).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Print the full default specification file and define every variant as the default with exactly one change (pp. 32–33).
- [D4] Before comparing efficiency, discard runs that converged to a different critical value (0.001% filter), and say how many were discarded per variant (p. 38).

- [D4] Diagnose a surprising failure with one targeted a posteriori rerun, here a changed perturbation threshold (pp. 36–37).

- [D7] End with separate lists of package-specific and general conclusions, and name the options you would remove (p. 50).

### S013 · On the convergence of derivative-free methods for unconstrained optimization (Approximation theory and optimization: tributes to MJD Powell 1997, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K. Scheinberg (Columbia University, supported by an IBM Cooperative Fellowship), Ph. L. Toint (Namur); Conn at IBM T.J. Watson (pp. 1, 22) · **Position in trajectory**: This was an invited presentation at the Powellfest, Cambridge, July 1996, and is dedicated to Powell's 60th birthday (pp. 1–2). The published version is in Iserles and Buhmann (eds.), pp. 83–108 (as cited in S020, p. 10). It gives the first convergence theory for the Conn–Toint 1996 algorithm (S019, card in c2-02), which it cites for "encouraging numerical results" (p. 5). The CST 1997 survey (S009, card in c02) reuses its geometry passage almost verbatim; for example the sentence on "an important property proved by Sauer and Xu" appears at S009 file p. 15, found by grep. It is also the predecessor that CSV 2008 (S016) and CSV 2009 (S012) later treat as impractical and set out to replace (see Cross-card notes). **Read**: full text, §1–§6, every proof and the reference list (pp. 1–25).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min f(x) with f smooth but ∇f (and so ∇²f) not computable (1.1) (p. 2). The entry is practitioner demand. In "the cases presented to the authors", f is very expensive, and derivatives are missing because f is a physical, chemical or econometric measurement or, "more commonly", the output of a large simulation whose source code is effectively unavailable. Such problems are "surprisingly frequent in the industrial world" (p. 2). Four user strategies are sorted (pp. 2–3):
  1. direct search (Nelder–Mead, PDS): little user effort, but smoothness is poorly exploited, so it needs many evaluations;
  2. automatic differentiation: not applicable to black boxes;
  3. finite differences with quasi-Newton: useful and sometimes surprisingly efficient, in the authors' words;
  4. modelling the function itself, which is the paper's focus. It is backed by a long quotation from Powell (1974): the secant method's rate of 1.618 per function value beats Newton with a differenced derivative, at about 1.414 (p. 3).

  The stated aim is theoretical: to define "a broad class of derivative free trust-region methods and consider their convergence theory" (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: open a theory paper by listing the options the user actually has, with the cost of each, so that the class you analyse is the one left standing (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The idea is to replace the Taylor-model accuracy of standard trust-region theory with an interpolation error bound. The bound is imported from multivariate approximation theory (Sauer–Xu 1995, simplified by Sauer 1996), and a checkable geometry condition makes it hold (pp. 2, 9).
  - **Representation chosen for the proof**: Newton fundamental polynomials (NFPs) are used instead of Powell's Lagrange polynomials. They have some computational advantages, but "the main reason for this choice is that we will exploit an error formula associated with this latter class of polynomials" (p. 7).
  - **Pivot-threshold construction**: procedure CNP builds the NFP basis block by block and accepts a point only if its pivot |N(y)| exceeds a threshold θ (pp. 8–9). Poisedness becomes "non-zero pivots", and well-poisedness becomes sufficiently large pivots (p. 9).
  - ***Adequate* geometry in a ball Q_k(δ)**: |Y| ≥ n + 1, all points in the ball, and all NFP values bounded by a constant κ_n > 2^{|Y[d]|} (3.14)–(3.16) (p. 11).
  - **The framework** (pp. 14–15):
    - Step 1 builds the model, possibly from fewer points than a full quadratic needs. *If ‖g_k‖ ≤ ε_g and Y is inadequate in A_k*, a ball of radius proportional to ‖g_k‖, it improves the geometry until Y is adequate in a ball of radius δ_k ∈ (0, μ‖g_k‖] (symbols reconstructed).
    - Step 2 minimizes the model in the trust region.
    - Step 3 inserts the trial point if ρ_k ≥ η₁; otherwise it improves the geometry in B_k if Y is inadequate there.
    - Step 4 increases the radius on success. It *reduces* the radius only if ρ_k < η₁ **and** Y is adequate in B_k; otherwise the radius is kept.
    - Step 5 moves to the best interpolation point x̂_k if ρ̂_k = (f(x_k) − f(x̂_k))/(m_k(x_k) − m_k(x_k + s_k)) ≥ η₀. So a geometry point can become the next iterate.
  - **Kept from standard trust regions**: the model, the ratio test and most of the radius management (p. 17).
  - **Changed** (p. 17): three differences that make "the standard convergence theory inapplicable" (p. 18):
    1. the model gradient is not the true gradient;
    2. a model cannot be judged bad, and the radius cut, until its geometry is certified;
    3. the iterate may move to a geometry point with a lower value, because expensive values are "exploited".
  - Transferable technique (Method 3 step 3 in embryo): make radius reduction conditional on a certified model, and add a small-gradient test that forces certification in a ball proportional to ‖g_k‖. The model is then never trusted at a point where it claims criticality (pp. 14, 17).
  - Transferable technique: when the construction of a model has a known error formula in one basis, adopt that basis, and say openly that the proof dictated the choice (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The authors choose a short list of assumptions "In order to keep our analysis simple and hopefully intuitive" (p. 6):
  - A.1: f ∈ C² with gradient *and* Hessian uniformly bounded on all of Rⁿ. A globally bounded gradient is a strong assumption.
  - A.2: f bounded below.
  - A.3: the model Hessians are uniformly bounded (p. 6).

  The models are at most quadratic, but higher degrees are said to extend "readily" (p. 16). No noise is modelled.
  - Admitted costs of the proof: κ_n "is very large, except for small values of n and d, and only serves a theoretical purpose" (p. 11). Lemma 3 shows that threshold pivoting yields an adequate set for linear models, but it is "of little theoretical use" because threshold pivoting does not in general *certify* adequacy (p. 12). The framework is "admittedly simplistic" compared with the practical codes of Powell and Conn–Toint (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: state the assumptions as a short list chosen for readability, then say which constants exist only for the theory (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The skeleton runs geometry → error bound → algorithm properties → standard trust-region lemmas, adapted in three places.
  - **Theorem 1** (p. 10): with pivot threshold θ, every NFP is bounded on Q_k(δ) by some n(θ). The proof is by induction over pivots: normalization divides by at most θ, and the block update adds at most M²/θ. The bound can blow up as θ → 0, which motivates Theorem 2 (p. 10).
  - **Theorem 2** (pp. 10–11) is an existence-by-construction theorem. Choose each new point as the argmax of the current NFP over the ball (3.11). Each normalized polynomial then has max ≤ 1, and each later update at most doubles it. So every NFP is ≤ 2^{|Y[d]|} on the ball (3.13). Taking y₁^[0] = x_k keeps the iterate in Y (p. 11).
  - **Lemma 3** (pp. 11–12): for linear models the pivots of this construction are at least δ, so any θ < δ works.
  - **Theorem 4** (pp. 12–13), the key lemma: adequacy gives |f(x) − m_k(x)| ≤ κ_em max[δ², δ³] on the ball and ‖∇f(x_k) − g_k‖ ≤ κ_eg max[δ, δ²].
    - The value bound comes from the Sauer–Xu path-sum bound (3.8) with d = 1, 2. Every factor in it is bounded by adequacy and by ‖y_i − y_j‖ ≤ 2δ.
    - The gradient bound comes from subtracting the two Taylor expansions (3.19) and choosing the test direction h = δ(∇f − g_k)/‖∇f − g_k‖.
  - **Lemma 5** (pp. 16–17):
    - (i) ρ_k ≥ η₁ implies ρ̂_k ≥ ρ_k, so the iteration is successful.
    - (ii) Every radius reduction happens with adequate geometry.
    - (iii) The Step 1 loop is finite unless ∇f(x_k) = 0. Proof: shrink the certification radius geometrically by ω ∈ (0, 1). An infinite loop gives ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≤ (1/ω + κ_eg) ωⁱ ε_g for all i, hence ∇f(x_k) = 0.
    - (iv) Only finitely many bad iterations occur before a radius cut.
  - **Lemma 6** (p. 18): the classical Cauchy decrease, which is not re-proved (Moré 1983).
  - **Lemma 7** (pp. 18–19): if ‖g_k‖ ≥ κ_g, the radius stays above a constant. Take the first k at which it would fall below; then k ∈ R, so the geometry is adequate. Theorem 4 then forces |ρ_k − 1| ≤ 1 − η₁, which contradicts the reduction.
  - **Theorem 8** (p. 19): finitely many successes imply the iterates end at a critical point.
  - **Theorem 9** (pp. 19–20): liminf ‖g_k‖ = 0, by summing the guaranteed decreases against A.2.
  - **Lemma 10** (p. 20) is the bridge from the model gradient to the true gradient. Once ‖g_k‖ ≤ ε_g, Step 1 certifies the model in a ball of radius ≤ μ‖g_k‖. So ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≤ (1 + κ_eg)‖g_k‖, up to constants garbled in the file.
  - **Theorem 11** (pp. 20–21): liminf ‖∇f(x_k)‖ = 0, with the caveat that the critical limit may be at infinity (p. 21).
  - **Theorem 12** (pp. 21–22): every limit point is critical. The argument brackets subsequences t_i < ℓ_i and bounds ‖x_{t_i} − x_{ℓ_i}‖ by the summed radii, which in turn are bounded by the function decrease.
  - The paper supplies road signs, for example "The intuition behind this result is that…" before Theorem 9 (p. 19) and a plain-language reading of Lemma 5 (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique (proof device): prove finiteness of a model-certification loop by shrinking the certification radius geometrically. A non-terminating loop squeezes the true gradient to zero through the model error bound (Lemma 5(iii), pp. 16–17).
  - Transferable technique (proof device): turn a model-gradient liminf into a true-gradient liminf by certifying the model in a ball proportional to ‖g_k‖ whenever ‖g_k‖ is small (Lemma 10, p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: specify a subroutine, here geometry improvement, only through the property the proof uses: finitely many improvements reach adequacy, and x_k stays in Y. Point to practical procedures elsewhere (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The conclusion says that "much additional work remains" to find which members of the class perform best (p. 22). Numbers are delegated to Conn–Toint 1996 (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: Section 2 is a history of Powell's derivative-free work, and it is used as the paper's genealogy (pp. 4–5):
  - conjugate directions (1964; VA04, VA24; Brent's PRAXIS);
  - nonlinear equations and least squares (1965; the 1970 hybrid method and NS01), credited with "promoting … the idea of a modeling the nonlinear function of interest within a trust-region";
  - multivariate interpolation (COBYLA 1994a; quadratic models 1994b, 1996), called "the main source of our inspiration" (p. 5).

  Footnotes record that PRAXIS and COBYLA have CUTE interfaces (pp. 4–5). The mathematical debts are to Sauer–Xu, Sauer, De Boor–Ron and Bernstein bases (pp. 7, 9); Sauer and De Boor are thanked for comments (p. 22). ### S020 · A derivative free optimization algorithm in practice (7th AIAA/USAF/NASA/ISSMO Symposium on Multidisciplinary Analysis and Optimization 1998, DOI 10.2514/6.1998-4718)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K. Scheinberg (now at IBM T.J. Watson), Ph. L. Toint (Namur); Conn at IBM T.J. Watson (pp. 1–2) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the "in practice" member of the Conn–Toint 1996 → CST 1997 ×2 → CST 1998 chain. It cites the convergence paper [5] (S013), the survey [6] (S009) and the prototype [7] (S019) as the algorithm's sources (p. 3). It was presented at an MDO venue next to Boeing's surrogate work (Booker [2]; Booker, Dennis, Frank, Serafini, Torczon, Trosset [3]) (pp. 3, 11). COIN-OR is not mentioned (pp. 1–12). **Read**: full text including Tables 1–3, Figures 2–3 (axes only) and the references (pp. 1–12).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is engineering use. The problem is min f(x) over x ∈ F, with f assumed C² but "in the application noise is typically present", derivatives unavailable and every evaluation expensive (p. 2). The goal is to use as few function evaluations as possible and "any function value as soon as it becomes available" (p. 2). Typical problems have fewer than 100 variables (p. 2). The applications:
  - a Boeing helicopter rotor-blade vibration problem with 11, 31 or about 50 variables, where one simplified simulation takes about a minute, so one finite-difference gradient for 31 variables takes over half an hour (pp. 2–3);
  - an engine-nozzle design with about 10 parameters and hours per evaluation (p. 3);
  - water-distribution network calibration by a University of Colorado at Denver group, with up to 100 variables and about 20 s per evaluation (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: open with the price of one evaluation and of one finite-difference gradient for a named application. That arithmetic justifies DFO before any algorithm is shown (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The algorithm is a quadratic-interpolation trust-region method, stated in words (Steps 0–6, pp. 3–4). It uses two kinds of sample points: points that should lower f (the trust-region step) and points that should improve the model (geometry). Both are found by minimizing a quadratic over the trust region; for geometry points the quadratic is an NFP (p. 5).
  - The trust region uses the ∞-norm (p. 4).
  - The radius decreases only if the ratio is bad *and* the model is at least fully linear, because a smaller radius does not in general give a more accurate model when the model is less than linear (pp. 4–5).
  - The iterate is always the best sample point (Step 6, p. 4).
  - Distant points are dropped to keep the set local (pp. 4–5).
  - The method can start with 2 points and uses 1–2 evaluations per iteration (p. 5).
  - Underdetermined models are not unique. Options include linear terms for less important variables and separable models when separability is known. "Currently, the best general strategy" is the model whose quadratic part has the smallest Frobenius norm (p. 6).
  - **Constraints** are split into three kinds by what is known about them (pp. 3, 5–8):
    - *easy* constraints (cheap values and derivatives, e.g. bounds, or the helicopter's linear mass bound) go into the subproblem, min over B_k ∩ F, so every evaluated point is feasible (p. 6). Equality constraints reduce the dimension of the interpolation space, e.g. 6 points instead of 15 in an affine plane of R⁴ (pp. 6–7);
    - *difficult* constraints (expensive, no derivatives) are left to future work (p. 7);
    - *virtual* constraints (the only information is whether an evaluation fails) get a heuristic. If the trial point fails, temporarily shrink the radius and re-solve. For geometry points, first try other candidates, then shrink (p. 8). The heuristic's flaw is admitted and drawn (Figure 1, pp. 7–8).
  - Transferable technique: classify constraints by the information available (analytic and cheap; simulated and expensive; only pass/fail), and give each class its own treatment (pp. 3, 5–8).
  - Transferable technique: handle a failed evaluation as a trust-region event. Shrink the radius temporarily and retry before concluding that the point is locally optimal (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is C², although noise is expected (p. 2), and all constraints are defined by smooth functions even when little is known about them (p. 5). Virtual constraints are *assumed* to be describable by smooth functions (p. 8). Global convergence is claimed "under reasonable assumptions", with the reader sent to [5], and the text states that convergence needs the model to be only fully linear (p. 5). This is a simplification: S013 requires the stronger *adequate* geometry, so the practical radius rule tests less than the theory does (compare S013 p. 11 with S020 p. 4). The noise claim rests on an argument: noise matters only once the radius is small relative to the noise level, which "typically happens in the proximity of an approximate solution" (pp. 5–6).
- **D4 Analysis**: No proofs. The paper's analytic arguments are qualitative:
  - poisedness, with the example that 6 points on a line in R² almost never support quadratic interpolation (p. 4);
  - why a fully linear model is the minimum needed to make progress (p. 5);
  - why difference-based methods suffer from noise of unknown level (p. 6);
  - why equality constraints lower the interpolation count (pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Tier 1**: CUTE unconstrained problems (26 problems, 2–16 variables, Table 1), Hock–Schittkowski problems with inequalities only (13, Table 2) and with equalities (16, Table 3) (pp. 9–10). The selection rule is not stated. All HS constraints were treated as easy (p. 10).
  - **Baselines**: PDS (direct search), LANCELOT with finite-difference gradients, and COBYLA (linear-interpolation DFO, in single precision) (p. 8). NPSOL solves the DFO subproblems (p. 8). PDS handles no constraints and COBYLA no equalities, so their columns drop out of Tables 2–3 (p. 10).
  - **Cost unit**: function evaluations plus the final value. "We do not list the CPU time, since it is not relevant in our context" (p. 10). Early terminations are starred (p. 10).
  - **Tolerances**: DFO stops at 10⁻⁴, the others at "appropriate comparable default values" (p. 10).
  - **My tally of Tables 1–3** (the paper does not summarize them):
    - DFO needs fewer evaluations than PDS on all 26 unconstrained problems and fewer than COBYLA on 22. COBYLA hits its 8000-evaluation cap on 10.
    - LANCELOT with finite differences needs *fewer* evaluations than DFO on 16 of 26, e.g. MOREBV 33 vs 476 and VARDIM 165 vs 2061. It often reaches much lower final values (p. 9).
    - On constrained problems DFO needs the fewest evaluations on 12 of 13 (exception HS38, bounds only) and on 15 of 16 (exception HS48) (pp. 9–10).
  - **Tier 2**: the helicopter problem. Ten runs each on the 11- and 31-variable instances start from the same point, with a random second point. The runs end in different local minima, and "most of them were better than the solutions previously obtained by other means" (p. 10). 15–20% of evaluations fail on the virtual constraint and are counted in the evaluation totals (p. 10). Good solutions come after about 60 evaluations and runs stop after about 200 (p. 11). For the 31-variable instance, one full quadratic model would need 528 points, but a "very good solution" came after about 180 evaluations (p. 5).
  - The comparison with other methods on the helicopter problem is deferred to [3] (p. 11).
  - There are no noisy test instances, although robustness to noise is claimed (pp. 5–6).
  - Transferable technique: when the runs of a multimodal industrial problem differ, report repeated runs that vary only one random ingredient (here the second initial point), and plot all runs (pp. 10–11).
- **D6 Positioning**: The algorithm is placed in the authors' own lineage ([5], [6], [7]) and credited as "inspired by" Powell's 1994 and 1996 talks [12], [13] (p. 3). Rivals are compared, not criticized: PDS and COBYLA are run as delivered, and LANCELOT with finite differences stands for the differencing school (p. 8). The Boeing group's surrogate framework [3] is cited only as the source of comparisons on the helicopter problem (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: This is an engineering-audience text. The algorithm is given in prose steps with no formulas beyond the ratio and the subproblem (pp. 3–4), with informal phrasing such as "Intuitively it can be said…" (p. 5). - **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are algorithmic and practical: a documented DFO code, constraint extensions for easy and virtual constraints, and evidence on CUTE, HS and helicopter problems (pp. 2–3, 8–11). Limits stated by the authors: difficult constraints are out of scope (pp. 3, 7); the virtual-constraint heuristic can stop at a non-minimizer (p. 8); convergence is only local, although a good local minimum is often found in practice (p. 5). ### S027 · Gradient-based optimization of custom circuits using a static-timing formulation (Proceedings of the 36th annual ACM/IEEE Design Automation Conference 1999, DOI 10.1145/309847.309979)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: I. M. Elfadel, W. W. Molzen Jr., P. R. O'Brien, P. N. Strenski, C. Visweswariah, C. B. Whan (IBM T.J. Watson Research Center and IBM Electronic Design Automation, Austin) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the first EinsTuner paper. It is the static-timing successor of the dynamic tuner JiffyTune ([3], IEEE TCAD 1998) and of "Circuit optimization via adjoint Lagrangians" ([2], ICCAD 1997) (pp. 1, 8). It uses LANCELOT [9, 13, 14] and the two-step update of Conn–Vicente–Visweswariah [15] (S056, card in s2-06) (p. 5). It cites the noise-constraints paper [19] (S039, card in s2-06) for future work (p. 7). The acknowledgements say EinsTuner "benefited greatly from the JiffyTune dynamic tuner development" (p. 7). **Read**: full text, §1–§9, Tables 1–2 and the references (pp. 1–8).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is transistor (and wire) sizing of custom high-performance digital circuits (p. 1). Two known approaches each fail in one way (p. 1):
  - *dynamic tuning* needs user-specified input patterns and optimizes only the measurements they exercise, which puts a heavy burden on the user to pose the tuning problem correctly;
  - *static tuning* (TILOS and the exact convex method of Sapatnekar et al.) is fast, but it rests on RC gate models and crude Elmore or Penfield–Rubinstein delays, "making them unsuitable for custom, high-performance design".

  The entry is to keep static timing's path-independence and to replace the crude delay model with transistor-level time-domain simulation, "an ideal framework for path-independent optimization" (p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: frame the contribution as the intersection of two existing methods' strengths, and name the one defect of each that the intersection removes (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Write static timing, a max-plus propagation of arrival times, as a *smooth* nonlinear program (pp. 1–2):
  - make every arrival time a variable;
  - replace each AT_j = max_i[AT_i + d_ij(w)] by the inequalities AT_j ≥ AT_i + d_ij(w);
  - minimize an auxiliary z ≥ each output arrival time (problems (1) → (2)).

  The result has no derivative discontinuities if the d_ij are C¹. At the solution the constraints along critical paths are tight (p. 2). The full model (pp. 2–3):
  - per node: rise and fall arrival times and rise and fall slews;
  - per channel-connected component (CCC): the design variables w_n and w_p;
  - per propagate segment: delay and slew inequalities (3);
  - the slew inequalities are chosen because they are conservative and, "unlike other choices", guarantee continuity (p. 3);
  - objectives: z, area, or a weighted sum, with the weight varied to trace a trade-off curve (p. 3).

  **Solver side** (pp. 4–5): LANCELOT with modifications.
  - Group partial separability is passed via SIF, "a key enabler" (p. 5).
  - The slack variables and z enter linearly, so they enter the augmented-Lagrangian merit function at most quadratically. After each step an analytic second step updates them (two-step, [15]) (p. 5).
  - The optimizer is made aggressive: a large initial radius and revised radius rules (p. 5).
  - When a CCC "fails" to switch, the simulator returns a code, and the optimizer skips the iteration, cuts the radius and retries (p. 5).
  - A stopping rule detects steps at which the data change is dominated by noise (p. 5).

  **Formulation engineering** (p. 4):
  - careful units and scaling;
  - slews bounded to a physical range;
  - a quick "mock timing" run gives lower bounds on arrival times, and the start is a weighted combination that is deliberately infeasible ("swift and aggressive optimization");
  - fanout capacitances are internal variables;
  - multipliers start at 1/n, because the KKT conditions of min z s.t. z ≥ c_i make the multipliers sum to −1;
  - inequalities become equalities where possible, e.g. for 1-input gates.
  - Transferable technique: remove a nonsmooth max from a model by promoting the intermediate quantities to variables and replacing each max with one-sided inequalities. The composite objective becomes a smooth NLP that a standard solver accepts (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: initialize the multipliers from what the KKT conditions force at any solution. For an epigraph min-max, they sum to −1, so start each at 1/n (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The d_ij are continuously differentiable in w (p. 2). Fanout capacitance is lumped and linear, as in most static timers (p. 3). The signal is a saturated ramp (p. 4). Each gate is a parameterized library cell, with all NFETs, and all PFETs, ratioed to one parameter (pp. 5–6). Circuits are combinational only (p. 6). Global optimality is not claimed: convexity holds under the simplified TILOS models, but "To the extent our more accurate transient-simulation-based modeling possibly destroys convexity", only a local improvement is sought (p. 2). SPECS is 70× faster than the SPICE-like AS/X at 5% relative stage-timing accuracy, with path delays predicted more accurately (p. 4). The authors stress that "any simulation-based data is inherently noisy" (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: say which property the simplified model had (convexity) and which the accurate model may lose, and scale the claim to match (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No theorems. The support for the formulation is a *validation experiment*. An earlier prototype with analytic delay equations (exact gradients, no noise) "consistently" converged to arbitrarily small gradient and constraint tolerances with default settings, which the authors take as validation of the formulation (p. 5). For gradients: one adjoint analysis gives the sensitivity of one measurement with respect to any number of parameters at a small incremental cost. "Group adjoints" give linear combinations of measurements, and chain rules pass through the diffusion capacitances (p. 4). About 15 million time-domain gradients were computed for c2670 (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: validate a new formulation on a noise-free analytic surrogate with exact gradients before plugging in the noisy simulator. Convergence there separates formulation bugs from noise effects (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: Fourteen combinational circuits (Table 1, p. 5), in two tiers.
  - **Tier 1**: benchmarks synthesized from ISCAS-85 into restricted library cells. Protocol (pp. 5–6): size them with a simple gain-based heuristic (gain factor 4.0), then minimize critical delay at the same area.
  - **Tier 2**: four actual microprocessor designs (adder, ifti, incrmtr, ioperdf), tuned from their real sizes at constant area and input loading. "These designs had already been well-tuned" (p. 6). The text groups the two artificial problems (inv3, a3 3) with the real designs, meaning they too start from given sizes rather than heuristic ones (pp. 5–6), although Table 2 still prints a "Heur." delay for them (p. 7).
  - **Problem sizes**: the largest, c2670, has 2,796 transistors, 5,643 variables and 5,665 constraints (pp. 5–6).
  - **Metrics** (Table 2, p. 7): delay before and after with % improvement, worst and average infeasibility of arrival-time and slew constraints, iterations, projected gradient at start and end, and CPU seconds.
  - **Results**:
    - Delay improvements range from 0.92% (incrmtr, a well-tuned real design) to 40.1% (a3 3) (p. 7).
    - Every constraint is satisfied to within 1.5 ps, and no run needs more than 160 iterations (p. 6).
    - adder ran for over three days, 284,445 s (pp. 6–7).
  - **Profiling** on smaller problems: about 20% of CPU in simulation and 80% in the optimizer. On c2670 there were 0.25 million CCC simulations, 15 million gradients and almost half a million CG iterations (p. 6).
  - **Head-to-head baseline**: the authors built their own TILOS-like tuner that uses the same accurate CCC simulation. It gives 7% vs 11% on the 22-gate macro iqia (not in the tables) and 12% vs 16% on ioperdf (p. 6).
  - **Data oddities in the file**: the ifti row of Table 2 repeats the c8 row exactly (584 transistors, 712.3 → 569.1 ps), although Table 1 gives ifti 880 transistors. ioperdf has 2,236 transistors in Table 2 against 2,360 in Table 1 (pp. 5, 7).   - Transferable technique: when you compare with a heuristic rival, re-implement it on *your* accurate evaluation engine, so that the comparison isolates the optimization method from the model accuracy (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself between dynamic tuning (DELIGHT.SPICE [1]; the authors' own [2], [3]) and static convex tuning (TILOS [4], Sapatnekar et al. [7]) (p. 1). It credits RITUAL [8] for a "very similar" formulation of the timing constraints (p. 2). The rival school is criticized on model accuracy, not on speed (p. 1). - **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The formulation is shown first on a three-gate example (§2, pp. 1–2) and then in full detail (§3, pp. 2–3). Design choices are listed as bullets with one reason each (pp. 2–4). An opinion is marked as one: "In our opinion, failure recovery is a necessary ingredient of efficient circuit tuning" (p. 5). There is an internal tension. p. 5 argues that "simulation and gradient computation are expensive compared to the optimization algorithms", which justifies effort in the optimizer, yet profiling on p. 6 finds 80% of the CPU in the optimizer.
