### S029 · A projection method for l_p norm location-allocation problems (Mathematical Programming 1994, DOI 10.1007/bf01581151)
**Read**: abstract. **Coauthors**: I. Bongartz, P. H. Calamai.
- **D1**: Location–allocation with an l_p distance, 1 < p < ∞. The {0,1} allocation constraints are relaxed, and locations and allocations are solved for simultaneously (abstract).
- **D2**:
  - Necessary and sufficient local-minimum conditions for the relaxed problem drive the algorithm, which finds stationary points on subspaces defined by the current activities (abstract).
  - The direction is projected. It carries second-order information for the locations and first-order information for the allocations (abstract).
- **D8**:
  - Under mild conditions the algorithm finds local minima with {0,1} allocations and converges quadratically (abstract).
  - A structure-exploiting implementation greatly reduces the cost of the linear algebra, and results are given for 36 test problems (abstract).
- **Quote**: "The descent direction is a projection onto the current subspace of a direction incorporating second-order information for the locations, and first-order information for the allocations." (abstract)

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅: problem structure is exploited in the linear algebra (abstract).
- Method 1 ✅: optimality conditions, a rate, an implementation and 36 test problems appear in one paper (abstract). Whether the test set is public was not read.
- Existing candidate **Split by type, give each block its natural treatment** ✅: second-order information for the locations, first-order for the allocations (abstract).
- Existing candidate **Characterisation-driven algorithm design** ✅: the local-minimum conditions are "used to develop" the algorithm (abstract).

**Transferable technique**: after relaxing binary assignment variables, prove that the algorithm's local minima are integral (here {0,1} allocations under mild conditions). The relaxation then needs no rounding step (abstract).

### S077 · Circuit optimization via adjoint Lagrangians (Proc. IEEE ICCAD 1997, DOI 10.1109/iccad.1997.643532)
**Read**: abstract. **Authors** (Crossref): Conn, Haring, Visweswariah, Chai Wah Wu, pp. 281–288. Scholar lists only `CW Wu`.
- **D1**: Circuit tuning is best done with gradient-based NLP, and for large circuits computing gradients is the bottleneck. The direct method suits many measurements and few parameters; the adjoint method suits the reverse. Each must be repeated (abstract).
- **D2**:
  - The gradient of the augmented-Lagrangian merit function is written as the gradient of a weighted sum of circuit measurements. The weights come from the nominal solution and from optimizer quantities such as multipliers.
  - With suitably chosen excitations of the adjoint circuit, one adjoint analysis gives every gradient the merit function needs.
  - This requires close integration of optimizer and simulator (abstract).
- **D8**: Implemented in JiffyTune. Gradient computation ran more than 35× faster, and the whole optimization up to 2.5× faster. A 6,900-transistor circuit was tuned in under two CPU hours, which makes large circuits feasible (abstract).
- **Quotes**:
  - "By suitably choosing the excitations of the adjoint circuit, the gradients of the merit function are computed via a single adjoint analysis, irrespective of the number of measurements and the number of parameters of the optimization." (abstract)
  - "This procedure requires close integration between the nonlinear optimization software and the circuit simulation program." (abstract)

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅: exact gradients are pulled out of the simulator by adjoints. There is no black box (abstract).
- Method 6 ✅: the augmented-Lagrangian merit function from the LANCELOT line is reused to cut the cost of gradients (abstract).
- Method 4 ✅: the method is built into JiffyTune, and what it buys is feasibility for large circuits (abstract).
- Existing candidate **Differentiate the merit function, not the constraints** ✅: this abstract is the earliest statement in the collection. See S039, s2-06.

**Transferable technique**: if the optimizer needs only ∇ of a scalar merit function, write that function as a multiplier-weighted sum of the simulator's outputs. One adjoint solve then gives its gradient, instead of one solve per output or per parameter (abstract).
