### S049 · Performance of a multifrontal scheme for partially separable optimization (Advances in Optimization and Numerical Analysis 1994, DOI 10.1007/978-94-015-8330-5_6)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould (Harwell), M. Lescrenier (Belgian National Fund for Scientific Research, FUNDP Namur), Ph. L. Toint (FUNDP Namur) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the report dates from January 1989 (p. 2). It sits between the CGT 1988 bound-constrained pair and LANCELOT (1992). - **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry is a **default component, questioned by swapping in an alternative**. To minimise the trust-region model in partially separable bound-constrained problems, "the iterative method of conjugate gradients has usually been used". The paper's aim is to compare a direct multifrontal scheme against CG with and without preconditioning (abstract, p. 2).
  - The reasons for the CG default are stated fairly before it is challenged (p. 7). A truncated CG that asymptotically takes the exact quasi-Newton step saves work in early iterations. Only matrix–vector products are needed, and in element form they need no assembled Hessian ((27), p. 7).
  - An OCR fragment shows some earlier authors ("they"; the antecedent is lost), in a passage about computing directions of negative curvature, conceding that Levenberg–Marquardt, "or an attempt to solve the trust region problem by another method", may be more appropriate (p. 3). Who they are cannot be read.
  - Problem class: min f(x) subject to simple bounds, where f = Σ fᵢ is partially separable, i.e. the element Hessians have low rank relative to n (pp. 3–4). The formalism is attributed to Griewank and Toint [8], and methods using it are said to have been very successful at large scale [9] (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **What is held fixed.** The outer algorithm is the CGT [3] trust-region class (p. 5):
    - an ∞-norm trust region, chosen so that it is aligned with the bounds (p. 5);
    - a generalized Cauchy point on the projected steepest-descent path (p. 6);
    - acceptance of the GCP if the reduced model gradient there is small enough by test (25). Otherwise the variables active at the GCP are fixed, and a better model minimiser is sought in the free subspace (p. 6).
    - Hessians use partitioned updating: element gradients and Hessians are stored in internal variables yᵢ = Uᵢx, and the full Hessian "is never explicitly assembled" (pp. 4–5). The products with Uᵢ are delegated to a user subroutine (p. 5).
  - **What is varied (the experiment's two factors).**
    - The inner solver:
      - (i) CG or diagonally preconditioned CG. It stops on a small restricted gradient, at the first bound hit, or on negative curvature, where it moves to the bound along that direction (p. 7).
      - (ii) A multifrontal direct method: Duff–Reid MA27 with Bunch–Parlett 1×1/2×2 pivots, used for symmetric indefinite systems (p. 8).
    - The Hessian source: exact, BFGS, or SR1 element updates (p. 10).
  - **Why the multifrontal method.** A partially separable Hessian (13) has the same form as a finite-element matrix (29), which is what frontal methods (Irons 1970 [10]) were built for (p. 8).
    - Caveat in practice: MA27 wants compact storage, not the element form, so "one had to assemble the Hessian" (p. 9).
  - **Handling the three inertia cases from one LDLᵀ factorisation** (32) of the restricted Hessian (p. 9):
    - Positive definite: solve the Newton equations (33) (p. 9).
    - Indefinite: take the eigenvector v of the most negative eigenvalue λ of the block-diagonal D. The direction is z = L⁻ᵀv, at the cost of one backward substitution, with curvature λ‖v‖² ((34)–(35), OCR-damaged) (p. 9).
    - Repair found by experiment: when indefiniteness persists, successive directions "often lie in the same subspace" and convergence is unacceptably slow. So the code **cycles through the negative eigenvalues** of D across iterations (p. 9).
    - Singular positive semidefinite: the Conn–Gould 1984 strategy [2], which solves (33) if it is consistent and otherwise finds a descent direction of zero curvature (36) (p. 9).
    - The step is then truncated to the bounds (37) (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Simple bounds only; element Hessians of low rank (p. 3); the user supplies the Uᵢ products (p. 5).
  - The paper states no convergence theorem of its own. Global convergence is inherited implicitly through membership of the CGT [3] class (p. 5).
  - The only stated condition is for a superlinear rate (p. 7):
    - the ratio of the restricted model gradient at the final point to that at the GCP tends to zero;
    - the Hessian approximations restricted to the variables active at the solution satisfy the Dennis–Moré condition.
    - The η(k) of test (25) is named as a suitable choice for the first condition.
  - Initial element Hessians are set to the identity. This is judged satisfactory because the test problems are reasonably well scaled, as far as the truncated sentence can be read; the rest of it is lost (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no theorems or proofs. The analytical content is the linear-algebra derivation of the negative-curvature direction ((32)–(35), p. 9) and the rate remark above (p. 7). Anomalies in the tables are explained by mechanism rather than by proof (pp. 14–15, 19):
  - **SR1 quadratic termination.** On problem 22 with the multifrontal solver, BFGS solves 19 linear systems and SR1 only 1. Every element Hessian is a constant 3×3 diagonal, so SR1 recovers it exactly in 3 steps. Those steps passed test (25), so one more iteration and one solve finish the job (pp. 14–15).
  - **BFGS losing positive definiteness in floating point.** On problem 36 some element Hessians become so ill-conditioned (the OCR reads "1017!", presumably 10¹⁷) that BFGS yields an indefinite system. The fix is a reset to the identity, which the authors call a "quick fix". They explain why a principled fix is hard: bad conditioning would have to be detected before negative curvature appears, while the element Hessians stay unfactored (p. 15).
  - **SR1 versus BFGS reversed.** In [4], SR1 often beat BFGS, but "In the context of partial separability, this conclusion does not appear to apply." The conjectured mechanism: projections of successive search directions into the range spaces of certain elements can be close to linearly dependent even when the overall directions are independent, which is harmful for SR1 (p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Problems.**
    - 8 problems from Toint's partially separable test set [15], with its starting points: 10 Rosenbrock, 11 linear minimum surface, 16 boundary value, 17 Broyden tridiagonal, 22 diagonal quadratic, 31 extended ENGVL1, 33 extended Freudenstein, 36 cube (p. 10).
    - 5 new problems (55, 56, 57, 59, 61), published in an appendix "so as to allow for other sparsity structures" (p. 10; pp. 20–21).
    - Problem 59's sparsity pattern is random. It is generated by the Harwell routine FA04BS "starting with the default seed", with a stated rejection rule (p. 21).
    - The set is called "representative of a larger set used by the authors" (p. 10).
  - **Design.** A full 3 × 3 factorial on every problem: {cg, pcg, multif} × {exact, BFGS, SR1} (p. 10).
    - Table 1: 13 problems at n = 100 (pp. 11–14).
    - Tables 2–7: a "fairly representative" subset scaled up to n = 5000 (p. 15). Captions visible: Table 2 = problem 11, Table 3 = problem 16 (p. 16), Table 6 = problem 59, Table 7 = problem 61 (p. 18).
  - **Platform and settings.** CRAY 2 at Harwell; the SBMIN code in Fortran 77; CPU seconds (p. 10).
    - Δ₀ = 0.1‖g₀‖∞ (OCR-damaged); μ = 0.25, η = 0.75, γ₁ = 1/√10, γ₂ = √10.
    - Stop when the projected gradient norm is below 10⁻⁸ (p. 10).
  - **Reported per run.** f calls, g calls, "linear system stats" (CG iterations, or counts of positive definite solves and negative-curvature directions for the direct method), and total CPU (p. 11). A CPU cap is reported as ">1200" (pp. 16–18).
  - **Failure accounting.** "*" marks a stop because the trust region became too small. The authors note that the projected gradient was already small in those cases (exponent garbled in the OCR) and attribute the stop to rounding in the ratio ρ (p. 10).
  - **Selected numbers.**
    - Problem 16, exact Hessian, n = 100: cg 3 f-calls in 0.94 s; multif 3 f-calls in 0.11 s (p. 12).
    - Problem 36, exact Hessian, n = 100: cg 1029 f-calls in 17.81 s; pcg 1023 in 11.74 s; multif 944 in 22.42 s (p. 13).
    - Problem 59 (random sparsity), n = 5000: multif takes 447.67–1070.84 s against 7.10–51.98 s for cg/pcg (Table 6, p. 18).
    - Problem 61, n = 5000: multif hits the cap with BFGS and with SR1 (Table 7, p. 18).
    - The text cites Table 5 as the convex case where the multifrontal method is excellent (p. 19). The second table on p. 17 (caption lost) plausibly shows multif at n = 1000 with about 17–19 f-calls and 9–15 s against 143–673 f-calls and 65–153 s for cg/pcg. That figure is my alignment of scrambled OCR columns and is unverified.
  - **Robustness check.** Runs from different starting points "did not affect the conclusions above". They are stated, not tabulated (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution.** An empirical and algorithmic study, with no theory: when a direct factorisation beats CG as the trust-region subproblem solver. The direct method "can sometimes lead to very significant improvements in terms of computation time", but only when the model is convex or not too often non-convex, and when the Hessian structure is regular enough to avoid high fill-in (p. 19).
    - Summary of the regimes: competitive with exact and BFGS Hessians; CG preferable with SR1 (p. 19).
  - **Limits, stated specifically.**
    - The negative-curvature strategy is "clearly inefficient" (Tables 3 and 6; p. 19).
    - The BFGS reset is a "quick fix" (p. 15).
    - MA27 forced assembly of the Hessian (p. 9).
    - Random sparsity causes fill-in that makes the direct method uncompetitive (Table 6; p. 19).
    - The authors add a reverse lesson: CG's early neglect of negative curvature (it sees only the first few Krylov subspaces) "might be advantageous" (p. 19).
  - **Future.** A code offering "a choice of methods more adapted to the specific problem being solved", to be provided through the Harwell library (p. 20; the preceding sentences are lost).
  - **Positioning (D6).**
    - Builds on Griewank–Toint partial separability [8], [9] and the CG trust-region line of Toint [14], Steihaug [12] and Stoer [13] (pp. 3, 7).
    - Imports Duff–Reid [6], [7], Bunch–Parlett [1] and Irons [10] (p. 8).
    - Self-citations: [2], [3], [4] (p. 21).
    - No rival software is benchmarked; all comparisons are between the authors' own options.
    - Their own earlier SR1 finding [4] is openly qualified (p. 19).
  - **Writing (D7).** Claims are hedged throughout: "seems to be really competitive with the iterative schemes in some cases" (p. 14); "can sometimes lead to" (p. 19). Every anomaly singled out in the text gets a mechanism (pp. 14–15), and the shortcomings are named in the authors' own words (pp. 15, 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): the numerical study sits on a class-level theory. The algorithm is declared a member of the CGT [3] class (p. 5), and the tested options (three inner solvers × three Hessian sources, p. 10) are members of that class. The conclusion promises a code offering a choice of methods through the Harwell library (p. 20). LANCELOT A later offers an MA27 option (S040 pp. 18–19, per the c2-03 card); whether that is the promised code is not stated here.
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant, pre-CUTE): the test set is small and chosen by the authors: 13 problems at n = 100 and a subset scaled to n = 5000, called "representative of a larger set" (pp. 10, 15). Test-bed habits are already visible:
  - new problems are published in full in an appendix (pp. 20–21);
  - the random instance is reproducible from a named generator and seed (p. 21);
  - failures and CPU-cap exhaustion are marked (pp. 10, 16–18).
  - The cost unit is CPU time as well as f- and g-calls. That is right for a linear-algebra question, and it differs from the evaluation-only unit of later DFO work.
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): partial separability is exploited throughout:
  - element storage in internal variables with no assembled Hessian (pp. 4–5);
  - element-wise matrix–vector products for CG ((27), p. 7);
  - the choice of the multifrontal solver itself, because the Hessian has finite-element form (p. 8).
  - The paper also names where exploitation stopped: MA27 needed the assembled Hessian (p. 9).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): frontal and multifrontal elimination is transplanted from finite-element analysis (Irons [10]) and sparse linear algebra (Duff–Reid [6], [7]) into trust-region optimization. Bunch–Parlett pivoting is reused to extract directions of negative curvature (pp. 8–9).
- New-pattern candidate, existing: **anomaly harvested as an explicit conjecture**, as a variant. The SR1-over-BFGS result of [4] fails under partial separability, and a mechanism is conjectured: near linear dependence of element-projected search directions (p. 19). *My note*: this is the same property that S010 (1991) later assumes away as uniformly linearly independent steps (AS.4, per the c01 digest).
- New-pattern candidate, existing: **factorial study of own variants before external comparison**. Here the design is a full 3 × 3 factorial of the authors' own options on every problem, with no external comparison at all (p. 10).
- New-pattern candidate, new: **win/lose regime conclusion**. The conclusion states the conditions under which the new option wins (convex model, regular sparsity) and loses (negative curvature, fill-in from random sparsity), each tied to a table (p. 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To test a component, freeze the outer algorithm and its convergence class. Vary only the component, crossed with the other main option (here the Hessian source). Report the component's own work (CG iterations, or factorisations by inertia) beside f-calls, g-calls and CPU (pp. 10–11).
- Add test problems built to stress the property under study, such as other sparsity structures, including a random one. Publish them in an appendix with starting points, and name the generator and seed of the random one (pp. 10, 20–21).
- When a factorisation-based step meets repeated indefiniteness, do not always take the most negative eigenvalue of D. Cycle through the negative eigenvalues, so that successive negative-curvature directions do not stay in one subspace (p. 9).
- In a results table, separate numerical-precision stops from genuine failures with a distinct marker, and say why (here, rounding in the trust-region ratio when the projected gradient is already small) (p. 10).
- For every anomalous table entry, either give a mechanism (SR1's quadratic termination on constant element Hessians, pp. 14–15) or label the repair a quick fix and say why a principled fix is hard (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "We are well aware that this technique is merely a “quick fix”, and that a more sophisticate procedure is desirable." (p. 15)
- "The proposed strategy seems to be clearly inefficient and other strategies must definitely be found to handle such cases, if direct methods are to be used." (p. 19)
