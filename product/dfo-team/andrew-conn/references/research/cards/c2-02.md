### S005 · A globally convergent augmented Lagrangian algorithm for optimization with general constraints and simple bounds (SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis 1991, DOI 10.1137/0728030)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The CGT trio's Waterloo–Harwell–Namur period. Conn was at Waterloo (p. 1). The paper was received in October 1988 and revised in April 1990 (p. 1). It is the outer loop placed around the authors' own bound-constrained trust-region method (CGT 1988a/b, p. 2) and is the theory behind the augmented-Lagrangian layer of LANCELOT. Software and numbers are promised, not shown (p. 4). Two direct descendants: S022 (linear constraints handled separately, announced here on p. 4) and S007 (Lagrangian barrier; card in c02). Annick Sartenaer, later a coauthor of S022, is thanked for a careful reading (p. 27). **Read**: full text §1–§8 and the reference list. The algorithm boxes (pp. 7–8) are garbled; the tolerance rules below are read from the legible parts plus their use in the proof of Theorem 5.3 (pp. 18–19).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap in scale. The authors' principal interest is large-scale problems. Apart from a few exceptions (Murtagh–Saunders, Lasdon, Drud), there has been little progress on algorithms for such problems, although many applications are waiting (p. 2). Unconstrained optimization had been rescued in the preceding decade by three ideas (p. 2):
  1. exploit structure (sparsity, low rank, partitioned updates);
  2. solve the Newton equations only crudely early on (inexact Newton, truncated CG);
  3. use trust regions to handle negative curvature, since second derivatives are commonly available.

  Constraints add a combinatorial obstacle: active-set methods change the active set slowly, which is costly for large problems, whereas for simple bounds rapid changes are easy (p. 2). SQP is set aside for large problems because solving a QP to completion at every iteration is judged too expensive, by analogy with solving the Newton equations exactly (p. 3). The problem is min f(x) subject to c_i(x) = 0 and l ≤ x ≤ u. Inequalities are converted to equalities with slack variables, so that the combinatorial side lives only in the bounds (p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: list the ideas that made the easier problem class scalable, then design the method for the harder class so that each idea can be plugged in unchanged (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Keep the bounds out of the augmented Lagrangian Φ(x, λ, S, μ) = f + Σ λ_i c_i + (1/2μ) Σ s_ii c_i² (1.4), with diagonal constraint scaling S. Minimize Φ only approximately over the box, using the authors' bound-constrained algorithm (pp. 1–2). The novelty claimed is approximate subproblem solution plus efficient handling of bounds (pp. 2–3). There are two algorithmic models (pp. 7–9):
  - **Inner iteration**: any x^(k) in the box whose projected gradient satisfies ‖P(x^(k), ∇_xΦ)‖ ≤ ω^(k) (3.1). Here P(x, v) = x − P[x − v] (2.2) and P[·] is the projection onto the box (pp. 4, 7).
  - **Switching test** ‖c(x^(k))‖ ≤ η^(k) (3.2). If it holds, update the multipliers with the first-order estimate λ̄ = λ + S c(x)/μ (2.5), keep μ, and tighten ω and η. Otherwise reduce μ by a factor τ < 1 and reset ω and η as powers of the new penalty parameter (pp. 7, 18–19).
  - **Algorithm 2** accepts any multiplier estimate: first-order, least-squares (2.14), or those in Tapia's survey. It adds tighter control so that the estimates cannot grow too fast (pp. 8–9).

  The rationale takes one paragraph (p. 8). Driving μ to zero is the last resort; the iteration then becomes the quadratic penalty method, whose convergence is known (Gould 1989). The feasibility test is set at the size one would expect if the multiplier iteration were converging, so that the multiplier iteration can take over. Kept from classical AL is Powell's 1969 device for keeping the penalty parameter away from zero (p. 3). Changed: inexact subproblems with explicit tolerances, bounds handled inside the subproblem, and no assumption that the multipliers stay bounded (pp. 3, 11). Typical parameter values for well-scaled problems are suggested (p. 9; mostly illegible in the file).
  - Transferable technique: pair a brute-force mechanism that is sure to converge (penalty → 0) with a fast one (multiplier updates), and switch between them with a test calibrated to the progress the fast mechanism should make (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: specify the subproblem solver only through a stopping test on a computable criticality measure. Every solver that passes the test is then covered, including solvers that use scaled tests (8.6) (pp. 7, 27).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The assumptions are stated where they are needed:
  - global: AS1 f, c_i ∈ C² (p. 1); AS2 iterates in a closed, bounded domain; AS3 full rank of the Jacobian columns of the floating variables at limit points. AS3 is shown to be equivalent to linear independence of the constraint gradients and active bounds, and is noted as commonly assumed (p. 9);
  - local: AS4 Lipschitz second derivatives; AS5 nonsingularity of the reduced KKT matrix for every index set J made of J1 plus *any* subset of the dual-degenerate set J2 (p. 13). AS5 is related to second-order sufficiency (Fletcher, Luenberger, Gould 1985) and defended because small perturbations can move degenerate indices between J1 and J2, so it insures against every case (p. 13);
  - later: AS6 strict complementarity (p. 20) and AS7 uniform positive definiteness of the augmented Lagrangian Hessian on the floating variables (p. 25), each added only for the theorem that needs it.

  Simplifications admitted for exposition or proof: lower bounds 0 and upper bounds ∞ until §8 (p. 4); zero final tolerances ω*, η* in the analysis (p. 9); deliberately rigid tolerance-update rules (p. 27). The bounded-penalty theorem needs the *whole* sequence to converge to a single limit point (Theorem 5.3, p. 17), and §6 shows that this cannot be dropped (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: when you cannot know which degenerate indices will end up active, assume the nondegeneracy condition for every combination, say plainly that it is insurance, and then split the sequence into finitely many subsequences with a fixed pattern (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The skeleton runs global convergence (§4), then error bounds and bounded penalty (§5), then a necessity example (§6), then second order (§7).
  - **Floating/dominated split** (p. 5). A variable is dominated when the projected gradient equals the variable itself, and floating otherwise. Lemma 2.1: if the projected gradient → 0, dominated variables go to their bounds and the floating components of ∇Φ go to 0 (p. 6).
  - **Least-squares multipliers as an analytical device**: λ(x) in (2.14) needs x*, so it is not computable (p. 6). It is used in the proofs, and after active-set identification it becomes computable (5.51) (p. 20).
  - **Lemma 4.1** (Algorithm 1): if μ → 0, then μ‖λ‖ → 0. Between two penalty reductions the multipliers grow by sums of S c/μ. The feasibility test bounds these sums, and the accumulated growth obeys a linear recursion (4.9), bounded separately in three cases according to whether a parameter ratio is < 1, > 1 or = 1 (4.10)–(4.13) (pp. 9–10; the ratio itself is garbled in the file). A remark says it suffices that the summed violations are bounded by a multiple of a positive power of μ (p. 10). Lemma 4.2 does the same for Algorithm 2 using the acceptance test (pp. 10–11).
  - **Lemma 4.3**, the general convergence lemma (pp. 11–12). It is in the spirit of Bertsekas's Proposition 2.3, but it drops bounded multipliers and adds the bounds. It gives error bounds on the multiplier estimates in terms of ω^(k) and ‖x − x*‖ (4.18)–(4.19) and a bound on ‖c‖ (4.20). If c(x*) = 0, the limit is a KKT point. Devices: a uniformly bounded generalized inverse, and the integral mean-value theorem with the derivative of λ(x) from Lemma 2.2.
  - **Theorem 4.4** (pp. 12–13) has two cases. If μ stays bounded away from zero, the feasibility test eventually always passes and η → 0 forces c → 0. If μ → 0, Lemma 4.1/4.2 plus (4.20) forces c → 0. The theorem holds for any tolerance sequences that go to zero (p. 13).
  - **Lemma 5.1** (pp. 13–17) removes the dependence on ‖x − x*‖: the errors in x and λ̄ are bounded by a constant times (ω + μ‖λ − λ*‖). Devices:
    - split into at most 2^{|I5|} subsequences, each with a fixed floating/dominated pattern for the degenerate variables (p. 14);
    - Taylor remainders with quadratic bounds (5.13), (5.16) (p. 15);
    - a reduced KKT system (5.19), inverted with norm M, where the quadratic terms are absorbed once k is large (5.28)–(5.32) (pp. 16–17).

    A one-sentence road sign gives the plan before the estimates (p. 16).
  - **Theorem 5.3**: μ is bounded away from zero (pp. 17–19). The proof is by contradiction plus induction. Assume the penalty is reduced infinitely often. First show the multipliers converge. Then start from the first iterate after a reduction at which μ is small enough, and show by induction that the feasibility test passes at every later iteration (5.45)–(5.49). So there are only finitely many reductions. Exponent conditions (5.35)–(5.36) are required (p. 17).
  - **Theorem 5.4** (active-set identification): under AS6, the floating variables are eventually exactly those away from their bounds (p. 20). **Theorem 5.5**: R-linear convergence, with an R-factor given by a power of the final penalty parameter (pp. 20–21).
  - **§6 counterexample** (pp. 21–25): a one-variable problem with constraint x² − 1 = 0 and two stationary points x = ±1 (6.1)–(6.3). An admissible cycle of j + 1 iterations is built: j iterates near one point pass the feasibility test, then one iterate beyond the other point fails it. The cycle repeats, so μ → 0 when there are two limit points (pp. 21–24). A cycle of length j + 2 gives the same for Algorithm 2 (pp. 24–25).
  - **Theorem 7.1**: AS7 gives an isolated local solution; a semidefinite version gives second-order necessary conditions (pp. 25–26).
  - Transferable technique: to prove a parameter stays bounded away from zero, assume it is reduced infinitely often, then show by induction from a well-chosen iterate that the acceptance test must pass forever (pp. 17–19).
  - Transferable technique: to show a hypothesis is necessary, construct an admissible iterate cycle between two KKT points with different multipliers that satisfies every test of the algorithm (pp. 21–25).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The authors say they have deliberately left out numerical testing because building appropriate software is not trivial and they want to do it thoroughly; results will follow (p. 4). The choice of constraint scalings is deferred until significant numerical testing has been done (pp. 26–27).
- **D6 Positioning**: The lineage runs from Hestenes and Powell (1969), with credit for Powell's device (p. 3). Prior globally convergent AL methods are compared by their assumptions (p. 3):
  - Powell: exact minimization and a unique solution;
  - Rockafellar, Bertsekas, Polak–Tits: inexact minimization but bounded multipliers;
  - Hager: a particular update and inner method, and a convergent multiplier subsequence;
  - Yamashita and Bartholomew-Biggs: QP subproblems. Yamashita needs bounded QP multipliers; for Bartholomew-Biggs, convergence is only hinted at.

  SQP methods with an AL merit function (Schittkowski; Gill et al.) are classed as not pure AL methods, because they search in x and λ jointly (p. 3). The authors agree with Bertsekas that AL methods suit large problems (p. 3). The results are placed against Bertsekas's Propositions 2.3, 2.4 and 2.14: the claimed advance is removing bounded multipliers and adding bounds (pp. 11, 13). The inner solver is their own (CGT 1988a/b) (p. 2). No one is dismissed; each predecessor is restated by its assumptions.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract names exactly two results: global convergence, and a penalty parameter bounded away from zero (p. 1). A roadmap follows (p. 4). The algorithm boxes are followed by a plain rationale paragraph (p. 8) and by suggested parameter values (p. 9). Each assumption is followed by its justification (AS3, p. 9; AS5, p. 13; AS6, p. 20). Remarks after the proofs say how much weaker the hypotheses could be (pp. 10, 13). The long estimate in Lemma 5.1 has a road-sign sentence (p. 16). The negative result gets its own section (§6). The paper ends by declining to publish a more general update rule, because its conditions would be complicated and unlikely to be more practical (p. 27). Hedging is mild and first-person, e.g. that it is not clear to the authors how to build an efficient truncated SQP (p. 3). About 20 of the 28 pages are statements and proofs.
  - Transferable technique: when a proof's opening estimates are long, add one sentence stating the order the right-hand side will be shown to have before grinding through it (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory for an algorithm class meant to become software:
  - global convergence without bounded multipliers (Thm 4.4);
  - bounded penalty (Thm 5.3);
  - active-set identification (Thm 5.4);
  - R-linear rate (Thm 5.5);
  - second-order limit points (Thm 7.1);
  - a necessity example (§6).

  The limits are concrete: no numbers (p. 4); the scalings S are left open (pp. 26–27); the tolerance rules are rigid (p. 27); a single limit point is needed (§6). Future directions: numerical experience (p. 4), and treating linear constraints differently from nonlinear ones, which the authors say they are already pursuing (p. 4) and which became S022.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the theory leg is published alone, and code and numbers are explicitly deferred (pp. 4, 27). The triad exists only across papers, spread over years. Step 1 (write the algorithm as a framework) ✅: the inner solver and the multiplier formula are free (pp. 7–9), and Theorem 4.4 holds for any tolerances that go to zero (p. 13).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: bounds are handled exactly and kept outside the penalty term, and slacks put all the combinatorics into bounds (p. 1). Large-scale structure (sparsity, and the Hessian of the AL compared with that of the Lagrangian) drives the design (pp. 2, 4).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the three large-scale unconstrained ideas and the authors' own bound-constrained trust-region code are transplanted into a constrained outer loop, reusing Powell's 1969 device (pp. 2–3).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (by analogy): what is certified is inner-solve accuracy (projected gradient ≤ ω) and feasibility (≤ η). The theorem needs only this interface (pp. 7, 13). There is no model and no geometry.
- New-pattern candidate: **show an assumption is needed by a constructed counterexample** (§6, pp. 21–25). This is the original example that S022 invokes (S022 p. 24) and that S007 mirrors (c02).
- New-pattern candidate: **insurance assumption over all combinatorial branches**: AS5 is required for every subset of the degenerate indices, and the proof enumerates at most 2^{|I5|} subsequences (pp. 13–14). It recurs in S022 (AS6, p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: **split by type** (from s01): bounds versus general constraints (p. 1); announced for linear versus nonlinear constraints (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Put the sure-to-converge fallback (penalty → 0) behind a test calibrated to the fast mechanism's expected progress, so the fallback is rarely used (p. 8).
- [D2] Make every tolerance a power of the current penalty parameter and reset the tolerances when the penalty drops. This is what lets the proof show that the fast regime eventually always wins (pp. 7, 18–19).
- [D3] Assume nondegeneracy for every subset of the degenerate index set and call it insurance. Then reduce the proof to finitely many subsequences with a fixed pattern (pp. 13–14).
- [D4] Use an uncomputable quantity (least-squares multipliers at x*) as an analytical device, then show it becomes computable once active-set identification holds (pp. 6, 20).
- [D4] Bounded-parameter proof: by contradiction plus induction from the first iterate after a late reduction (pp. 17–19).
- [D4] Show necessity with an admissible iterate cycle between two KKT points with different multipliers (pp. 21–25).

**Quotes**:
- "the construction of appropriate software is by no means trivial and we wish to make a thorough job of it." (p. 4)
- "As we do not know which might defect under such perturbations, (AS5) is a form of "insurance" against all possible eventualities." (p. 13)

### S019 · An algorithm using quadratic interpolation for unconstrained derivative free optimization (Nonlinear optimization and applications 1996, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4899-0289-4_3)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: Conn was at IBM T. J. Watson and Toint at Namur. The text is Namur Report 95/6 (12 September 1995), which was to appear in the Di Pillo–Giannessi volume (p. 1). It is Conn's first DFO algorithm paper. It follows Powell's 1994 quadratic-interpolation proposal (p. 4) and precedes the CST 1997 survey (S009, card in c02). S009's text reuses this paper's geometry-improvement passage almost word for word, e.g. the sentence crediting Powell (1994b) for the Lagrange-function identity (S009 file p. 11, found by grep; S009 was not re-read for this card). **Read**: full text, both tables and the reference list.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a class of applications: objective values produced by measuring a real-world quantity, e.g. the depth of a layer in geophysical exploration (p. 2). Three features define the class: each evaluation is very costly, so linear algebra should be traded for evaluations; no derivatives are available; and there is noise (p. 2). A derivative audit follows (p. 3):
  - finite differences cost extra evaluations and are unreliable under noise unless the step is adapted to the noise level;
  - automatic differentiation cannot apply to a physical measurement.

  For costly simulations with truncation noise (in-flight vibration of a helicopter rotor), AD differentiates the noise, needs the source code, and in fluid dynamics may bring in unwanted dependence on discretization parameters or artificial viscosity (Burns 1995) (p. 3). Formally: min f(x), where f is twice differentiable but its derivatives cannot be computed, and one evaluation costs more than solving dense n × n linear systems (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: before proposing a DFO method, write one sentence for each derivative route (finite differences, AD, source-level differentiation) saying why it fails for your problem class (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The method is a trust-region algorithm with a quadratic model (2.2) fixed by interpolating known function values (2.3), which needs p = (n+1)(n+2)/2 points (2.4). It follows Powell (1994b), but insists on taking long steps and on making progress as early as possible with every available evaluation (p. 4). The design components:
  - **Geometry**: the interpolation set must be poised (Sauer–Xu), i.e. the determinant δ(I) of the basis-evaluation matrix (2.5) is nonzero; six points on a line do not determine a quadratic in two variables (p. 5). Geometry is *good* (Powell's definition) when all points lie within 2Δ of x_c and |δ(I)| cannot be doubled by moving one point within Δ (p. 5).
  - **Explicit model improvement**: shrinking Δ does not improve an interpolation model the way it improves a Taylor model, so geometry must be improved explicitly after unsuccessful iterations (pp. 5–6).
  - **Replacement measure**: replacing x− by x+ multiplies |δ| by |L(x+, x−)|, independent of the basis, a result credited to Powell (1994b) (p. 6). A scaled measure S(x, x−) (2.7)–(2.9) treats points farther than Δ from x_c (p. 6).
  - **Models before p points exist**: the known points I and the not-yet-evaluated points J (with I ∪ J poised) give two variants. Variant 1 takes the minimum-Frobenius-norm model, min ‖g‖² + ‖H‖_F² subject to interpolation (2.29). Variant 2 takes a sub-quadratic Sauer–Xu model (pp. 6–7, 10).
  - **Reuse** of previously evaluated points that lie close to x_c (p. 7).
  - **Algorithm A1**, a geometry improver with six ordered steps (pp. 7–9): (1) swap in past points; (2) replace far points of J; (3) replace far points of I (farther than 2Δ); (4) replace a close point of J if S > 1; (5) replace a close point of I if S ≥ 2; (6) declare the geometry satisfactory.
  - **Step**: the trust-region subproblem is solved exactly (the Newton step if it fits, otherwise Levenberg–Marquardt, (2.20)), because evaluations dominate the cost (p. 9).
  - **Jumps**: if ρ ≥ 0.9, check the ratio of actual to predicted change at past points. Let δ be the largest distance at which that ratio is ≥ 0.85, and solve a larger trust-region problem of that radius. The long step is accepted if its ratio is ≥ 0.05 and it gives a lower value (2.22)–(2.24) (pp. 9–10). The radius is capped at 1000 (2.32) (p. 12).
  - **Radius**: Δ is reduced at every unsuccessful iteration, whether a geometry step or a trust-region step. Waiting until the geometry cannot be improved takes about p iterations (p. 10).
  - **Stopping**: stop when Δ is below a tolerance, or when f(x_c) − f_low is below a tolerance for a user-given lower bound f_low (0 for least squares) (p. 10).
  - **Noise test**: if the predicted reduction is not larger than the absolute and relative noise levels, improve the geometry instead of evaluating the step (2.31) (p. 12; comment 6, p. 15).

  The complete algorithm has Steps 0–7 (pp. 11–13) and is followed by six numbered comments (pp. 13–15).
  - Transferable technique: before spending an evaluation on a long step, check the model on points already evaluated (a history ratio test) to decide how far the model can be trusted (pp. 9–10).
  - Transferable technique: compare the predicted reduction with the user's noise level. If it is not larger, improve the model rather than evaluate the step (p. 12, p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is twice differentiable and its derivatives exist but are unavailable (p. 4). There are no constraints. Bounds are called straightforward, and general constraints could go through an exact penalty (p. 2) or an augmented Lagrangian (pp. 19–20). Noise enters as absolute and relative levels supplied by the user (p. 11). For the algorithm to be well defined, I ∪ J must stay poised (comment 1, p. 13). There is no list of assumptions for convergence, because no convergence result is claimed.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There is no convergence analysis. The one proof shows that the exchanges that *force* a new point into the set ((2.37) and A1 Steps 2–3) keep I ∪ J poised (pp. 13–14):
  1. write the new point's basis vector φ(x_c + s) as a combination of the columns of the interpolation matrix (2.43);
  2. not all coefficients can vanish, because the basis must represent the constant quadratic;
  3. exchanging a point whose coefficient is nonzero multiplies δ by that coefficient (2.44).

  Numerical near-singularity is handled differently by the two variants: variant 2 shrinks the interpolation set, and variant 1 forces geometry improvement (p. 14). A convergence theory covering the final algorithm is named as highly desirable (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: to prove an exchange keeps an interpolation set poised, expand the incoming point's basis vector in the current columns and use the determinant identity for a column replacement (pp. 13–14).
- **D5 Experiments**: The setup:
  - Code and machine: both variants in MATLAB 4.1.1 on an IBM RS6000 (p. 15).
  - Problems: 20 small unconstrained CUTE problems with n = 2 to 4 (p. 15; Table 1, p. 16).
  - Settings: radius and function-value stopping tolerances, and initial radius 1 (3.1) (p. 15). The model is declared ill-conditioned when the Vandermonde condition number exceeds a multiple of 1/ε_M or the smallest pivot is below a threshold (pp. 15–16). The exponent signs of these constants are lost in the file, so their values are not reported. A run fails after 1000 unsuccessful iterations (p. 16).
  - Baseline and cost unit: LANCELOT with finite differences and SR1 (p. 16). Only function evaluations are counted. LANCELOT's evaluations are estimated by a stated formula, 1 + iterations + n(gradient evaluations + 1) (footnote, p. 16).
  - Randomness: because the initial set is random, each entry is the mean of 10 runs, rounded (p. 16).

  Noiseless results (Table 1, p. 16): the authors conclude that the two variants behave alike and are comparable to finite-difference LANCELOT. They seem better on the ill-conditioned or strongly nonlinear problems CLIFF, HAIRY, PFIT1LS and SNAIL (p. 17). My count from Table 1: the min-norm variant uses fewer evaluations than LANCELOT on 7 of 20 problems and the sub-quadratic variant on 8. LANCELOT fails on BROWNDEN (p. 16).

  Noisy results (Table 2, p. 18):
  - Setup: absolute and relative noise of 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻², with the algorithm's noise parameters set to the true level (p. 17).
  - Baseline: a trust-region code assembled from LANCELOT subroutines, with the finite-difference step chosen from the noise level following Dennis–Schnabel (pp. 97–99). The authors call it a reasonable yardstick (p. 17).
  - Reporting: bracketed entries mark practical failures, i.e. runs that met a stopping test but ended far above the optimal value (p. 17).

  The authors' conclusions (p. 18):
  - the variants are comparable, and both beat the finite-difference code;
  - the min-norm variant is less robust (it fails on PFIT1LS at both noise levels);
  - effort falls as noise grows for the new methods, but not always for the finite-difference code;
  - the best value found is often within [f*, f*(1 + ε_r) + ε_a].

  My count from Table 2: at noise 10⁻⁴ the min-norm and sub-quadratic variants use fewer evaluations than the finite-difference code on 8 and 10 of 20 problems, and that code has 3 bracketed practical failures. At 10⁻² the counts are 14 and 16 of 20, with 4 bracketed failures (p. 18). The authors warn that noise can stop the algorithm at any point where the slope is of the order of the noise (p. 17), and that the testing is very limited (p. 18). There is no engineering or application tier.
  - Transferable technique: when a baseline does not report your cost unit, give the conversion formula in a footnote (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: report runs that met a stopping test but ended far from the optimum as practical failures, marked visibly, not as successes (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: run the same problems noiseless and at two noise levels, set the method's noise parameters to the true levels, and give the baseline the same noise information (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: for randomized initial designs, report the mean over a fixed number of runs (10 here) (p. 16).
- **D6 Positioning**: DFO methods are sorted into five classes (pp. 3–4):
  1. finite-difference gradient methods;
  2. pattern search (Nelder–Mead, Torczon, Dennis–Torczon);
  3. random sampling;
  4. line searches along coordinate or conjugate directions (Rosenbrock, Powell 1964, Brent);
  5. model building (Powell 1994a, linear; 1994b, quadratic).

  Design-of-experiments global modelling is mentioned separately (pp. 3–4). The paper places itself in the model-building class, following Powell (1994b), and sets itself apart only by long steps and early progress (p. 4). Powell is credited for the geometry definition and for the Lagrange-function identity (pp. 5–6), and Sauer–Xu for poisedness and sub-quadratic models (pp. 5, 7). The authors' own work supplies the test problems (CUTE), the baseline (LANCELOT) and the noise-versus-predicted-reduction rule (Conn et al. 1993) (pp. 15–16). No competitor is criticized.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper is framed as work in progress: the current state of ideas in what is likely to be a longer-term project (p. 4). The algorithm is presented first in prose with its rationale (pp. 5–10), then formally (pp. 11–13), then through six numbered comments that expose its weak points: well-definedness, initialization and slow termination (pp. 13–15). Results come as numbered tentative conclusions (pp. 17–18), followed by an explicit caution (p. 18). The paper closes with a long bulleted list of open directions (pp. 19–20).   - Transferable technique: after stating an algorithm, add numbered comments that point out its own weak spots (well-definedness, initialization, termination cost) and a possible remedy for each (pp. 13–15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an algorithm, a prototype code and preliminary numbers. The limits are specific:
  - heavy linear algebra per iteration (p. 19);
  - an inefficient mix of Newton interpolation with Lagrange-function point selection (p. 19);

  - no convergence theory (p. 20);
  - very limited testing (pp. 18, 20).

  Future directions (pp. 19–20): models of higher degree; cheaper linear algebra; large problems via partial separability, with a model and a geometry per element; bounds, then general constraints via augmented Lagrangians or exact penalties; a convergence theory; objectives whose evaluation accuracy can be chosen at a price; more numerical experience.

### S022 · Convergence properties of an augmented Lagrangian algorithm for optimization with a combination of general equality and linear constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 1996, DOI 10.1137/s1052623493251463)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. Gould, A. Sartenaer, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: Conn was at IBM T. J. Watson, Gould at RAL, and Sartenaer and Toint at Namur (p. 1). The paper was received in June 1993 and revised in March 1995 (p. 1). It is the sequel to S005 (reference [5] here) that S005 announced (S005 p. 4). LANCELOT [6] is cited as the implementation of the bounds-only approach (p. 2). The flexible-multiplier extensions are proved in technical reports [9] and [10] (p. 26). **Read**: full text §1–§8, including §7 and the reference list. The Step 0 and Step 4 formulas of Algorithm 3.1 are partly illegible (p. 6).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a design shortcut in the authors' own software. LANCELOT keeps only the bounds outside the augmented Lagrangian and turns the other linear inequalities into equations with slacks inside it. The authors say this may be inefficient, because there are effective techniques designed for linear constraints (Arioli et al., Forsgren–Murray, Toint–Tuyttens, Vanderbei–Carpenter), and this matters most for large problems (p. 2). The goal is therefore to keep the linear constraints (1.3) outside the AL and handle them in the subproblem, so that specialized packages can solve it (p. 2). A second practical wish: separate penalty parameters for subsets of constraints with different degrees of nonlinearity (Fletcher, Powell, Bertsekas). This is adopted because it is useful and because its analysis cannot simply be inferred from the single-parameter case (p. 2). The problem is min f(x) subject to c_i(x) = 0 and Ax − b ≥ 0 (p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: take a shortcut in your own released code and make its removal the next theory paper, stating the practical cost of the shortcut first (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The subproblem is min Φ(x, λ_k, μ_k) over B = {x : Ax ≥ b} (3.1), where Φ is the multi-penalty augmented Lagrangian (1.5) (pp. 2, 4). The inner stopping rule is geometric (p. 5):
  - dominant constraints D(x, ω) are those with a_iᵀx − b_i ≤ κ0 ω (3.2);
  - N(x, ω) is the cone of their outward normals and T(x, ω) its polar tangent cone;
  - stop when ‖P_T(−∇Φ)‖ ≤ ω (3.3).

  The rule is built to cover the tests of existing linearly constrained codes (MINOS, LSNNO, Harwell VE14 and VE19) (pp. 5–6; §7.2). Algorithm 3.1 (p. 6):
  - disaggregated updates: for each subset Q_j, update its multipliers if its violation is ≤ η_k (Step 3a); otherwise reduce its own penalty by τ_{k,j} (3.9) (Step 3b);
  - aggregated updates: α_k = max_j μ_{k,j} decides whether ω and η are reset or tightened (3.10)–(3.13).

  A coherence check shows that ω_k and η_k → 0 (3.14). Practical relaxations and the LANCELOT parameter values are listed (p. 7). The update (3.9) balances violations gradually across subsets. This is contrasted with Powell's rule, which uses the ∞-norm and updates multipliers only when all subsets are within tolerance (p. 7). Linear equalities are covered by working in the affine subspace they define. The linear constraints leave the Hessian structure of the AL untouched (pp. 2–3).

  Changes from S005: bounds become general linear inequalities; the box projection becomes a projection onto the tangent cone of the dominant constraints; one penalty becomes q penalties. Kept: the two-regime outer loop, first-order multipliers (2.1), tolerances tied to the penalty, and the whole proof architecture.
  - Transferable technique: define the inner stopping test with ε-active (*dominant*) constraints and a tangent-cone projection, so that it contains the tests existing solvers already perform (pp. 5–6, 26–28).
  - Transferable technique: with per-block parameters, update each block separately, but drive the tolerances by one aggregate (the largest penalty) (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The global assumptions:
  - AS1: B is nonempty (it may be unbounded) (p. 3);
  - AS2: f and c_i are C² (p. 3);
  - AS3: bounded iterates, argued to hold always in practice, because bounds on the variables, actual or implied by finite precision, are among the linear constraints (p. 8);
  - AS4: J(x*)Z* has rank ≥ m, presented as the direct generalization of CGT's AS3 (p. 8).

  The local assumptions:
  - AS5: Lipschitz second derivatives (p. 15);
  - AS6: nonsingularity of the reduced KKT matrix for every choice of weakly active constraints. Like S005's AS5, it is called a safeguard against the effect of small perturbations (p. 15);
  - nondegeneracy (5.2), identified with strict complementarity (p. 15);
  - AS7: second order (p. 24).

  Admitted simplifications: μ0 < 1 (3.6), imposed only to simplify the exposition (p. 7); zero final tolerances (p. 8). The bounded-penalty results need a single limit point, and the authors state that this cannot be relaxed (p. 24).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The architecture copies S005 result by result. Lemma 4.4 matches CGT Lemma 4.3. Theorem 4.6 is analogous to CGT Theorem 4.4 (p. 13). Theorem 5.1 generalizes CGT Theorem 5.4 (p. 14). Lemma 5.3 is an analog of CGT Lemma 5.1 (p. 15). The proof of Theorem 5.5 is inspired by CGT's technique (p. 20). The new devices:
  - **Lemma 4.1** (pp. 8–9): the cone-projection test bounds both the reduced gradient Z*ᵀ∇Φ and its complement. The constant comes from a minimum over the finitely many possible dominant sets.
  - **Lemma 4.3** (pp. 9–10): the limit gradient is a nonnegative combination of the active rows. The device is the criticality measure σ_k = max{−∇Φᵀd : A(x_k + d) ≥ b, ‖d‖ ≤ 1} (4.3). It is shown to vanish using the Moreau decomposition and the continuity of optimal values from convex perturbation theory (Lemma 4.2, Fiacco).
  - **Lemma 4.5** (pp. 12–13): μ_{k,j}‖λ_{k,[Qj]}‖ → 0. When α_k → 0, a counting argument shows that over q successive decreases the largest penalty falls by a fixed factor, because at most q − 1 other penalties can sit in the interval.
  - **Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 5.2** (pp. 14–15): strongly active constraints, those whose removal destroys criticality, are eventually dominant. Under nondegeneracy, the dominant set equals the active set, which amounts to active-set identification.
  - **Lemma 5.3** (pp. 16–19): restrict to subsequences with a constant dominant set (finitely many). Use Taylor expansions, the orthogonal decomposition Y ⊕ W and the reduced KKT system (5.17) with inverse norm M. A road-sign sentence gives the order of the right-hand side (p. 18).
  - **Theorems 5.5 and 5.6**: first the *largest* penalty is shown to stay bounded away from zero, with the same contradiction-plus-induction proof as S005 (pp. 20–22). Then *all* penalties are. The authors mark this second step as the new difficulty: the earlier theorem sufficed for q = 1 (p. 22). Theorem 5.6 is proved by contradiction through a recursion (5.42) on an aggregate quantity for the subsets whose penalty would vanish (pp. 22–23; the definition of that quantity is garbled in the file).
  - **Theorem 5.7** gives R-linear convergence (pp. 23–24). **Theorem 6.1** gives second-order limit points (pp. 24–25).
  - **§7.2** (pp. 26–29) shows that practical tests imply the abstract test (3.3):
    - Lemma 7.1: a condition (7.1) on nonpositive *dominant multipliers* implies (3.3), so solvers that produce approximate multipliers qualify (pp. 26–27);
    - the familiar reduced-gradient criterion also qualifies (pp. 27–28);
    - the test σ_k ≤ ω_k (7.4) gives only global convergence, and the local theory needs σ_k ≤ ω_k² (7.5) (p. 28);
    - Theorem 7.2: (7.5) implies a variant of (3.3) (pp. 28–29).

    Lemma 7.1 and Theorem 7.2 do not depend on the form of the augmented Lagrangian (p. 29).
  - Transferable technique: to certify many solvers at once, prove a short lemma of the form *this family's native stopping test implies my abstract test*, once per family (Lemma 7.1, Theorem 7.2; pp. 26–29).
  - Transferable technique: to extend a one-parameter bounded-penalty proof to q parameters, first prove it for the largest parameter, then run a recursion on the violations of the blocks whose parameter would vanish (pp. 20–23).
  - Transferable technique: when a constant depends on which constraints are near-active, take the extremum over the finitely many possible index sets (pp. 8–9, 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The conclusion states the intention to run extensive numerical experiments on large problems, noting that coupling an efficient subproblem solver to this class needs considerable care and sophistication (p. 29).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues the authors' [5] (S005) and [7] (on inner iterations per outer iteration), and cites LANCELOT [6] for the existing approach (p. 2). Fletcher is credited with the advantages of handling linear constraints separately and of using several penalties; Powell and Bertsekas are credited for several penalties (p. 2). The balancing update is contrasted with Powell's on technical grounds only (p. 7). Tools are borrowed with credit: Moreau's decomposition (p. 9), Fiacco's perturbation result (p. 9), Burke–Moré–Toraldo and Dunn for nondegeneracy (p. 15), and the authors' own 1993 criticality measure [8] (p. 28). Named software whose stopping tests are covered: MINOS, LSNNO, VE14, VE19, IMSL LCONG and NAG E04UCF (pp. 5–6, 27–28).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract lists the design features, then three results: global convergence, fast linear convergence, and penalty parameters bounded away from zero (p. 1). The introduction motivates from practice (p. 2). The algorithm is followed by a coherence check, practical parameter values and a comparison with Powell (p. 7). Each result is tied explicitly to its predecessor in S005, and what is new is marked (pp. 13–15, 20, 22). Hedging is moderate: AS6 is called reasonable in view of perturbations (p. 15), and the stronger stopping condition is judged possibly too strong in practice (p. 28). The conclusion rests the practical claim on one point: the stopping rule covers the tests of available packages (p. 29).
  - Transferable technique: in a generalization paper, tie each lemma and theorem to its counterpart in the predecessor by number, and spend the prose on the one place where the generalization is genuinely harder (pp. 13–15, 22).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory for a class that extends S005 to general linear inequality constraints and several penalty parameters. It also certifies existing linearly constrained codes as inner solvers, and adds active-set identification and all-penalties-bounded results. The limits are concrete:
  - no numbers (p. 29);
  - no local theory under the weaker test σ_k ≤ ω_k (p. 28);
  - a single limit point is necessary (p. 24);
  - the flexible-multiplier proofs are only in technical reports (p. 26).

  Future directions: large-scale experiments (p. 29), and use with other merit functions, since §7's results do not depend on the AL form (p. 29).

Method 3 looks like the DFO specialization of an older habit: prove the theorem about an abstract test, then make codes satisfy it.
