### S010 · Convergence of quasi-Newton matrices generated by the symmetric rank one update (Mathematical Programming 1991, DOI 10.1007/bf01594934)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The CGT trio's Waterloo–Harwell–Namur period (affiliations p. 2–3). It is the theoretical sequel to the pair CGT 1988 *SINUM* ([1], convergence of a class of trust-region methods) and CGT 1988 *Math. Comp.* ([2], testing), and it explains a numerical surprise found in [2] (p. 5–6). It is a quasi-Newton paper: gradients are available, only the Hessian is approximated. **Read**: full text, including the appendix (FA01 random-number recurrence, p. 21). The reference list was read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an anomaly in the authors' own testing paper. In [2], SR1 inside a trust region beat BFGS, DFP and PSB, which questioned the "traditional supremacy" of BFGS (p. 5). [2] had suggested that this might be linked to SR1 matrices converging better to the true Hessian, and this paper sets out to clarify that suggestion (p. 6). A second motivation is given for studying indefinite updates at all (p. 5):
  - far from the solution the Hessian need not be positive definite;
  - with bounds or general constraints it may be indefinite even at the solution;
  - positive definiteness is incompatible in practice with symmetry, the secant equation and sparsity (Sorensen [14]), which matters for large-scale problems.

  Trust regions are what make indefinite approximations usable (p. 5). The problem is stated as: give conditions under which the SR1 matrices B_k converge to ∇²f(x*) for a general smooth nonquadratic f, and relate the rate to that of {x_k} (abstract p. 2–3; roadmap p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: take the most surprising table in your own previous experimental paper and ask which property of the method would explain it. Then prove that property.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key insight is that SR1's exact-Hessian property on quadratics (n linearly independent steps give B_{n+1} = H, whatever method produced the steps; Fiacco–McCormick, p. 8) survives approximately for nonquadratic f. The secant error on an old step grows at most geometrically with the number of later updates, times the spread of the iterates (Lemma 1, p. 9–10). The algorithmic model (Algorithm 1, p. 6) is deliberately broad, and each freedom is justified in a numbered comment (p. 6–7):
  1. the step s_k is left unspecified, so it can come from a line search, a trust region with truncated CG, or partitioned updating for partially separable functions [9];
  2. there is no stopping rule, since only the asymptotics matter;
  3. SR1 is applied only when |r_kᵀs_k| ≥ c1‖r_k‖‖s_k‖ (17), and otherwise B_{k+1} = B_k;
  4. the same update formula is used at every iteration.

  Kept from the classical theory: the secant equation (3) and the SR1 formula (10). Removed: the line search and every positive-definiteness requirement (p. 12).
  - Transferable technique: state the algorithm with the step left free, so one theorem covers line-search, trust-region and structured variants (p. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: (AS.1) f is C²; (AS.2) the Hessian is Lipschitz with constant c2; (AS.3) x_k → x*; (AS.4) the steps are uniformly linearly independent: within every window of m ≥ n iterations there are n normalized steps whose matrix S_k has σ_min(S_k) ≥ c3 (p. 7). A variant (AS.4b) only asks that the last n normalized steps form a nonsingular S*_k (p. 12). AS.4 is justified with data from [2]: in those runs the smallest singular value of n successive normalized steps was always above 10⁻⁴ (p. 8). The authors then call AS.4 "relatively strong" and name two ways it fails (p. 8):
  - with α_k = 1 the SR1 denominator can become tiny, B_{k+1} becomes nearly g_{k+1}g_{k+1}ᵀ/(g_{k+1}ᵀs_k), and later steps become nearly orthogonal to g_{k+1} and hence dependent (21)–(22);
  - in partially separable problems whose minimum is found at different iterations in independent subspaces, some step components become small and dependent.

  An analysis without AS.4 is declared desirable but "likely to be very difficult" (p. 8). Notably, x* need not be a stationary point (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: justify a strong assumption by measuring it in your own earlier runs, then list the concrete scenarios in which it fails (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: A short direct argument (p. 9–13). Its devices:
  - **Lemma 1** (p. 9–10): ‖y_j − B_{j+1}s_j‖ = 0 and ‖y_j − B_i s_j‖ ≤ (c2/c1)(2/c1 + 1)^{i−j−2} η_{i,j}‖s_j‖, where η_{i,j} is the diameter of the iterates between j and i (26). The proof is an induction on i. The mean-value theorem gives y_l = H̄_l s_l with the averaged Hessian H̄_l = ∫₀¹ H(x_l + t s_l) dt (28)–(29); AS.2 turns H̄_k − H̄_j into the iterate spread (30); the skip rule (17) bounds the SR1 denominator (31).
  - **Theorem 2** (p. 10–11): ‖B_{k+m+1} − H(x*)‖ ≤ c5 ε_k, where ε_k is the largest distance to x* over the window (35), and B_k → H(x*). Two ingredients: the secant pairs are close to the true Hessian, ‖y_j − H(x*)s_j‖ ≤ c2 ε_k‖s_j‖ (39), and B is close to the secant pairs by Lemma 1 (40). The triangle inequality then gives a small error along each of n directions (41). The step from directions to the whole matrix is ‖A‖ ≤ (1/c3)‖A S_k‖ (42). The constant c5 is explicit and carries a √n (43).
  - **Corollary 3** (p. 11–12): if ‖x_{k+1} − x*‖ ≤ c6‖x_k − x*‖, then ‖B_{k+m+1} − H(x*)‖ ≤ c7‖x_k − x*‖. Faster iterates give faster matrices (p. 12).
  - **Corollary 4** (p. 12): under AS.4b the bound is c8 κ(S*_k) ε_k. It involves the condition number of the last n normalized steps, which is a quantity one can compute from a run. The authors say this bound is "probably best related" to practice (p. 12).
  - Honest remarks after the theorems (p. 12–13): the constants "can be very gross" because they depend on c1 and c2; skipped updates only delay convergence unless they happen infinitely often; and convergence without stationarity means that negative curvature at x* will eventually appear in B_k and push a trust-region method away from x*.
  - Transferable technique: when an estimate holds along n well-spread directions, convert it into a matrix-norm bound with ‖A‖ ≤ ‖A S‖/σ_min(S) (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: write the secant difference y_l as H̄_l s_l with the averaged Hessian, so a Lipschitz assumption turns every Hessian difference into a distance between iterates (p. 10–11).
- **D5 Experiments**: §4 has a stated double purpose: verify the error estimate for SR1, and compare SR1 with BFGS, DFP and PSB (p. 13). The setup:
  - Machine: FORTRAN 77 on the Harwell CRAY X-MP in extended precision (ε_M ≈ 10⁻²⁹), chosen so the asymptotic behaviour can be seen before rounding interferes (p. 13).
  - Test functions: quartics (52) with H = R D Rᵀ built from a Householder reflector R, where the eigenvalues in D are spaced between 1 and 2^{−ν}, so ν sets the conditioning. The minimizer is the origin (p. 13–14).
  - Reproducibility: the coefficients come from the Harwell routine FA01 with stated seeds, and its recurrence is printed in an appendix "To allow reproducibility" (p. 13, p. 21).
  - Three kinds of iterate sequence, all from x₀ = (1, …, 1) with B₀ = I (p. 14): (i) artificial sequences with a prescribed linear, superlinear or quadratic rate, random directions on the unit sphere and a descent-acceptance test (p. 14–15); (ii) the trust-region algorithm of [2] (p. 17–18); (iii) a bisection line search with sufficient decrease (61), restricted to SR1 vs BFGS because BFGS is at home there (p. 18–19).
  - Metric: the entrywise error E (59), printed beside ε_k and κ(S*_k) so it can be compared with Corollary 4 (p. 15–16).

  Results:
  - Corollary 4 is confirmed with c8 "not much larger than 0.1" (p. 15).
  - BFGS and DFP degrade from n = 3 to n = 10. PSB degrades with conditioning, which is expected because it is not scale-invariant (p. 15).
  - Iterations needed to reach 10⁻⁶ fall from 70 to 10 for SR1 as the rate improves, while BFGS and DFP often fail within the run (Table 3, p. 18).
  - Inside the trust region, SR1 reaches errors of 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹³ against about 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴ for the others. The cost unit is ng, the number of gradient evaluations, and SR1 needs fewer than BFGS in 4 of 5 cases (Tables 4–5, p. 18).
  - In the line search, SR1 is still far more accurate than BFGS and needs fewer gradient evaluations (Tables 6–7, p. 19).

  Caveats reported by the authors: random directions favour SR1 because they make the AS.4 failure unlikely (p. 15); the superlinear and quadratic runs use only n = 3, because the n-iteration delay in Corollary 4 must be short relative to the rate (p. 17); the skip tests never fired (p. 14). Only n = 3 and 10 are used. Extraction and source issues: the file shows c1 as "1012", presumably 10⁻¹² since c1 ∈ (0, 1) (p. 14); the text on p. 17 cites the iteration counts as Table 4, but the table is captioned Table 3 (p. 18).
  - Transferable technique: isolate a component from the optimizer. Feed the update synthetic iterate sequences with prescribed convergence rates, and only then confirm on sequences produced by real trust-region and line-search codes (p. 14–19).
  - Transferable technique: print the theory's predicted quantity (here ε_k and κ(S*_k)) as columns next to the observed error, so the reader can estimate the hidden constant (Tables 1–2, 5, 7; p. 15–19).
  - Transferable technique: use a test generator with one conditioning knob (H = R D Rᵀ, eigenvalues 1 to 2^{−ν}) and publish the random-number recurrence (p. 13–14, p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues the authors' own [1] and [2] (p. 5). §2 surveys the earlier results on quasi-Newton matrix convergence with care (p. 8–9):
  - Fiacco–McCormick: SR1 recovers the Hessian of a quadratic in n steps (p. 8).
  - Powell 1970: PSB matrices converge under uniform linear independence, via the least-change Frobenius property. The paper adds that the same Powell paper was also seminal for trust regions (p. 8).
  - Ge–Powell: DFP and BFGS matrices converge, but not necessarily to the Hessian. Their proofs are "quite involved" and depend on the choice of steps, but they do not need an AS.4-type assumption, a strength the authors credit (p. 9).
  - Schuller: a BFGS rate bound (23) under assumptions called "quite strong", including an asymptotically exact line search, which is unrealistic when there is no line search at all (p. 9).

  The claim is made relative to Schuller: the result is similar, and although it holds only for SR1, its assumptions are "substantially weaker" (p. 12). Prior work is assessed through its assumptions, not dismissed. Powell is thanked for comments on a first version (p. 20).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution is framed modestly as clarifying a suggestion from [2] (p. 6). A roadmap follows at once (p. 6). The algorithm is given in its broadest form, then justified by numbered comments (p. 6–7). Assumptions come with their evidence and with their failure modes (p. 8). Each result is followed by a plain-language interpretation, including an exclamation mark for the rate corollary (p. 12), and then by candid remarks about loose constants (p. 12). Hedging is moderate and attributed ("in the authors’ opinion", p. 5; "the authors believe", p. 8). The conclusion limits the explanatory claim: it "may not completely explain" the trust-region SR1 vs line-search BFGS gap in [2], but "throws some light" on it (p. 20). Theory takes about 4 pages (p. 9–13) and numerics about 6 (p. 13–19).
  - Transferable technique: after each theorem, add one sentence saying what it means for practice and one saying how loose it is (p. 11–13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical: the quadratic-case SR1 result is extended to all sufficiently smooth functions, with a rate tied to the rate of the iterates, and the numbers confirm it (p. 20). The limits are specific:
  - AS.4 is strong, with named failure modes (p. 8);
  - the bounds are gross (p. 12);
  - the paper only partly explains the performance gap it set out to explain (p. 20).

  The future direction is an analysis without AS.4 (p. 8). Not stated by the authors: the experiments use only n ≤ 10 and quartic test functions (p. 13–17).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the triad runs in reverse here. Numerics came first (CGT 1988 *Math. Comp.*, [2]) and produced an unexplained result; the theorem follows; new experiments then check the theorem's predicted constant (p. 5–6, p. 15). It fits the paired-publications pattern of Method 1, but the direction is test → theory.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence for step 1 (write the algorithm as a framework): the step is left unspecified so that the theorem covers line-search, trust-region and partitioned-update methods (p. 6–7). This also matches Research Taste mark 2 (theory covers a class).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pre-DFO precursor): the accuracy of a model (here the Hessian approximation) is tied to a geometric condition on the sample directions, σ_min(S_k) ≥ c3 or κ(S*_k), and Corollary 4 makes the error proportional to the conditioning of that geometry (p. 7, p. 12). This is the same logical shape as poisedness in the later DFO work. The difference is that geometry is only *assumed* here: the algorithm has no improvement step to enforce it, and the paper names the scenarios where it fails (p. 8).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (partial): cost is counted in gradient evaluations (ng) when codes are compared (p. 18–19); the generator is reproducible, with its recurrence published (p. 21); results are reported for every update and every sequence type, including failures within budget (Table 3, p. 18).
- Method 5 ✅ weak evidence: the paper is motivated by large-scale structure. Positive definiteness conflicts with sparsity (p. 5), and partitioned updating for partially separable functions is one reason the step is left free (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *anomaly-driven theory*: turn an unexplained numerical result from your own testing paper into a theorem, and say plainly how much of the anomaly it explains (p. 5–6, p. 20).
- New-pattern candidate: *experiments aimed at the theorem's predicted quantity, not only at the algorithm*: the tables print the quantities of the bound so the constant can be read off (p. 15–19).
- New-pattern candidate: *assumption calibrated on your own run data, with failure modes listed* (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Mine your previous experimental paper for a result you cannot explain and make its explanation the next paper (p. 5–6).
- [D3] Support a strong assumption with a statistic measured in your own earlier runs (here σ_min > 10⁻⁴), then give the concrete mechanisms that break it (p. 8).
- [D4] Induction on the number of updates since a secant pair was created, with a geometric growth factor (2/c1 + 1) that the safeguard rule controls (Lemma 1, p. 9–10).
- [D4] Use ‖A‖ ≤ ‖A S‖/σ_min(S) to lift error bounds along n directions to a full matrix bound (p. 11).
- [D4] State a corollary whose bound is computable from a run (κ(S*_k)ε_k), so the experiments can test it (p. 12, p. 15).
- [D5] Drive the component under study with synthetic sequences of prescribed rate before using real optimizer trajectories (p. 14–19).
- [D5] Build test problems with a single conditioning parameter and publish the random-number recurrence (p. 13–14, p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "Somewhat surprisingly, the traditional supremacy of the BFGS update is questioned by these numerical experiments" (p. 5)
- "Thus we obtain the quite interesting result that, the faster the convergence of {xk} to x∗, the faster the convergence of {Bk} to H(x∗)!" (p. 12)

### S004 · CUTE: Constrained and unconstrained testing environment (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 1995, DOI 10.1145/200979.201043)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: I. Bongartz, N. I. M. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: Conn at IBM T. J. Watson (p. 2). A spin-off of the LANCELOT project: the facilities were first built and tested with LANCELOT (p. 3), and the problems are written in LANCELOT's standard input format, SIF (p. 2–3). It is the ancestor of CUTEr and CUTEst (not discussed in the paper). **Read**: full text. Appendix A (illustrative SIF file, p. 27–28) and Appendix C (a select session, p. 35–39) were read. Appendix B (argument lists of the evaluation tools, p. 28–34) was skimmed. The reference list was read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is the developer's own testing burden. Anyone building a major package must obtain, specify and manage a comprehensive problem collection and wants to compare algorithmic approaches; "All of these situations occurred during our own researches" (p. 3). Two gaps make it hard (p. 3–4):
  - there is no consensus on an ideal interface between problems and algorithms;
  - most software was not written for SIF.

  The problem is posed as the sequence of four questions a user would ask (p. 3): (1) what is available for my class of problems; (2) how do I add my own problem and classify it; (3) which solvers already have interfaces, and how do I use them; (4) how do I build an interface for my own solver.
  - Transferable technique: pose an infrastructure paper as the ordered list of questions a new user will ask, and answer them in that order (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: Decouple problems from solvers. One input format (SIF) is read by a decoder; evaluation tools return values and derivatives in the formats a solver wants; classification metadata and database tools make the collection searchable; thin shell-script interfaces connect external solvers. The components:
  - **Collection**: 738 problems on 15 December 1994, with more than a thousand further instances suggested through size parameters (p. 4). It absorbs the existing sets rather than competing with them: the Argonne set (Moré et al. 1981), Buckley, Hock–Schittkowski, Dembo, Moré–Toraldo, Boggs–Tolle, Toint–Tuyttens, Gould's QPs, PSPMIN, Minpack-2 and Schittkowski's second collection, plus original application problems (p. 4). About a third have constraints beyond bounds (p. 5). Tables 1–2 list application problems with n up to 15 625 and m up to 10 000 (p. 6–7). The SIF extends the MPS format, so linear programming test sets are also reachable (p. 4–5).
  - **Classification** string `XXXr-XX-n-m`, inspired by Hock–Schittkowski and Bus (p. 5, p. 8–9). It encodes the objective type (N, C, L, Q, S = sum of squares, O), the constraint type (U, X, B, N = network, L, Q, O), regularity (R/I), the degree of analytic derivatives supplied (0/1/2), the origin (A academic, M modelling exercise, R real application), explicit internal variables (Y/N), and n and m (V when user-chosen). Its scope is "consciously limited" (p. 5). Extensions wait for user feedback and must stay compatible (p. 9).
  - **Tools**: select queries a .DB classification file interactively (p. 9–10); classify and classall build and maintain it (p. 12–13).
  - **SIF decoder**: it assumes group partial separability (3.1)–(3.2), which covers any function with a continuous sparse Hessian (Griewank–Toint) (p. 11). It generates Fortran subroutines plus a data file (p. 11–12).
  - **Interfaces**: COBYLA, MINOS, NPSOL, OSL, TENMIN, UNCMIN, VA15, VE09, VE14 and VF13, plus MATLAB (p. 14–15). All use the same sdgen/gen script pattern, which doubles as the template for new interfaces (p. 17–18). Table 4 gives one small check problem per package to verify the installation (p. 18).
  - **Evaluation tools**: unconstrained (U…) and constrained (C…) families, which "gather" the partially separable data into f, ∇f and the Hessian of the objective or Lagrangian. Hessians come dense, in coordinate form or in finite-element form, with Hessian-vector products, band extraction and name queries (p. 20–23, Tables 5–6).
  - **Distribution**: ANSI Fortran 77 in single and double precision, automated installation on six platform families, and ftp from RAL and Namur (p. 24–25).
  - **Growth mechanism**: the LANCELOT licence requires most users to submit typical problems (p. 4).

  Kept: SIF and the decoder from LANCELOT. Changed: the collection and tools are separated from LANCELOT so that other solvers can use them. CUTE ships no external solver code; users obtain the solvers from their authors (p. 4, p. 22–24).
  - Transferable technique: ship one problem-evaluation API with several derivative formats and a template driver, so that adding a solver means writing a thin wrapper (p. 17–23).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The two problem classes are bound-constrained optimization (6.4)–(6.5), and general equality and range constraints plus bounds (6.6)–(6.9). f and c_i are assumed smooth and group partially separable, and each constraint uses a single group (p. 11, p. 21–22). Irregular problems are admitted in the collection and flagged I (p. 8). The Lagrangian L = f + λᵀc is the object of the constrained Hessian tools (p. 22). The authors acknowledge a UNIX and C-shell bias in the presentation (p. 2). There is no noise model, and nothing DFO-specific beyond the COBYLA interface and the derivative-degree flag (p. 8, p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: none. This is a software and infrastructure paper. The only mathematical content is the definition of group partial separability and the statement of its generality (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: none in the paper. There is one illustrative UNCMIN run on MOREBV (n = 10), whose output reports function, gradient and Hessian evaluation counts (13, 13, 2), the final f and gradient norm, and timings (p. 16–17). Solver results are deferred to the LANCELOT numerical-experiments report and to a technical report with "nearly twenty thousand separate runs" (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself "much in the same spirit as" Buckley (1992) (p. 3), inherits the Hock–Schittkowski/Bus classification (p. 5) and absorbs the earlier collections by name (p. 4). No earlier collection or rival solver is criticized. Competing solvers are described neutrally, with where to obtain them (p. 14–15). The one comparative remark favours the authors' own code: LANCELOT exploits much more structure than the interface tools provide (p. 16). Nocedal and a referee are thanked (p. 25–26).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Manual-like prose with little hedging. The sections follow the user's four questions (p. 3; §2–§6). Worked terminal sessions with real output are included (p. 12–13, p. 16–17, p. 35–39). The authors are candid about scope: the UNIX bias (p. 2), the limited classification (p. 5, p. 9), and the fact that interfaces have to be general because no consensus exists (p. 3). The conclusion is a bullet list of six purposes followed by an appeal to use the environment and report experiences (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is at the software and infrastructure level: a test collection, a classification scheme, database tools, evaluation tools and solver interfaces (p. 25). The limits are stated concretely:
  - the classification leaves out finer constraint subdivision, structural considerations and solution characteristics, which are planned (p. 9);
  - there is no agreed problem–algorithm interface (p. 3);
  - external solvers must be obtained separately under their own licences (p. 4, p. 14–15).

  The stated future is growth of the collection through licensed users and through user feedback (p. 4, p. 25). Not stated by the authors: there is no performance-reporting methodology (profiles or similar) and no noisy problem variants anywhere in the paper.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ strong evidence (primary practice source). The test bed is published as a research product in ACM TOMS. It is public (ftp, p. 25), classified for reproducible subset selection (p. 8–10), extensible (p. 10–13) and solver-agnostic (p. 14–23). Interfaces to rival-school codes are included, among them Powell's derivative-free COBYLA and the SQP codes NPSOL and VF13 (p. 14–15). This supports step 4 (baselines from competing schools).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the noisy-variants part of Method 2 is absent from CUTE, which has no noise model (p. 8, p. 21–22). The second tier of real problems exists only as metadata: the origin letters A, M and R (p. 8) and the application tables (p. 6–7). This supports the SKILL.md limitation note that the 1990s CUTE style predates data profiles and noisy testing.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: CUTE is the test leg of the LANCELOT triad. The facilities were built with LANCELOT (p. 3), and the companion reports hold the LANCELOT option results, including about 20 000 runs (p. 5).
- Research Heuristic 7 ✅ evidence (release the test environment and input format with the solver): SIF, the decoder and the collection come from LANCELOT and are released for general use (p. 2–4).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: structure is built into the format. The paper says large-scale problems "contain a great deal of information in their structure" (p. 10). Group partial separability is the input model (p. 11). The classification records a sum-of-squares objective (S) and the degree of analytic derivatives supplied (p. 8).
- Method 4 ⚠ weak variant: problems reach the authors through their users, because the LANCELOT licence obliges users to submit typical problems, which should make the collection "more user-oriented" (p. 4). This is Method 4's idea that problems come to you through users (Heuristic 9), at the level of software users rather than industrial partners.
- New-pattern candidate: *metadata-first benchmark*: a compact, machine-queryable classification string for every problem plus a selection tool, so a study can report results for every problem of a given class (p. 5, p. 8–10, p. 35–39).
- New-pattern candidate: *solver-agnostic evaluation layer*: one problem format, a decoder, and a gather-style evaluation API with a template driver per solver (p. 11–12, p. 17–23).
- New-pattern candidate: *licence-driven problem harvesting*: make contributing test problems part of the software licence (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Give every benchmark problem a fixed-format class string (objective type, constraint type, smoothness, analytic derivative degree, origin academic/modelling/real, n, m or V) and ship a query tool. Select test subsets by class, not by hand (p. 8–10, p. 35–39).
- [D2] Record each problem's origin (academic, modelling, real application) as metadata, so results can later be split into tiers (p. 8).
- [D2] Put one evaluation API in front of all solvers, offering f, ∇f, dense, coordinate and element Hessians, and Hv products, with a template driver script for adding a new solver (p. 17–23).
- [D5] Ship one small smoke-test problem per interfaced solver to check installations before any benchmark (Table 4, p. 18).
- [D7] Order an infrastructure paper by the user's questions and include a verbatim session transcript (p. 3, p. 35–39).
- [D8] Delay extending a shared classification until users have given feedback, and commit to backward compatibility (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "Moreover, not only can one assume that the collection will grow steadily, but because of the LANCELOT licensing agreement, which requires the submission of typical problems by most users of the package, the new problems are likely to be even more user-oriented than the present ones." (p. 4)
- "The only way to really judge the effectiveness of the environment is for the reader to use it." (p. 25)
