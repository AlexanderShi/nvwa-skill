# Paper cards · batch s2-01 (supplement)

Batch intent: See how the pre-IBM Conn (1978–1989) designed algorithms. The batch has four kinds of paper. Three belong to the nonsmooth programme: the 1978 direct minimax method (S233), the 1980/82 exact-penalty method (S030) and the 1988 Conn–Li characterisation of nonlinear Chebyshev problems (S130). Two are linear-algebra papers: the LINPACK condition estimator (S048) and directions of infinite descent (S096). One is the 1989 LANCELOT manifesto (S046). Two are published corrections of Conn's own theorems (D001, S059). The questions are: how often an algorithm is built from an optimality characterisation plus one maintained factorisation (horizontal/vertical steps, ε-active sets, projections); how the LANCELOT principles of Method 1 and Method 5 were stated when the project began; and how Conn handled errors in his published proofs. The batch tests Methods 1, 2, 5 and 6 at their origin.
Focus dimensions: the supplement set: D1, D2, D3, D4 (brief), D5 (one line) and D8. D6/D7 notes are added where the paper is unusually explicit (S233 on dual versus direct methods; S048's conclusion).
Material roles: all supplement.

### S233 · DIRECT APPROACHES FOR THE MINIMAX PROBLEM (Computers and Mathematical Programming: Proceedings of the Bicentennial Conference (NBS Special Publication 502) 1978, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author; Dept. of Combinatorics and Optimization, Waterloo, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Waterloo nondifferentiable-optimization period. The paper merges Charalambous–Conn (report CORR 74/19, nonlinear minimax) and Bartels–Conn–Charalambous (CORR 76/30, linear ℓ∞) into one algorithm (pp. 3, 10). **Read**: all 10 pages (full), from noisy OCR text.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Problem: minimize M_f(x) = max_i f_i(x) with f_i twice continuously differentiable (p. 1).
  - Entry: M_f has discontinuous first derivatives wherever two or more f_i tie at the maximum, so gradient methods cannot be applied directly (p. 1).
  - Three observations frame the approach (p. 1):
    1. the nondifferentiability;
    2. the equivalent NLP P* (min z subject to z − f_i(x) ≥ 0), which Waren–Lasdon–Suchman solve as a standard NLP in n+1 variables;
    3. the KKT characterisation of a minimax optimum as a convex combination of active gradients (2a–2d).
  - The approach is "direct": P* is used only to determine the search direction, and a related direction is then applied directly to M_f (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Descent: all active functions must be decreased simultaneously. Active functions correspond to active constraints of P* (p. 2).
  - Direction: d = −Pe, where P = I − N(NᵀN)⁻¹Nᵀ projects onto the null space of the active-constraint gradients in (z, x)-space and e = [1, 0, …, 0] (eqs. 3–5, p. 2).
    - For linear or concave active functions, no active constraint is violated, and eᵀd = −‖Pe‖² < 0 (eqs. 7–9, p. 2).
    - For convex active functions (Example 2), all active f_i still decrease locally (eqs. 10–11). The paper concludes that the approach is "more general than just solving the problem P*" (p. 3).
  - Two directions per iteration (p. 3):
    - The *horizontal* direction keeps the ε-near-active set constant and decreases M_f.
    - The *vertical* direction is a least-squares solution of the underdetermined linearised system that makes the near-active functions exactly equal. The two are orthogonal in the limit.
  - Linear algebra: a QR factorisation of the active-gradient matrix, updated by Givens rotations when a column is added or dropped. Linear functions enter first, and their part of the factorisation is stored and updated rather than recomputed (pp. 4–5, 7).
  - Dropping: when Pe = 0, solve Rw = Q₁ᵀe. If w ≥ 0 the point is optimal; otherwise drop the function with the most negative w_j (Step 3c, p. 5).
  - Vertical-step trigger: the active set is unchanged for three consecutive iterations and ‖d‖ < 0.1, or n+1 functions are in the projection. In the second case the step is forced and ε is set to ε/10 (Steps 7 and 9, p. 6).
  - Line search on M_f itself: estimate the breakpoints where an inactive function would become active by linearisation (eqs. 24–27). Take the candidate with the best linearised max. Accept it if the true max improves; otherwise fall back to a cubic search. In practice only sufficient decrease is asked for (pp. 6–7).
  - Changed versus ref. [5]: a stable implementation plus exploitation of linearity. Versus Zangwill [17]: the horizontal direction is the same, but the vertical step replaces Zangwill's reduction of the activity tolerance to zero (pp. 7, 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f_i ∈ C² (p. 1).
  - Active gradients are assumed linearly independent. Degeneracy is "cleared up" by negligible perturbations of the data, and the dependent case is ignored (remark iv, p. 7).
  - Convergence holds under some fairly natural assumptions (paraphrase); the proofs are in [5], and finite convergence for the linear case is in [4] (remark v, p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - No proofs appear here. The only arguments are local Taylor arguments showing that −Pe decreases the active functions: linear or concave case (eqs. 7–9, p. 2) and convex case (eqs. 10–11, p. 3).
  - Positioning device: the Barrodale–Phillips dual LP algorithm is restated in primal terms, stage by stage. Each stage can be done with the same projection linear algebra as Algorithm 2.2 (pp. 8–9).
- **D5 Experiments**: "Numerical results are not reported here". They are delegated to [5], [3] and [6], which cover applications, general nonlinear and linear problems (p. 9). Ref. [6] is Charalambous–Conn, "Optimization of Microwave Networks", IEEE Trans. Microwave Theory Tech. 1975 (p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning (note)**:
  - The linear-case relatives are compared in detail: Bartels–Conn–Charalambous [3] and its three line searches (p. 8), and Cline [10], which is restricted to Haar-condition problems (p. 8).
  - On Barrodale–Phillips: there is no well-documented evidence that the dual approach is best for linear ℓ∞ problems (p. 8). The paper ends that discussion with a rhetorical question (p. 9; Quotes).
  - Zangwill's separate full-rank and almost-full-rank cases are called "numerically undesirable" (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: algorithmic synthesis plus a stable numerical-linear-algebra implementation (pp. 3, 7).
  - Limits, all stated: no numbers and no proofs here (pp. 7, 9). Iterations are steepest-descent-like in subspaces. A second-order generalisation "is not obvious, even assuming it is desirable", because P* has a linear objective and a straightforward non-orthogonal projection will not work (p. 9).
  - Stated future: added constraints can be handled naturally, because the method is projection-based (p. 9).
  - Acknowledgement: Bartels taught the importance of numerical linear algebra, and Charalambous introduced the minimax problem (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the method works on the components f_i of the max rather than on M_f as a black box. Linear f_i are singled out and their factorisation is stored (pp. 1–2, 7).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): gradient-projection machinery from constrained NLP (Rosen, Zoutendijk, Conn's own 1976 conference paper *Projection Matrices*, refs. [8], [15], [18]) is carried into nonsmooth minimax, with active functions treated as active constraints (p. 2).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): a survey-synthesis paper whose theory and numbers live in companion reports [3], [5] and [6] (pp. 7, 9). The triad is spread across a cluster, not held in one paper.
- Method 4 ✅ (weak evidence): Conn's minimax work was tied to an application, microwave-network design, by 1975 (ref. [6], pp. 9–10). SKILL.md dates the minimax-to-circuits link to the IBM years.
- new-pattern candidate: **Horizontal/vertical step split**: a null-space step that decreases the merit function with the activities held fixed, plus a least-squares range-space step that restores the activities (p. 3). The same split is in S030 and S130 (see Cross-card notes).
- new-pattern candidate: **ε-active working set with automatic tolerance reduction** (ε ← ε/10, p. 6). Also in S030 and S130.
- new-pattern candidate: **Characterisation-driven algorithm design** (from s2-02): the method is built "based on three properties" of the minimax solution (p. 1).
- new-pattern candidate: **Answer an old question from one's own early work** (from s01): the 1978 dual-versus-direct question (p. 9) reappears as a title ten years later: Bartels–Conn–Li, *Primal methods are better than dual methods for solving overdetermined linear systems in the l∞ sense?* (S130, ref. [5], p. 33).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a max-type objective, lift to (z, x). Take d = −Pe, with P the projector onto the null space of the active-constraint gradients. Read optimality or the index to drop from the sign of the least-squares multipliers w = R⁻¹Q₁ᵀe (pp. 2, 5).
- For a piecewise-smooth merit function, a cheap line search: linearise each inactive function against the current max to predict its breakpoint, then evaluate the true function at the best predicted breakpoint. Fall back to a standard search only if that fails (pp. 6–7).
- Order the factorisation so that constant (linear) columns come first, and store that block. Each iteration then updates only the nonlinear part (pp. 4, 7).
- To contest a rival's claimed advantage, rewrite the rival's algorithm in the language of your own method, stage by stage, so the comparison is like for like (pp. 8–9).

**Quotes**:
- "It then seems natural to ask the question that if the dual approach to the minimax problem is superior to the direct approach, when is a dual approach indirect?" (p. 9)
- "R. Bartels impressed on me the importance of numerical linear algebra in the context of mathematical programming" (p. 9)

### D001 · Erratum (Math. Program. 1981, DOI 10.1007/bf01584255)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Thomas F. Coleman · **Position in trajectory**: a one-page notice correcting Coleman & Conn, "Second-order conditions for an exact penalty function", Math. Programming 19 (1980) 178–185 (p. 1). That paper is ref. [6] of S030, where it is the source of the *strict second-order point* terminology (S030, pp. 17, 27). **Read**: the whole notice (full; one page). The last line of the corrected condition is garbled in the text file and was not interpreted.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: the entry is outside readers. A. Ben-Tal and J. Zowe pointed out that condition (iii) of Corollary 3 (top of p. 182 of the original) was incorrectly stated (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea (the correction)**: As far as the text shows, the corrected condition quantifies over a set of test directions y defined case by case (p. 1):
  - equality yᵀ∇φ_i = 0 when the multiplier w_i lies strictly inside (−1, 1);
  - one-sided inequalities when w_i = −1 (≤ 0) or w_i = 1 (≥ 0);
  - for the second index set, equality when λ_i > 0 and ≥ 0 when λ_i = 0.
  So the set of test directions is a cone, not a subspace. The final inequality of the condition is garbled and not read.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: not stated in the notice; they are those of the 1980 paper, which was not read in this batch.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none.
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a corrected second-order condition for an exact penalty function.
  - Integrity practice: the finders are credited by name (p. 1). The notice is minimal: one corollary, one condition.

**Method links**:
- new-pattern candidate: **Public erratum crediting the finder**. Here the finders are Ben-Tal and Zowe (p. 1). S059 does the same, thanking Moré and Toraldo (S059, p. 4). It sits next to the existing candidates *Self-audit note on one's own solver* (s01) and *correct a published theorem* (c2-04).
- Method 6 (context only, no evidence either way): the exact-penalty second-order theory corrected here is the toolkit that S030 builds on (S030, p. 17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a second-order condition involves multipliers that can sit on the boundary of their range (here ±1, or 0), write the test-direction set case by case: equality for interior multipliers, one-sided inequality for boundary ones. The error corrected here was in exactly this kind of condition (p. 1).

**Quotes**:
- "It has been brought to the authors' attention by A. Ben-Tal and J. Zowe that condition (iii) of Corollory 3 (see top of page 182) is incorrectly stated." (p. 1)

### S030 · Nonlinear programming via an exact penalty function: Global analysis (Mathematical Programming 1982, DOI 10.1007/bf01585101)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: T. F. Coleman (Argonne; Conn's 1979 PhD student per SKILL.md) (p. 4) · **Position in trajectory**: the global-convergence half of a pair. The 2-step superlinear rate is in the companion report CS-80-30 ("asymptotic analysis", ref. [7]) (pp. 4, 27). **Read**: all 28 pages from page images (full). The source is Waterloo report CS-80-31 (July 1980), not the journal version.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Problem: min f(x) subject to φ_i(x) ≥ 0, with f and φ_i in C². Equality constraints are omitted only to simplify the presentation (p. 4).
  - Reformulation: the exact ℓ1 penalty p(x, μ) = f(x) − (1/μ) Σ min(0, φ_i(x)) (eq. 2.1). The major difficulty is its nondifferentiability (pp. 4–5).
  - Entry: the Conn–Pietrzykowski method [9] is based on a first-order projected direction. It is globally convergent but in general only linearly convergent, "since h* is obtained by minimizing only up to first-order terms" (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Horizontal step: minimise the change in p to second order, subject to zero change in the ε-active constraints (eqs. 2.9–2.10). This is not explicitly computable, so it is approximated by a min–max Lagrangian with multiplier estimates λ̃ (eqs. 2.11–2.12). The result is the projected Newton step h = −Z(ZᵀBZ)⁻¹Zᵀ∇p̄ (eqs. 2.16–2.17, pp. 6–7).
  - Far from stationarity (‖Zᵀ∇p‖ > Λ), multipliers "have little meaning" and λ is not computed (p. 7).
  - Multiplier estimates: least squares on A_kλ = ∇p̄ (eq. 2.19, p. 8).
  - Vertical step: v = −A(AᵀA)⁻¹Φ(x + αh) (eq. 2.22), which satisfies the ε-active constraints more precisely near x* (p. 8).
  - Dropping step: when some λ_j ∉ [0, 1/μ], move along h̃ = σ_j Z_{k\j} Z_{k\j}ᵀ ∇φ_j (eqs. 2.23–2.25). Dropping and the vertical step are "complementary activities" (pp. 8–9).
  - Direction choice (p. 9): use h alone when ‖Zᵀ∇p‖ > Λ; otherwise try a drop or the Newton step h + v with unit step, falling back to a line search along h (pp. 9–10).
  - Tolerance rule: ε and Λ are halved when a drop or a Newton step fails (p. 10).
  - Algorithm 1 (p. 11): a breakpoint line search for the piecewise-linear case. For nonlinear functions it is extended by linearisation, with a sufficient-decrease test δ and a cubic-interpolation fallback. Choosing the breakpoint costs at most one penalty-function evaluation (pp. 11–12).
  - Algorithm 2 is given as a flowchart (p. 14). Algorithm 3 wraps it and sets μ ← μ/10 when μ appears too large (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The ε-active gradient matrix A_k is always assumed to have full rank (p. 17).
  - Line-search condition: p(x^k) − p(x^k + αh^k) ≥ γ₁((h^k)ᵀg^k)². This extends Conn–Pietrzykowski's Proposition 1 (p. 18).
  - Theorem 1 assumptions (p. 22):
    1. C² functions;
    2. iterates in a compact set W;
    3. finitely many stationary points of p in W;
    4. every first-order point is a strict second-order point;
    5. linear independence of the ε-active gradients;
    6. the line-search condition holds;
    7. consistent second-order approximation along subsequences that converge to a first-order point.
  - Admitted gaps: scaling and the choice of Λ, ε are not addressed (p. 13), and the μ-reduction safeguards are "not guaranteed to work" (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Lemma 1: near a strict second-order point, with the correct active set and a good projected-Hessian approximation, p(x+h+v) − p(x) ≤ −δ(‖Zᵀ∇p̄‖² + ‖Φ‖₁) (p. 18). The proof expands the differentiable part p̄ and the active φ_i separately by Taylor's theorem. It uses λ_i − η_i/μ ≠ 0 (strictness) to absorb the cross terms (pp. 19–22).
  - Theorem 1 conclusions: ε_k does not tend to 0; x^k → x̄ in the set of first-order points; the Newton step is eventually always taken (pp. 22–23).
  - The proof of Theorem 1 has six parts (pp. 23–26):
    1. h is a descent direction;
    2. the dropping direction descends, by cases λ_j < 0 or λ_j > 1/μ;
    3. finiteness of the non-optimal stationary points gives a uniform descent constant;
    4. drops cannot occur infinitely often, since otherwise p(x^k) → −∞;
    5. the active set is eventually contained in the true one;
    6. Newton steps eventually succeed.
- **D5 Experiments**: eight classic problems (Rosen–Suzuki, Wong, Powell, Colville 1–3, Chamberlain 1–2), 11 problem/starting-point runs in all. The table gives the best and worst number of penalty-function evaluations over parameter ranges ε ∈ [1, 5], μ ∈ [.001, 1] and Λ ∈ [.001, .1], plus the digits of accuracy. The Chamberlain problems were designed to make recursive QP cycle (p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: algorithm plus global theory plus implementation plus preliminary numbers in one paper (pp. 3, 27).
  - Claims: a positive-definite full Lagrangian Hessian at the solution is not needed; the expensive gradient-difference Hessian is used only in the final stages (p. 27).
  - Limits: the direction-choice mechanism is "somewhat arbitrary" and based on limited testing, but the proof shows that "any number of selection rules" keep global convergence (p. 10).
  - Stated future: a full projected quasi-Newton implementation and scaling (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): theory, implementation (§4, pp. 15–16) and numbers (p. 26) in one paper, with the rate analysis in a twin report [7] (p. 4).
- Research Taste 2 ✅ (evidence): the theory is said to cover a class of selection rules, not the one coded (p. 10).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): the cost unit is penalty-function evaluations, and best *and* worst results over parameter ranges are reported (p. 26), as Method 2 steps 1 and 5 ask. The test set is a pre-CUTE handful of named problems.
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): Gill–Murray linearly-constrained techniques (QR, LDLᵀ, finite-difference projected Hessians, rank-2 updates) carried into a nonlinearly constrained penalty method (pp. 15–16). The first-order Conn–Pietrzykowski direction is upgraded to its second-order analogue (p. 7).
- new-pattern candidates **Horizontal/vertical step split** (pp. 5–10) and **ε-active working set with automatic tolerance reduction** (p. 10); see Cross-card notes.
- new-pattern candidate **Split by type, give each block its natural treatment** (from s01): linear constraints always precede nonlinear ones in the QR partition, so their columns never change (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To turn a first-order projected method into a second-order one, pose the subproblem *minimise the change in the merit function to second order, subject to zero change in the active constraints to second order*. Approximate its Lagrangian with lagged multipliers, and you get a reduced Newton system (eqs. 2.9–2.17, pp. 6–7).
- Gate the use of multiplier estimates on a projected-gradient threshold Λ, since far from stationarity they are meaningless. Halve Λ and ε whenever a gated step fails (pp. 7, 10).
- Report best and worst evaluation counts over a stated parameter box, not a tuned setting (p. 26).
- Split global and asymptotic analyses into twin reports, each self-contained (pp. 4, 27).

**Quotes**: none. The text file is empty, so no quote can be grep-verified.

### S048 · Generalizing the LINPACK condition estimator (Numerical Analysis: Proceedings of the Third IIMAS Workshop Held at Cocoyoc... 1982, DOI 10.1007/bfb0092961)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. K. Cline (UT Austin), C. F. Van Loan (Cornell); Conn at Waterloo CS (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: a pure numerical-linear-algebra side paper from Conn's Cornell contacts. The PDF is Cornell TR 81-462, June 1981 (p. 1). **Read**: all 22 pages from page images (full), preprint version.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Setting: the relative error of a computed solution is about b^{−t}κ_p(A) (eq. 1). A condition estimator is reliable if c₁κ ≤ κ̂ ≤ c₂κ with constants independent of A (eq. 2) (p. 5).
  - The SVD is provably reliable but costs about 15 times the flops of Gaussian elimination. The challenge is a reliable O(n²) estimate once A has been factored (p. 6).
  - Existing options: Forsythe–Moler has portability issues and unproven reliability; Karasalo's c₁ is of order 4^{−n} (pp. 6–7).
  - The LINPACK estimator is inverse iteration from a greedily chosen right-hand side, d_k ∈ {−1, +1} with "look-ahead" weights. It rests on an extra heuristic: a large solution of Uᵀy = d implies a large solution of Aᵀw = d (pp. 7–9).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: two generalisations (abstract, p. 3).
  - Look-behind (Algorithm 1): at step k choose a rotation (c, s) = (cos a, sin a). Scale the earlier d_1, …, d_{k−1} by s and set d_k = c, maximising s²Σy_i² + y_k² + Σw_i²(sp_i + t_ik y_k)² (p. 11). φ′(a) = 0 gives two closed-form sine–cosine pairs, and the better one is picked by substitution (eq. 6). The cost is about 5n² flops, and the result is an estimate of (σ_n, v_n, u_n) (eq. 7, p. 12).
  - Minimising instead of maximising estimates σ₁ (eq. 8). In the L1 version (Algorithm 2) the objective is convex in λ ∈ [0, 1], so only λ = 0 and λ = 1 need checking. The final d is then a column of the identity, and y a column of T⁻¹ (pp. 13–14).
  - Divide and conquer: combine two solved triangular subsystems with the rotation that maximises ‖z₁‖² + ‖z₂‖², in closed form (eq. 9, p. 15). Pair 1×1 blocks into 2×2, then 4×4, and so on, with a rule for dimensions that are not a power of 2 (p. 16). The cost is a few n² flops (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: A = T is lower triangular, i.e. already factored (p. 10). The weights w_i ≥ 0 are fixed (p. 11). No reliability theorem is claimed.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: a one-line SVD argument, ‖z‖₂/‖w‖₂ ≥ ‖A⁻¹‖₂ cos(v_n, d), motivates the aim of a large w (p. 8). Otherwise the paper consists of closed-form scalar maximisations (pp. 12, 15) and a convexity endpoint argument (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: three tests (pp. 18–20).
  - Test 1: 1000 random lower-triangular T (entries in [−1, 1], 100 each for n = 5, …, 50). The tables give the distribution of q_n = σ_n/σ̂_n and q₁ = σ̂₁/σ₁ in bins, for three variants E1–E3 (p. 18).
  - Test 2: T from QR with column pivoting. There q_n > 0.9 in at least 97.4% of cases (p. 19).
  - Test 3: 250 L1 cases, of which 78% have κ̂₁/κ₁ > 0.99 (p. 20).
  - The comments report the unexplained: no correlation with dimension or conditioning; σ₁ estimates "consistently inferior", with "no explanation"; no q below 0.05 (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing (note)**: the conclusion refuses to rank.
  - The results are only experimental and prove nothing (paraphrase, p. 21).
  - The authors ask whether to argue for inclusion in LINPACK, then admit that the divide-and-conquer estimator is easy to break with a known 4×4 counterexample (p. 21).
  - They conclude that one must not argue about whose estimator is "better" but work towards an efficient *provably reliable* technique (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: two estimator families plus an empirical study.
  - Limits: no proofs, known counterexamples, and the σ₁ weakness is unexplained (pp. 19, 21).
  - Stated future: Test 2 suggests a rigorous result may be possible when T comes from pivoted QR (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Research Taste warning sign 6 ✅ (evidence against overclaiming): the conclusion explicitly declines to claim superiority from experiments and names the counterexample to the authors' own method (p. 21).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): two instance tiers, random and structured (pivoted QR), reported as full distributions including the worst bin (pp. 18–20). The instances are synthetic ensembles, not a public collection.
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): algorithms and numbers with no theorem. The paper names provable reliability as the missing piece (p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: **Factorization-first algorithm design** (see Cross-card notes). This paper shows Conn's direct numerical-linear-algebra (NLA) credentials (pp. 3, 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Report an estimator's quality as a binned distribution of true/estimate ratios over a random ensemble and a structured ensemble. Include the worst bin, and state the minimum observed ratio (pp. 18–20).
- When one scalar parameter mixes an old partial solution with a new component, parametrise it as a rotation (c, s). Setting φ′(a) = 0 then gives two closed-form candidates, and you pick the better by substitution (pp. 12, 15).
- If the objective is convex in a mixing parameter on [0, 1], check only the endpoints. This can collapse the algorithm to a discrete choice (pp. 13–14).
- In a conclusion, separate what the experiments suggest from what can be proved, and name the theorem you would need (p. 21).

**Quotes**: none. The text file is empty, so no quote can be grep-verified.

### S096 · On the location of directions of infinite descent for nonlinear programming algorithms (SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis 1984, DOI 10.1137/0721072)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould. Both authors were at Waterloo, one in Computer Science and one in Combinatorics and Optimization; the OCR loses which footnote belongs to whom (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the earliest Conn–Gould paper in this corpus (received July 1983, p. 1), before CGT. **Read**: pp. 1–17 in full; references skimmed (full).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Setting: equality-constrained QP subproblems (EQP: min ½pᵀHp + gᵀp subject to Ap = 0) arise in SQP, active-set methods and the Coleman–Conn method (p. 1).
  - Entry: when EQP has no finite solution, the current estimate is far off or the active-set prediction is wrong. A *direction of infinite descent* can then be used to decrease a merit function and to make an inactive constraint active (p. 2).
  - Stance: the method that computes p should not be "disjoint" from the method that decides existence, and should exploit structure (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Definitions: a *dolid* has Ap = 0, pᵀHp = 0 and gᵀp < 0 (linear descent). A *donc* has Ap = 0 and pᵀHp < 0 (negative curvature) (p. 2). Doncs are preferred because Q → −∞ quadratically (p. 3).
  - Existence by inertia (p. 4): Lemma 1.2 gives In(K) = In(ZᵀHZ) + (t, t, 0) and In(K) = In(H) + In(−AH⁻¹Aᵀ). Theorems 1.3–1.6 then read the four cases (unique minimiser, weak minimiser, dolid, donc) off the inertias used by the null-space, Lagrangian and range-space methods (p. 5).
  - Construction from the Bunch–Parlett–Fletcher–Kaufman generalised Cholesky factorisation PᵀGP = MDMᵀ, which the method already uses (p. 6).
    - A dolid is the projection of the right-hand side onto the null space of the coefficient matrix (Lemma 2.1 and corollaries), built from the zero 1×1 blocks of D (pp. 6–7).
    - Doncs combine conjugate negative vectors taken from the negative 1×1 and the 2×2 blocks of D (eq. 3.1, p. 9).
    - For Lagrangian methods, k₋ > t negative vectors are combined to satisfy t constraints. This reduces to a null vector of a "lower triangular-like" matrix N (eq. 3.4), found by backward column elimination with no structural fill-in (pp. 8, 10–11).
  - Novelty claim: the null-space version is "basically" Bunch–Kaufman (1980); the Lagrangian and range-space extensions are new (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: A has full rank t ≤ n (p. 1). n is small enough for direct dense factorisations (p. 4). Range-space methods need H nonsingular (pp. 8, 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Existence proofs are one-liners from the inertia identities (p. 5).
  - Dolids come from a Fredholm-type alternative (Lemma 2.1, p. 6).
  - Doncs come from a counting argument: k > t conjugate negative vectors always admit a combination satisfying t homogeneous constraints (p. 8).
  - Efficiency comes from a structural-zero argument showing that N is lower triangular-like (p. 11).
  - An operation-count table (p. 14) and a worst case of t³/3 + O(t²) for the Lagrangian step (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: pseudo-random EQPs with n = 30 and t varied from 1 to 29, H diagonal with 6 negative eigenvalues, A full (pp. 14–15). Results (p. 16):
  - For t ≤ 17, N was already triangular. For t ≥ 18, 3–15% of the superdiagonals needed elimination.
  - The range-space method needed more work, hence the advice to prefer Lagrangian methods on nonconvex problems with large h₋.
  - Residuals of Ap were about 10⁻¹⁶ (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a unified characterisation plus constructions for three method families, with costs.
  - Limits: two unresolved issues (how triangular N is; how many columns the range-space method needs), and experiments "by no means exhaustive" (p. 14).
  - Practical rules (p. 16): use null-space methods when t is large; if t is less than the number of negative eigenvalues of H, a donc must exist; for QP with many negative eigenvalues, always use null-space methods.
  - Stated future (p. 17): the Ap = d variant; for sparse problems, trading some stability for structure through threshold pivoting in the generalised Cholesky factorisation; keeping the factors of H as the leading block of K; updating the factors when A changes.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the certificate is built from matrices the underlying EQP method already factors (p. 3). The future work puts structure preservation ahead of full pivoting stability (p. 17).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): negative-curvature techniques from unconstrained optimisation (Fletcher–Freeman, Sorensen, Moré–Sorensen, Goldfarb) are carried into the constrained EQP setting for three method families (pp. 2, 6).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): controlled synthetic instances with a fixed number of negative eigenvalues are swept over t to measure the structural property that the cost depends on (Table 2, p. 15). There is no public test set.
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): theory, construction, operation counts and a small experiment, but no code release.
- new-pattern candidate: **Make the subproblem's failure informative**. When the subproblem has no solution, return a certificate (a dolid or donc) that the outer algorithm uses to repair its estimate and its active set (p. 2).
- new-pattern candidate: **Factorization-first algorithm design** (see Cross-card notes).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Decide existence and compute the step with *one* factorisation. Read the inertia from the block-diagonal D of PᵀGP = MDMᵀ, and build the certificate from the same factors (pp. 5–7).
- If Nx = b is inconsistent, the normalised projection of b onto null(N) gives y with Ny = 0 and bᵀy = 1. This is a direction of linear infinite descent at no extra cost (pp. 6–7).
- When a cost depends on an unknown structural property (here how nearly triangular N is), measure that property directly across a parameter sweep, rather than reporting only run times (Table 2, pp. 14–16).
- Quick unboundedness test for an equality-constrained QP: if the number of constraints is less than the number of negative eigenvalues of H, a direction of negative curvature exists (p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "We take the point of view that it is important that our method of determining p is not disjoint from our method of determining existence." (p. 1)
- "The numerical experiments are by no means exhaustive; it is merely intended that they illustrate that the approaches discussed in 3 are viable." (p. 14)

### S130 · The computational structure and characterization of nonlinear discrete Chebyshev problems (Cornell University 1988, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yuying Li (Cornell CS / Mathematical Sciences Institute; Conn's 1988 PhD student per SKILL.md) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: the characterisation paper of the Conn–Li minimax cluster. Its algorithm is deferred to companion report [10] (Waterloo CS-88-41) and Li's thesis [15] (pp. 32–33). The s2-02 cards (S164, S108) read that algorithm. **Read**: all 34 pages from page images (full). P. 2 was not viewed; its image is near-empty, so presumably blank.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Problem: min_x max_i |f_i(x)|, a local minimum, f_i continuously differentiable (p. 3).
  - Existing methods fall into first-order and second-order classes, and "all ... emphasize the role played by the underlying mathematical programming problem" (p. 4).
  - Entry: linear Chebyshev algorithms succeed by exploiting the *characterisation* of the solution (alternants, references) (pp. 5, 8). Classical nonlinear theory (unisolvent and varisolvent functions) depends on an unknown degree of solvency that varies with x, is restrictive, and cannot be applied to discrete problems (p. 10).
  - Aim: recognise the characterisation of the local solution "from the computational point of view" (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Generalised objects:
    - *cadre*: a minimally dependent set of l+1 gradients of rank l, i.e. every l-subset also has rank l (Def. 17, p. 12). It is Brannigan's "minimum H-set" and Descloux's cadre generalised to nonlinear discrete problems (pp. 12–13).
    - *cadre multipliers*: normalised to sum 1, or to sum 0 with λ₀ = 1. They are deliberately distinguished from Lagrange multipliers because they exist at any point (Def. 21, p. 14).
    - *reference set* and *levelled reference set*: same-sign function values and positive multipliers on a cadre (Defs. 23 and 26, pp. 15–16).
  - Characterisation: every local minimiser carries a levelled reference set with the maximum deviation (Theorem 31, p. 17).
  - Recipe: locate a cadre, build a reference set on it, then level it (p. 18). This mirrors the second Remez algorithm, whose speed is attributed to exploiting the structure of the best approximation (pp. 19–20).
  - Working set: the ε-active functions plus others, chosen so that A = [∇f_{i0} − ∇f_{ij}] has full rank (Def. 33, p. 20). The ideal working set (a min-norm convex-combination QP) is too expensive, and its existence is not guaranteed (p. 21).
  - Vertical (levelling) direction: v = −A(AᵀA)⁻¹Φ (eq. 7.4, p. 21).
    - On a non-levelled reference set it decreases all active functions (Lemma 35, pp. 22–23).
    - On a cadre that is not a reference set, a sign-flip rule (eq. 7.5) builds the set to level (p. 24).
    - Lemma 40 (pp. 29–30): if the multipliers sum to 1, active functions with negative multipliers decrease fastest. That is *multiple dropping*, versus the single deletion of usual active-set NLP (p. 31).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f_i ∈ C¹, local minimum only (p. 3).
  - The working-set equivalence assumes every stationary point is a nondegenerate local minimum (p. 20). Nondegeneracy means the active gradients form a cadre or are linearly independent (Def. 32, p. 18).
  - Lemma 37 holds "only in the linear case"; for nonlinear problems it is a constructive step that holds approximately (p. 25).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Lemma 20 characterises a cadre by rank plus a dependency with all λ_j ≠ 0, proved by contradiction (pp. 13–14).
  - Lemma 34 is Gordan's theorem (p. 22).
  - Lemma 35: write ∇f_μ as a convex combination of gradient differences. Then ∇f_μᵀv = −Σλ_j(f_μ − f_ij) < 0 (pp. 22–23).
  - Lemma 38 is a two-case sign argument (pp. 26–28). Lemma 39 is an algebraic identity for the normalised multipliers (eq. 7.16), which yields the decrease-rate ordering of Lemma 40 (pp. 28–30).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The efficient algorithm is in [10] (p. 32).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: theoretical, a computational characterisation meant to drive an algorithm (pp. 31–32).
  - Hedged claims: "We feel" in the abstract (p. 3); "we believe that these concepts continue to characterise" (p. 16).
  - Limits: open existence and cost of the ideal working set (p. 21); linear-only exactness of Lemma 37 (p. 25); no numbers.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the paper criticises methods that reduce everything to the underlying NLP. It instead exploits the structure of the problem *and of its solution* (pp. 4–5, 19–20).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): linear Chebyshev theory (references, levelling, Remez) is carried into nonlinear minimax. The NLP notion of dropping a constraint is reinterpreted as multiple dropping (pp. 11–18, 31).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): theory-only report. The algorithm and numbers are in companion report [10] and the thesis [15] (p. 32).
- new-pattern candidate **Characterisation-driven algorithm design** (from s2-02): the recipe cadre → reference set → level is read directly off Theorem 31 (pp. 17–18).
- new-pattern candidates **Horizontal/vertical step split** (vertical levelling direction, p. 21) and **ε-active working set** (p. 20); see Cross-card notes.
- new-pattern candidate **Answer an old question from one's own early work**: ref. [5] is Bartels–Conn–Li, *Primal methods are better than dual methods for solving overdetermined linear systems in the l∞ sense?* (p. 33). It takes up the question S233 asked in 1978 (S233, p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before designing an algorithm, restate the first-order conditions as a *constructible object* (here a levelled reference set on a cadre). Then design one step per property: find, sign-correct, level (pp. 17–18).
- To show that a restoration (levelling) step is also a descent step, write the gradient of the currently maximal function as a convex combination of gradient differences, using the cadre multipliers. The directional derivative then telescopes into a negative sum of function gaps (Lemma 35, pp. 22–23).
- Name new multipliers differently from Lagrange multipliers when they exist away from stationarity. This prevents readers importing the wrong intuitions (p. 14).

**Quotes**: none. The text file is empty, so no quote can be grep-verified.

### S046 · An introduction to the structure of large scale nonlinear optimization problems and the LANCELOT project (University of Waterloo. Department of Computer Science 1989, no DOI/arXiv)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould (Harwell), Ph. L. Toint (Namur) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the CGT manifesto at the start of LANCELOT. The project was "started ... more than two years ago" (p. 10). The paper precedes the 1992 book and the 1995 CUTE paper, and cites the SDIF proposal CS-89-61 as ref. [8] (p. 14). **Read**: all 15 pages; pp. 2–15 from page images and the cover (p. 1) from an OCR probe (full).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Entry: the widely held view that only small problems can be handled. More than 20 variables is thought "a risky modelling approach". That was true ten years earlier but no longer is (p. 2).
  - The paper offers the authors' "personnal (and maybe biased)" view on two topics: (group) partial separability and the LANCELOT project (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & design**:
  - Partial separability by example: the discretised minimum-surface problem (pp. 3–5). Each element depends on four corner variables but is invariant along a subspace N_ij (eq. 5). It is therefore a function of two *internal variables* obtained by a fixed map W (eqs. 6–12).
  - Consequence: storage drops from O[5(m+1)²] to O[3(m+1)²] (pp. 4–5). The italicised moral is that sparsity follows from structure but not the reverse (p. 5).
  - General definition (p. 6): f = Σf_i, where each f_i has a nontrivial invariant subspace, with internal variables u = W_i x (eqs. 14–16). Only the small ∇f̂_i and ∇²f̂_i are stored (eq. 17). W_i is usually a variable-selection list plus a small, often shared, transformation.
  - Theorem 1: every C² function with a sparse Hessian is partially separable (p. 7).
  - Group partial separability: f = Σ g_j(l_j(x) + Σ_{i∈J_j} f_i(x)) (eqs. 19–20). It covers least squares and (augmented) Lagrangians, and the SBMIN algorithm handles it (pp. 7–8).
  - Design arguments (p. 8):
    - using the computational graph for derivatives (AD) differs from using it inside the algorithm;
    - storage is of the same order as for specialised least-squares solvers;
    - user black boxes enter as element functions (pp. 8–9).
  - Parallelism (p. 9): element evaluations are asynchronous, the linear algebra is like finite-element assembly, and element Hessian updates are independent.
  - LANCELOT's six declared characteristics (pp. 11–13):
    1. structure use, with the cost on unstructured problems acknowledged;
    2. efficiency and reliability: theory first, "final implementations and the studied algorithms should differ as little as possible", and intensive testing on practical and academic problems;
    3. scope: smooth problems of any constraint type, nonsmooth not planned, no competition with LP;
    4. ease of use through the SDIF input format;
    5. adaptability: modular and hierarchical, with several preconditioners and reverse communication;
    6. portability: strict Fortran 77 across CRAY, IBM, DEC and SUN.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: element and group functions are C² (p. 7). The focus is smooth problems; nonsmooth problems are out of scope (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 1 is stated with a pointer to Griewank–Toint [15] (p. 7). Otherwise there are storage counts only (pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. SDIF-specified practitioner problems (energy modelling, finite elements, more than 1000 variables) were specified, validated and solved by the pilot LANCELOT in under an hour (p. 12, footnote 4). Detailed experiments "will shortly be reported elsewhere" (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a conceptual tutorial plus a software design statement (p. 13).
  - Limits: no numbers; the design is still a pilot; the structure is admitted to cost efficiency on unstructured problems (pp. 11, 13).
  - Conclusion: coherence of theory with real-world applications, and of the theoretical concepts with each other, is "of central importance" (p. 13).
  - The reference list cites CGT 1988 together with its 1989 correction, i.e. S059 (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (stated evidence, earliest in this corpus): implementations and the analysed algorithms "should differ as little as possible". Theory is necessary but not sufficient, and must be backed by intensive testing (p. 11). This is also the stated source of the anti-pattern *theorem about an idealised algorithm, code that does something else*.
- Method 5 ✅ (stated evidence): exploiting structure "is the only reasonable way" for large problems (pp. 3, 11). Black boxes are embedded as element functions inside a structured description rather than making the whole problem a black box (pp. 8–9).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): the standard input format (SDIF) and a "fairly sizeable set of large problems" (p. 12). Both practical and academic test problems are required (p. 11).
- Method 4 ✅ (weak evidence): the test problems come from energy-modelling and finite-element practitioners (p. 12), and "final efficiency and reliability achieved is best judged by the end-users" (p. 11).
- new-pattern candidate **Design the new standard as a superset of the incumbent** (from s2-02): partial separability is presented as extending sparsity (pp. 5, 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Describe structure by *invariant subspaces* and internal variables, not by sparsity patterns. Then store element derivatives in internal coordinates and count the storage saving on a model problem (pp. 4–6).
- Open a software paper with a numbered list of design requirements, each with its admitted cost. Here: structure (cost on unstructured problems), reliability (theory-code agreement plus testing), scope (what is *not* covered), ease of use, adaptability, portability (pp. 11–13).
- Give a usability metric from real users' problems: time from specification to validated solution (under one hour for problems with more than 1000 variables) (p. 12).

**Quotes**: none. The text file is empty, so no quote can be grep-verified.

### S059 · Correction to the paper on global convergence of a class of trust region algorithms for optimization with simple bounds (SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis 1989, DOI 10.1137/0726044)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould (Harwell), Ph. L. Toint (Namur) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: repairs the theory half of the CGT 1988 twin (SINUM 25:433–460), which SKILL.md cites as its first Method 1 example. Received June 8, 1988, one year after the original (p. 1). **Read**: all 4 pages (full). The OCR loses many symbols, so formulas are described, not transcribed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Scope: explicitly not a reconsideration of global convergence and active-set identification, only the correction of one error. The reader is assumed to know [1], and braces refer to its equations (p. 1).
  - The error: in the proof of Theorem 14 (finite identification of the correct active set), {169} holds only for successful iterates but was used to deduce {176} for all k (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea (new proof)**: the statement of Theorem 14 is unchanged. Under (AS.1)–(AS.7) and {155}, I(x_k) = I(x_*) for all large k (p. 1).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: exactly those of [1], (AS.1)–(AS.7) and {155}, which are not restated here (p. 1).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: the proof skeleton (pp. 1–4):
  1. Fix a limit point, its connected set of limit points X_* (Lemma 12) and a radius δ that isolates X_*. Near X_*, the active set is contained in I(X_*) and the relevant gradient components keep sign and magnitude ≥ ε (eqs. 2–4) (p. 1).
  2. Take the subsequence of *successful* iterations inside N(X_*, δ) (eq. 6). Assume for contradiction that the active sets there are strictly smaller than I(X_*) (eq. 8). A Cauchy-decrease bound (Lemma 6) plus (AS.6) then gives b_kΔ_k → 0 along the subsequence (eqs. 9–11) (p. 2).
  3. Trapping: steps are small, so iterates cannot leave N(X_*, δ). The subsequence is therefore all successful iterations, and "the mechanism of the algorithm" gives Δ_k → 0 (eqs. 12–15) (p. 2).
  4. Show that all iterations are eventually successful. Take an unsuccessful iteration followed by a successful one (eq. 16; symbols partly lost) and derive ρ_k ≥ η in two cases (pp. 3–4):
     - the Cauchy point has a strictly smaller active set (eqs. 19–22);
     - it has the full active set (eq. 23). This case uses a decomposition of g_k and s_{k+1} − s_k into components in C(x_*) and its orthogonal complement, with a sign argument (eqs. 25–35).
  5. All iterations successful contradicts eq. 15. So eq. 8 is false, and Lemma 13 gives the result (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a repaired proof with the theorem's statement preserved (p. 1).
  - Integrity: the authors thank J. J. Moré and G. Toraldo for pointing out the error, and M. Lescrenier and A. Sartenaer for comments on the correction (p. 4). CGT later cite the correction next to the original in S046's reference list (S046, p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): the theory half of SKILL.md's flagship triad needed a published repair a year later. It was found by outside readers, Moré and Toraldo (pp. 1, 4). The triad is not error-free; errors are fixed in public.
- new-pattern candidate: **Public erratum crediting the finder** (with D001; see Cross-card notes).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a lemma holds only on successful iterations, take the subsequence of successful iterates *inside a neighbourhood that isolates a connected set of limit points*. Then show steps are too short to leave it, so the subsequence is in fact all successful iterations (eqs. 6, 13, p. 2).
- To prove that all iterations are eventually successful, argue on a pair: an unsuccessful iteration k followed by a successful k+1. Show ρ_k ≥ η, which contradicts the failure at k (eqs. 16–35, pp. 3–4).
- Write a correction note that keeps the theorem's statement and the original equation numbering (braces), and replaces only the broken inference (p. 1).

**Quotes**:
- "As a consequence, {176}, and hence {177}, only holds for successful iterates, which is not enough to obtain the desired contradiction." (p. 1)
- "We now wish to show that all iterations are successful for k sufficiently large." (p. 3)

### Cross-card notes (batch s2-01)
- **Horizontal/vertical step split: three papers in this batch, plus S164 in s2-02.** Each builds a null-space ("horizontal") step that decreases the merit function with the activities held fixed, and a least-squares range-space ("vertical") step v = −A(AᵀA)⁻¹Φ that restores or levels the activities:
  - S233 (1978): minimax (pp. 3, 6);
  - S030 (1980): exact penalty (eq. 2.22, pp. 8–10);
  - S130 (1988): Chebyshev levelling (eq. 7.4, p. 21);
  - S164 (s2-02): h + v (p. 10).
  This meets the ≥3-paper threshold as a new-pattern candidate. It is an algorithm-design signature of the pre-trust-region Conn. S030's merit function is the exact ℓ1 penalty, so the split also bears on Method 6.
- **ε-active working set with automatic tolerance reduction: three papers.** S233 (ε ← ε/10 when n+1 functions enter, p. 6); S030 (ε and Λ halved after failed steps, p. 10); S130 (the working set includes all ε-active functions, p. 20).

  - S233: QR with Givens add/drop, and multiplier signs from R⁻¹Q₁ᵀe (pp. 4–5);
  - S030: Gill–Murray QR and LDLᵀ (pp. 15–16);
  - S096: inertia and certificates from generalised Cholesky (pp. 5–11);
  - S048: triangular-factor condition estimation (pp. 11–16).

- **Public erratum crediting the finder: two papers.** D001 names Ben-Tal and Zowe (p. 1); S059 names Moré and Toraldo (p. 4). S046 then cites CGT 1988 together with its correction (p. 13).
