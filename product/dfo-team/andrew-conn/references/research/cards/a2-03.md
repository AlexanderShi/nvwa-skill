### S076 · Reservoir uncertainty quantification using probabilistic history matching workflow (SPE Annual Technical Conference and Exhibition 2014, DOI 10.2118/170893-ms)
**Read**: abstract (abstracts.json, via Crossref).

**Coauthors**:
- Crossref lists Tzu-hao Yeh (Scholar gives "Y Tzu-hao"), Eduardo A. Jimenez, Gijs van Essen, Chaohui Chen, Alejandro Girardi and Paul Gelderblom (both Shell), and Lior Horesh (IBM). Conn (IBM) is listed last.
- The sources disagree on the author list. Crossref's affiliation field for the first four authors reads "Long Jin". S2 lists Long Jin as a ninth author.
- Other details: paper SPE-170893-MS, conference date 27 Oct 2014, 14 references (Crossref).

**D1**: The paper starts from an industry practice. One model, or three cases (high-mid-low), cannot describe subsurface uncertainty and gives poor forecasts. An ensemble spanning the uncertain parameters is needed, but trying every combination means millions of models (abstract).

**D2**: A two-step workflow (abstract).
- Step 1 samples the local heterogeneity. The abstract gives the ensemble size as "103 to 105" realizations; the superscripts were lost, so this reads as 10^3 to 10^5. All realizations are constrained to the geostatistical data.
- The ensemble is screened by "dynamic fingerprinting": streamline time of flight or drainage time over a short time window.
- The fingerprints are clustered by principal flow pattern via SVD, and one representative is kept per cluster.
- Step 2 treats the global parameters (e.g. water-oil contact, fault transmissibility). For each representative, Design of Experiments builds proxy response surfaces, and MCMC conditions them to the production history.

**D8**: The result is a small set of models based on realistic geology. They keep the flow-relevant uncertainty and are conditioned to production data. The workflow is shown on a real field example (abstract). Numbers, validation and limits: not read.
- Transferable: Split a mixed uncertainty space by type. Reduce the high-dimensional, cell-based part by clustering cheap short-horizon dynamic signatures and keeping one representative per cluster. Then spend the expensive proxy-plus-MCMC sampling only on the few global parameters, once per representative (abstract).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅: an IBM–Shell workflow, published in the users' venue (SPE) and applied to a field case (abstract; Crossref affiliations).
- Method 5 ⚠: it uses the simulator's physics (streamline time of flight) to cut cost before black-box sampling. The structure serves ensemble screening and uncertainty quantification, not derivative-based optimization (abstract).
- New-pattern candidate: **flow-equivalence screening**. Realizations are clustered by their dynamic response, not by distance in parameter space. S142 uses the same device.

**Quotes**: "The concept behind this methodology is that for each distinct property realization, a given production schedule will generate a flow pattern which, like a fingerprint, is unique to that realization." (abstract) · "Each cluster aggregates a set of model realizations that despite their apparent difference in the model space, all correspond to similar principal flow trends." (abstract)

### S147 · Decision Support for Daily Production Optimization: In Genuine Real-Time (SPE Intelligent Energy International Conference and Exhibition 2014, DOI 10.2118/167861-ms)
**Read**: abstract (abstracts.json, via Crossref).

**Coauthors**: S. Ursin-Holm, A. Sandnes, V. Gunnerud and B. A. Foss (NTNU); F. Zenith (SINTEF ICT); A. F. Teixeira (Petrobras). Conn is listed with IBM Research (Crossref).

**Metadata**: paper SPE-167861-MS, 1 Apr 2014, 9 references (Crossref). The references include:
- the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente 2009 book;
- Gunnerud et al. 2013, "Embedding Structural Information in Simulation-based Optimization";
- Gunnerud et al. 2010, a piecewise-linear model solved with two decomposition strategies;
- the IO Center's "SmartOpt" and "SmartX" films (2013), and Zenith 2013 on SmartX well testing.

**D1**: The problem is production networks with a complex bottleneck structure (abstract). The engineer needs two things:
- the current state, e.g. the oil, gas and water flow from each well, which is often not easily measured;
- the effect of changes, e.g. to a choke valve, on the bottlenecks.

Until now, optimization tools have often been too slow to fit into the engineer's workflow (abstract).

**D2**: This is a discussion paper, not an algorithm paper. Its argument is that fast response times enable different workflows: instant re-optimization inside a well-optimization decision meeting. It supports this with "two most promising contributions" from the IO Center (abstract). The abstract does not name them. SmartOpt and SmartX in the reference list are likely candidates (inferred from references; not confirmed).

**D8**: The contribution is a discussion of the challenges and opportunities of online decision support tools. The abstract states that relying only on human interpretation is suboptimal and sometimes dangerous. Evaluation, results and limits: not read.
- Transferable (problem framing): Name the meeting or decision cadence in which the user will act on the recommendations. Make time-to-recommendation short enough to re-optimize live within it, and report that latency as a result (abstract).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅: the problem is framed from the production engineer's workflow and decision meeting. The paper was published at an SPE venue with NTNU, SINTEF and Petrobras coauthors. SKILL.md already records Conn's NTNU technical-board seat (abstract; Crossref).
- Method 5: the abstract does not support a link. The structure-embedding paper appears only in the reference list (Crossref).

**Quotes**: "These effects could be complex, and only relying on human interpretation when searching for the best production strategy is clearly suboptimal, and indeed, sometimes dangerous." (abstract) · "It highlights the potential of fast response times that enables totally different workflows, as a result of the fact that instant re-optimizations could be conducted in a well optimization decision meeting." (abstract)

### S063 · Solving air traffic conflict problems via local continuous optimization (European Journal of Operational Research 241(2):502–512, 2015, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2014.08.045)
**Read**: abstract (abstracts.json; the HAL notice hal-00912785 has the same text but no file).

**Coauthors**: Clément Peyronne, Marcel Mongeau, Daniel Delahaye (Crossref). The HAL keywords are semi-infinite programming, genetic algorithms, continuous optimization, B-splines and air traffic conflict problem (HAL notice).

**References**: 23 in total (Crossref). They include:
- Conn 1987, "An exact penalty function for semi-infinite programming";
- the CSV 2009 book and Powell's BOBYQA (2009);
- Stein 2012, "How to solve a semi-infinite optimization problem";
- Visweswariah et al. 2000, "Noise considerations in circuit optimization";
- genetic-algorithm, ant-colony and mixed-integer approaches to conflict resolution.

**D1**: Conflict resolution is traditionally solved with discretized, combinatorial methods. The paper sets out to show that a continuous formulation is viable on realistic problems (abstract).

**D2**: Trajectories are modelled with B-splines, and the separation constraint is written as a semi-infinite programming constraint. The resulting continuous formulation needs one optimization variable per aircraft trajectory. Local continuous optimization then improves the overall global optimization of this nonconvex problem (abstract). The HAL keyword "genetic algorithms" suggests that the global layer is a GA. The abstract does not say so (inferred from the HAL keywords).

**D8**: Numerical experiments on realistic test problems are called "encouraging". Local methods need fewer iterations and therefore fewer costly function evaluations (abstract). Problem sizes, baselines and the choice of local solver: not read.
- Transferable (modelling move): Give each agent's path a smooth parameterization (B-spline) driven by a single variable. Write pairwise separation as a semi-infinite constraint over continuous time. A combinatorial problem then becomes a small continuous NLP that a local solver can polish inside a global search (abstract).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅: continuous and semi-infinite machinery is carried into a domain dominated by combinatorial and metaheuristic methods. Conn's own 1987 semi-infinite exact-penalty paper is in the reference list (abstract; Crossref).
- Method 4 ✅: an application with air-traffic researchers (ENAC HAL deposit) published in an OR journal. SKILL.md already lists it under Method 4 practice (Crossref; HAL notice).
- Method 2 ✅, step 1 only: the cost unit is costly function evaluations (abstract).
- The corpus continues this line in S166 (ROADEF 2017) and S099 (2023) (inferred from title).

**Quotes**: "It involves very few optimization variables in that one needs only one optimization variable to determine each aircraft trajectory." (abstract) · "Not only does one not need to rely on the traditional, discretized, combinatorial optimization approaches to this problem, but, moreover, local continuous optimization methods, which require relatively fewer iterations and thereby fewer costly function evaluations, are shown to improve the performance of the overall global optimization of this non-convex problem." (abstract)

### S091 · Efficient solution of quadratically constrained quadratic subproblems within the mesh adaptive direct search algorithm (European Journal of Operational Research 268(1):13–24, 2018, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2017.10.058)
**Read**: abstract (abstracts.json). The same text is on Optimization Online 2016/11/5731 and GERAD G-2016-45, but neither now serves a PDF.

**Coauthors**: Nadir Amaioua, Charles Audet, Sébastien Le Digabel (Crossref). The GERAD report is dated June 2016, revised November 2016, 17 pages.

**References**: 59 in total (Crossref). They include:
- Conn 1973 (nondifferentiable penalty) and Coleman–Conn 1980 and 1982 (two papers);
- Conn–Gould–Toint 1994 (slack variables) and the 1996 LANCELOT experiments;
- Conn–Le Digabel 2013 and the CSV 2009 book;
- Moré–Wild 2009, Hestenes 1969 and Powell 1969.

**Thesis**: Amaioua's thesis thanks Andy Conn, whose ideas pushed the author to undertake the PhD (thesis p. iv).

**D1**: MADS's optional search step builds quadratic models, which produces a series of QCQP subproblems. The question is how to solve them efficiently by exploiting the structure of the quadratic models (abstract).

**D2**: The paper compares three solvers: an ℓ1 exact penalty, an augmented Lagrangian, and a new algorithm that combines the two (abstract). The Amaioua 2018 thesis, a related document and not the paper, adds two points:
- The combined "ℓ1 augmented Lagrangian" optimizes a piecewise quadratic problem instead of the quartic one that the standard augmented Lagrangian gives (thesis p. vi).
- Its inner loop is the Coleman–Conn ℓ1 exact-penalty inner loop, and its outer iteration is like that of the standard augmented Lagrangian (thesis p. 28).

**D8**: The methods are implemented in NOMAD and tested on 65 analytical problems and 4 simulation-based engineering applications (abstract). Thesis ch. 4 says its work was published in EJOR (thesis p. 30). Its list of figures shows performance and data profiles, the engineering problems AIRCRAFT_RANGE, LOCKWOOD, SIMPLIFIED_WING and WELDED, and "200 instances" of industrial applications (thesis p. xi). Which solver wins, and the paper's own limits: not read.
- Transferable (algorithm design, from the thesis): When the subproblem has quadratic constraints, put an ℓ1 penalty on the constraints inside the augmented Lagrangian. The merit function then stays piecewise quadratic, not quartic, and an existing ℓ1 exact-penalty inner solver can be reused (thesis pp. vi, 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅: Conn's ℓ1 exact penalty (1973, Coleman–Conn 1980/82) and augmented-Lagrangian ideas are reused inside the rival school's MADS. SKILL.md already cites this abstract. The card adds the reference-list evidence and the thesis statement that the Coleman–Conn inner loop is reused (abstract; Crossref; thesis p. 28).
- Method 2 ✅: two tiers of problems, 65 analytical and 4 simulation-based engineering (abstract).
- Method 1 ✅: the algorithms are delivered as NOMAD code (abstract).

**Quotes**: "One of the optional MADS features is a versatile search step in which quadratic models are built leading to a series of quadratically constrained quadratic subproblems." (abstract) · "These methods are implemented within the NOMAD software package and their impact are assessed through computational experiments on 65 analytical test problems and 4 simulation-based engineering applications." (abstract)

### S135 · A derivative-free exact penalty algorithm: basic ideas, convergence theory and computational studies (Computational and Applied Mathematics 41, article 56, 2022, DOI 10.1007/s40314-021-01748-4)
**Read**: abstract (abstracts.json, from Springer).

**Coauthors**: Caio Merlini Giuliani, Eduardo Camponogara. Published 22 Jan 2022 (Crossref). This is after Conn's death: Views-and-News 27(2) (November 2019) carries "In memory of Andy Conn" by Toint and Gould, p. 9 of that issue. The Giuliani 2019 UFSC thesis, advised by Camponogara, thanks Conn for help during a visit and acknowledges Petrobras support (thesis PDF p. 9).

**References**: 28 in total (Crossref). They include:
- Coleman–Conn 1980 and 1982, and the CST 1997 survey;
- CSV 2008 (two papers), CSV 2009 SIOPT and the 2009 book;
- Amaioua et al. 2018 (S091) and Audet–Dennis 2009 (progressive barrier);
- Powell 1994 (COBYLA) and Sampaio–Toint 2016 (trust funnel);
- Giuliani–Camponogara 2015 (gas-lift) and Camponogara et al. 2018 (offshore platform);
- CUTEst and NOMAD.

**D1**: Constrained problems where the gradients of the objective and the constraints are not available. Because this is a penalty method, iterates may be infeasible and the constraints need not be known explicitly (abstract).

**D2**: A derivative-free trust-region method on the ℓ1 exact penalty, which builds constraint models during the run. Under conditions on feasible problems, it converges directly to a constrained optimum once the penalty is large enough. The ℓ1 penalty is not differentiable near active constraints, so steps are taken in directions with guaranteed descent (abstract).

**D8**: The method is compared with other algorithms on analytical test problems, where good performance is reported, and results on one simulation-based problem are given. The abstract states its scope: modestly sized problems, where computing the search direction is not too expensive (abstract). Baselines, theorem statements and assumptions: not read.
- Transferable (algorithm design): Where an ℓ1 merit function has kinks near active constraints, accept only steps along directions with certified descent for the penalty. Then give the method's scope as a cost ratio: it suits problems where computing the direction is cheap (abstract).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅: Conn's own ℓ1 exact-penalty line (Coleman–Conn 1980 and 1982 are among the references) is carried into a model-based DFO trust region (abstract; Crossref).
- Method 1 ✅: the title's triad, "basic ideas, convergence theory and computational studies", packages theory and computation in one paper (abstract).
- Method 2 ✅: an analytical test set plus a simulation problem (abstract).
- Method 3: the abstract does not support a link. The CSV geometry papers appear only in the reference list (Crossref).
- Method 4 ⚠: the coauthors work on oil-production optimization with Petrobras support (inferred from the references and the thesis).

**Quotes**: "By the use of an exact penalty, under certain conditions for feasible problems, the method converges directly to a constrained optimum, once a sufficiently high penalty is selected." (abstract) · "To address the fact that the ℓ1-penalty used is not in general differentiable near the active constraints, steps are computed in directions with guaranteed descent." (abstract)
