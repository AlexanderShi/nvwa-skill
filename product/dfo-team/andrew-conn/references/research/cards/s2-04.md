# Paper cards · batch s2-04 (supplement)

Batch intent: Read the 1993–94 CGT cluster that surrounds the LANCELOT/CUTE period from four sides. (1) The field view: two versions of the group's large-scale survey (S086, written 1992; S061, 1994), to see how Conn's group framed the field and its own agenda. (2) The community view: a proposal for an open, bias-controlled comparison of small-scale NLP software on CUTE (S188). (3) The follow-up papers that S061 cites in its Further Developments section (§5) and nearby: restructuring partially separable functions (S085), an alternative first Newton step for barrier subproblems (S082), and augmented Lagrangian theory with linear constraints kept out of the penalty function (S107, the local half, is cited; its global half S113 is not in S061's reference list). (4) A multifrontal linear-algebra study (S049), which turned out to be unreadable. The batch tests Method 1 (theory–code–test), Method 2 (test bed as infrastructure), Method 5 (structure before black box), Method 6 (transplanting machinery) and a pre-DFO variant of Method 3, and it looks in the survey for early signs of the later DFO turn.

### S086 · Large-scale nonlinear constrained optimization (Linear Algebra for Large Scale and Real-Time Applications 1993, DOI 10.1007/978-94-015-8196-7_3)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nick Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: LANCELOT period of the CGT trio. The text was written in 1992: it expects the test set to be on netlib this year (1992) (p. 15), after the LANCELOT book [41] and before CUTE appeared. It is the first version of the survey that S061 updates. **Read**: main text in full (pp. 1–18). The reference list (pp. 18–29, 137 entries) was not read, except the self-citations [31]–[45] (pp. 20–22).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry is a gap between progress and practice. Ten years of progress in constrained NLP meet considerable obstacles at large scale, visible in the paucity of software even for large general QPs (abstract, p. 1).
  - The survey is framed as a personal response to three questions: why large-scale NLP matters, what the difficulties are, and what progress has been made (p. 1).
  - *Large* is defined along four axes: the machine, the problem's nonlinearity, its structure, and how often the same class is solved. Fifty variables is small; 5000 variables with 1000 nonlinear constraints is large (p. 2).
  - The motive is stated as need: much of the joint effort went into writing software, preparing input and testing (pp. 2–3). Linear models are often used only because the simplex method is well understood (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & design of the survey**:
  - Difficulties first (§3, pp. 4–6): small-scale algorithms do not transfer; structure must be exploited, and doing so complicates stability; scaling; global versus fast local convergence with little information per iteration; how to input structure; test problems; how to evaluate results.
  - Then approaches, grouped by parent technology (p. 6): large-scale LP (simplex, MINOS, GRG, SLP; pp. 6–9), small-scale NLP (SQP and LANCELOT; pp. 9–16), and mixed LP/interior point (p. 16).
  - LANCELOT is the worked example (pp. 10–16). It uses a trust region on simple bounds with a generalized Cauchy point on the projected-gradient path, an augmented Lagrangian for the general constraints, and group partial separability for storage and matrix–vector products (pp. 10–12).
  - Linear-algebra stance: invariant subspaces matter more than sparsity. The example is x₅₀⁴ versus Σxᵢ⁴: one Hessian is sparse, the other dense, but both have an (n−1)-dimensional invariant subspace (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions**: problem (1.1)–(1.4) with f and c_j twice continuously differentiable and possibly infinite bounds (pp. 1–2). Only local minimizers are sought; the authors argue these suffice in many situations and that practice often wants marked improvement, not assured optimality (pp. 2–3).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no proofs. Results are summarised with pointers:
  - convergence to a Kuhn–Tucker point, and finite identification of the active bounds under strict complementarity [31] (pp. 11–12);
  - for the augmented Lagrangian: first-order stationarity; with a single limit point the penalty parameter stops decreasing; asymptotically one inner iteration per outer iteration [38], [42] (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. Figure 1 lists 19 applications solved with LANCELOT, giving only n and m (n up to 15625, m up to 7000). Detailed analysis is promised for a separate report (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a field map plus a position statement. Large NLPs are solvable on workstations, two packages (LANCELOT, MINOS) exist, and input formats matter (p. 18).
  - Stated limits: the overview is not meant to be complete, and length of treatment signals novelty, not importance (p. 1). Degeneracy needs more research (p. 16). User interfaces have been neglected (p. 18).
  - Future work on LANCELOT: a Lagrangian barrier method [44], structured trust regions [39], interior points for linear constraints, and more testing (pp. 15–16).
  - Positioning: broad citation (137 references). Rival codes are described neutrally: MINOS is called a bridge from LP to NLP (p. 7), and SQP methods hold promise but lack computational experience (p. 10).
  - Writing: first-person plural and openly persuasive; the authors say it is one of their tasks to convince the reader to solve nonlinear models (p. 3). Martin Beale's remark on algorithms versus computers is used as the motto (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence, stated): the authors say their joint effort went into software, input and testing as well as theory (pp. 2–3). The LANCELOT theory [31], [38], [42], the code [41] and the test set [40] are presented as one package (pp. 12–15).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): test sets are a primary requirement. The group has over 900 instances in its standard input format and asks the community for more (p. 6). The input format itself is treated as a research problem (pp. 5–6).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): large problems are solvable only because they are structured, and structure means more than sparsity (p. 5; p. 14).
- Existing candidate *Survey as a research agenda* ✅: the survey closes its LANCELOT section with the group's own next projects (pp. 15–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before surveying a field, define its key notion (here *large*) along several explicit axes (machine, nonlinearity, structure, frequency of solves), with two anchor examples (p. 2).
- Organise a survey as difficulties → approach classes by parent technology → one worked system in depth → other issues → a short conclusion (pp. 4–18).
- Separate a concept from its usual proxy with a two-function example: sparse and dense Hessians that share the same invariant subspace (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "The fact that we are able to solve large problems at all is because they are structured." (p. 5)
- "Prefering to solve linear models, only because we understand how to solve large linear programs, should no longer be the normal practice." (p. 18)

### S061 · Large-scale nonlinear constrained optimization: a current survey (Algorithms for continuous optimization: the state of the art 1994, DOI 10.1007/978-94-009-0369-2_10)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: an updated and expanded S086, written for a volume with companion chapters by Bartholomew-Biggs, Fletcher, Björck, Shanno, Dixon and Schnabel (pp. 2, 4, 6, 18, 26). It now features CUTE and cites this batch's 1993 follow-ups as further developments: S049 = Conn et al. (1993a), S085 = (1993d), S107 = (1993e), S082 = (1993g) (reference list, p. 33). **Read**: main text in full (pp. 1–27). The references (pp. 27–44) were not read, except the Conn et al. 1993a–g entries (p. 33).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Same entry as S086 (progress versus large-scale obstacles), but the abstract now names software, numerical linear algebra and testing as topics, with emphasis on LANCELOT and CUTE (p. 1).
  - New question: is it still worthwhile to design algorithms that are unsuitable for large problems? The answer is yes, for example when function evaluations are very expensive or the problem is extremely nonlinear or badly scaled (p. 3). *My note*: this is two years before Conn–Toint 1996 opened the DFO line, where the expensive evaluation is the whole point.
  - The difficulty list adds that some of the hardest problems are small: LANCELOT cannot solve a problem with 149 variables and 100 nonlinear constraints (p. 3). Practitioners prefer good solutions to bad models, and users must be sophisticated (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & design**:
  - §2 background (pp. 4–7): steepest descent versus Newton as global versus asymptotic behaviour; PSB as a minimum-norm problem; sparse quasi-Newton updating failed, partitioned updating works; limited memory; direct (multifrontal, modified sparse Cholesky) versus iterative (PCG) linear algebra.
  - §3 existing methods (pp. 7–12): MINOS, transformed-Hessian SQP, the SBMIN trust-region kernel, a formal definition of group partial separability, and the augmented Lagrangian outer loop.
  - §4 testing environment: CUTE (pp. 12–14).
  - §5 further developments, told from a failed run. The discrete plate problem HAIFAL has 343 variables and 8958 inequalities, which become 9301 variables with slacks, and LANCELOT's defaults needed 117 hours (p. 15). This motivates the Lagrangian barrier (pp. 15–17). Then come a linear-algebra rearrangement that makes the slack augmented Lagrangian as cheap as the barrier (pp. 17–18), structured trust regions, element merging (S085), iterated subspace minimization, and linear constraints kept outside the augmented Lagrangian (S113/S107) (pp. 18–22).
  - §6 colleagues' work (pp. 22–26), including Biegler et al.'s implementation of Coleman–Conn (1982, 1984) (p. 23).
- **D3 Assumptions**: same problem class and smoothness as S086; local minimizers only (pp. 1–2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no proofs. Two derivations motivate designs:
  - the augmented Lagrangian is the classical Lagrangian of a shifted quadratic-penalty reformulation, and the log barrier is treated the same way (Polyak's form) (pp. 15–17);
  - a block factorisation reduces the Newton system of the slack augmented Lagrangian to one system in B (5.17)–(5.19) (p. 18).
- **D5 Experience reported** (not a controlled study):
  - HAIFAL: LANCELOT defaults 117 h; modified barrier (Jensen et al., already somewhat tuned) 31 min 54 s; LANCELOT with a tuned penalty parameter 4 h 24 min 28 s; MINOS 5.3 2 h 36 min 30 s; MINOS 5.4 1 h 30 min (pp. 15, 17).
  - Iterated subspace minimization against default LANCELOT on 39 unconstrained CUTE problems (about 1000 variables on average): at least twice as fast 11 times, at least ten times as fast twice, twice as slow 5 times, the rest comparable. Problems over 30 CPU minutes and variants were excluded (pp. 20–21).
  - Changing the initial trust-region radius from one to two can change solution time by orders of magnitude (p. 15).
  - LANCELOT's own tests of all options on about 1000 instances conclude that it is robust and that SR1 is the best quasi-Newton update in that trust-region setting (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a field map, an advertisement for CUTE, and the CGT group's agenda. Packages designed for large problems can also be excellent on small ones (p. 26).
  - Self-critique: LANCELOT A's major defect is its handling of linear constraints (p. 21). A quadratic model is a poor model of a log barrier (p. 18). The success of naive preconditioners and limited memory may reflect limited testing on well-scaled problems (p. 6). In the MINOS–LANCELOT comparison the authors have more expertise with LANCELOT, and MINOS's basis is older technology (p. 25). They say their biases show (p. 27).
  - Normative close: practical algorithms need evidence of competitiveness on significant problems, and large-scale claims need large tests (p. 27).
  - Positioning: colleagues' work gets its own section, and others' conclusions are recorded even where they are unfavourable to MINOS (Eldersveld et al., p. 25).
  - Writing: the further-developments section opens with a story, introduced as a tale (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence, stated): the closing rule on publishing practical algorithms (p. 27), and testing all LANCELOT options on about 1000 instances (p. 25).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): §4 lists what a testing environment needs: a large managed database, comparison with the best packages, facilities to test ideas, and free availability (p. 13). CUTE has interfaces to nine external codes (pp. 13–14) and made it quick to test iterated subspace minimization (p. 21).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): the cost unit is CPU time, the iterated-subspace comparison excludes long runs (p. 21), and there is no noisy tier.
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): exact second derivatives come through structure. The authors' experience is that preferring quasi-Newton to exact second derivatives is very seldom necessary (footnote, p. 5).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the shift idea is carried from the quadratic penalty to the log barrier (pp. 15–17). Conn's 1982/84 reduced-Hessian method is reused by Biegler et al. (p. 23).
- Existing candidate **Self-audit of one's own solver starts a research line** ✅: the 117-hour default run motivates the barrier work (p. 15).
- Existing candidate *Survey as a research agenda* ✅ (pp. 15–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Open a further-developments section with one concrete failed run (problem name, size, hours), then show how each new idea changes that number, including the tuned run of your own code (pp. 15–17).
- When you compare against a rival while owning one of the codes, state your expertise bias and the rival's age in the same paragraph (p. 25).
- Report a win/loss tally against the default at fixed ratios (≥2×, ≥10×, ≤0.5×), with the exclusion rule in a footnote (pp. 20–21).
- Motivate a new merit function in a few lines by showing it is the classical Lagrangian of a reformulated (shifted) constraint set (pp. 15–17).

**Quotes**:
- "nobody should be publishing papers whose main purpose is to describe an algorithm that is intended to be practically useful, unless they also provide evidence that the algorithm is competitive on significant problems." (p. 27)
- "relatively innocuous seeming changes, like changing the initial trust region size from one to two, may change the solution time by several orders of magnitude." (p. 15)

### S188 · A Comparison for Small-Scale Software Nonlinear Optimization Software (CSSNOS) preliminary project outline (FUNDP 1994, no DOI/arXiv; Report 94/4)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: Report 94/4, 16 February 1994, with Conn at IBM T.J. Watson (p. 1). It was written after the idea came up at the Il Ciocco NATO conference in September 1993 (p. 2) and before CUTE appeared in ACM TOMS (listed as to appear, p. 11). It is a project proposal, not a research paper. **Read**: in full (pp. 1–12). Whether the project was carried out is not in the text.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Too little is known about the relative computational merits of small-scale NLP methods. A researcher with a new idea cannot compare with the best available results. The Hock–Schittkowski comparison is 13 years old and does not include today's popular codes (p. 2).
  - CUTE makes a new comparison realistic, both for choosing test problems and for interfacing many packages (p. 2).
  - Four aims: a state-of-the-art baseline for new approaches; attention on problem classes that no software solves; convincing practitioners; and a comparison of popular codes such as UNCMIN and NPSOL (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core design**:
  - Governance: a *core group* of 4–5 people manages the project and runs the tests; a *council* writes internal referee's reports so that the results can be accepted as unbiased (p. 4). Participants are expected to submit their own software (p. 4).
  - An open call with a fixed protocol, run inside CUTE on SIF problems. Automation serves three purposes: the harness can be sent to submitters, personal intervention (and bias) is minimised, and the comparison can be rerun in later years (p. 3).
  - Scope: small problems, Fortran, sequential machines. Large-scale and parallel testing are deferred for lack of consensus on how to represent structure (p. 4).
  - Submissions: source, a CUTE driver, specification files and a log of a preliminary run. A report tool records the name, SUCCESS/FAILED and the final objective value (p. 6). At most 5 algorithmic instances per code, the default instance compulsory, double precision, IEEE machine constants (p. 7).
  - Timeline: announce in MP, SIOPT, JOTA, Opt-Net and NANET; 6 months for submitters; final report 6 months after the deadline (p. 7).
- **D3 Measurement assumptions**:
  - Reliability = number of problems solved. Efficiency = normalised CPU time and the number of function (and derivative) evaluations. Iterations are rejected because an iteration means different things in different algorithms (pp. 4–5).
  - Timing noise on small problems: repeat each run until the accumulated CPU time passes a threshold, or use the total time over the whole set (p. 5).
  - No iteration limit; a uniform execution-time limit instead. The common accuracy level is left open, with a question mark (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis**: none (no theory).
- **D5 Protocol** (no results):
  - The test list is fixed in advance from CUTE. Parametrised problems are run at three sizes: below 10, around 50, just below 100. Starting points are also scaled by 10 and 100 (pp. 5–6). Problem classes (unconstrained, bounds, linear, general) are still under discussion (p. 6).
  - Harness: a shell script and one result file per code. A new CUTE common block counts f, g and H evaluations. A STATS.d record holds date, software, problem and dimensions, outcome, the f/g/H counts and the final objective (p. 8). A crash is detected when STATS.d is missing, CPU time is below the limit, and a core file exists (p. 9).
  - Analysis: reliability tables flag failures and convergence to alternative solutions. Performance is compared either on the subset solved by a sufficiently large share of codes (50%?, p. 9) or by counting how often each code is best, second best and so on, with failures in the worst class (p. 9). LaTeX tables and graphics files are generated automatically (p. 9).
  - Across machines: use one machine or identical ones, and a frozen reference package on a fixed problem set as the time yardstick (pp. 9–10).
  - Possible extension: a public performance database for authors and referees (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: an infrastructure and governance proposal, explicitly built on the LANCELOT testing experience [3], [4] (pp. 8, 11).
  - Limits: the note does not claim completeness (p. 3). It ends with a list of open issues: test set, C codes, stopping conditions, a CPU benchmark, timing, starting-point variation, presentation of results, and workload (pp. 10–11).
  - Writing: tentative throughout (it seems; parameters with question marks), as an invitation to debate. Discussions with Biggs, Buckley, Fourer, Gould and Nocedal are acknowledged (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (strong evidence): a community test bed with a public protocol treated as a research product (pp. 3–9). The cost unit is evaluations and CPU time, not iterations (Method 2 step 1; pp. 4–5). Variants are submitted separately with the default compulsory (step 5; p. 7).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): harder instances come from distant starting points (×10, ×100), not from noise, and there is no engineering tier (pp. 5–6).
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): the protocol reuses the harness of the LANCELOT testing twin reports [3], [4] (pp. 8, 11).
- Heuristic 7 ✅ (release the test environment): the harness is to be handed to submitters (p. 3).
- Existing candidate *solver-agnostic evaluation layer* ✅: every code is run through CUTE's interfacing tools (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *bias-controlled open benchmark*: two-level governance (core group plus a council of referees), an automated harness, a test list fixed in advance, and a cap on variants per code (pp. 3–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Fix and publish the test-problem list before accepting submissions (p. 5).
- Cap the number of variants per code (here 5) and make the default configuration compulsory (p. 7).
- Detect crashes automatically: missing stats file + CPU below the limit + core file present (p. 9).
- Use a time limit, not an iteration limit, when codes' iterations are not comparable (pp. 5, 8).
- Normalise across machines by timing a frozen reference solver on a fixed problem set (p. 10).
- Summarise by counting how often each code is best, second best, and so on, with failures in the worst class (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "the amount of personal intervention (and thus of bias) during the testing procedure would be kept to a minimum" (p. 3)
- "This possible comparison should be extremely helpful for authors in evaluating the relevance of their algorithmic ideas, and for referees in their task to assess that relevance." (p. 10)

### S085 · Improving the decomposition of partially separable functions in the context of large-scale optimization: a first approach (Large Scale Optimization: State of the Art 1994, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4613-3632-7_5)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nick Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: FUNDP Report 93/15, 16 June 1993 (p. 1). A LANCELOT follow-up; S061 cites it as Conn et al. (1993d) among further developments (S061 pp. 19–20). **Read**: in full (pp. 1–12).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry reverses a standing assumption. Structure is usually taken as given and the algorithm exploits it; here the given structure is modified to improve performance (p. 2). The analogy is Chang and McCormick's work on making sparse matrices sparser, which the authors say has not been much studied in optimization (p. 2).
  - The target is precise: the cost of Hessian–vector products in the conjugate-gradient inner solver of a truncated Newton method, as in LANCELOT (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm**:
  - Two operations on f = Σ fᵢ: *merging* elements, i.e. partial assembly between the fully assembled and fully disaggregated products, and *expansion* of an element from internal to elemental variables (pp. 3–4).
  - Closed-form flop rules. Expand if nᵢ² ≤ 2dᵢ + pᵢ² (3.1) (p. 5). Merge two elements if the overlap area n_ik² is at least the area of the two off-diagonal zero blocks, 2(n_k − n_ik)(nᵢ − n_ik) (3.2) (pp. 5–6). A general test (3.4) uses per-element costs cᵢ (3.3) (p. 6).
  - The optimal merge is a large set-covering problem. Instead a greedy pass goes variable by variable, merging the first element with each later one when (3.4) holds, and repeats until no merge occurs (p. 7). All trivial groups are also merged into one (pp. 7–8).
  - Only aggregation is done. Disaggregation would need the user to refine the model (p. 4). The aim is to repair unfortunate user decompositions, not good ones (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f convex and C² for the motivation. The questions stay valid in the nonconvex case, where CG is used with modification or inside a trust region (pp. 3–4). The vector v is dense (p. 4). Efficiency is measured only by flop count (a flop = add–multiply pair), as an a-priori test before rewriting a code (pp. 4–5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no theorems. The arguments are cost comparisons read off block pictures (Figures 1–2, pp. 5–6). A naive pairwise scan costs O(m²) (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - A Fortran implementation was applied to CUTE problems. On a large number of them the structure was left unchanged; the changes on some of the others are reported, ten problems by my count of the table rows (pp. 8–10).
  - Table 1, trivial groups: e.g. NCB20B goes from 1000 groups to 1 and from 19620 linear coefficients to 1000 (pp. 8–9).
  - Table 2, elements/flops/storage: NCB20B goes from 1981 to 6 elements, from 393400 to 209025 flops per product, and from 207010 to 105060 storage (p. 10). Flop gains exceed 10% on all problems except HILBERTB (p. 9).
  - Expansion can triple storage, while merging reduces both storage and arithmetic (p. 10).
  - No solver run times are given. Relevance is argued from NCB20B needing 3819 CG iterations under LANCELOT's defaults (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a concept (merge/expand), a simple algorithm, and evidence on CUTE (p. 10).
  - Limits: the authors call the scope very limited (p. 11). The dependence on element ordering is unknown (p. 11). The criterion is flops only; effects on partitioned quasi-Newton updates and on parallel granularity are left open (p. 11).
  - Positioning: builds on Griewank–Toint partial separability (p. 2), and points to Toint (1987) for how element definition affects partitioned updating (p. 11).
  - Writing: a modest title (*a first approach*), and the assumption reversal stated in the first paragraph (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence, extended): not only exploit the structure, but improve the structure the user gave (p. 2).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): CUTE is the test bed, and the paper reports that most problems were unchanged before tabulating the rest (p. 8).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): an idea, a Fortran code and CUTE numbers, but no theory is needed and there are no end-to-end solver timings, only a-priori flop counts (pp. 5, 8–10).
- New-pattern candidate: *treat a given structure as a design variable* (reverse a standing assumption) (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before rewriting a solver, test an idea with an a-priori cost model (flops and storage) on a public test set (p. 5).
- Turn a data-structure choice into a geometric rule that fits in one sentence: merge when the overlap area is at least the zero off-diagonal area (p. 6).
- When the exact version is combinatorial, name it (set covering), then use a simple greedy pass that stops at a fixed point (p. 7).
- Say how many test problems were unaffected, then tabulate before/after for the affected ones (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "However, it is frequently assumed that the problem's structure is given, and that a good algorithm has to exploit it." (p. 2)
- "Despite its direct practical interest, we of course realize that the research described in this paper is very limited in scope." (p. 11)

### S082 · A note on using alternative second-order models for the subproblems arising in barrier function methods for minimization (Numerische Mathematik 1994, DOI 10.1007/s002110050046)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nick Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: FUNDP Report 93/16 (revised, 13 October 1993), dedicated to J. Stoer's sixtieth birthday (p. 1). S061 cites it as Conn et al. (1993g) for evidence that Lagrangian barrier methods beat standard barriers on bound constraints (S061 p. 18). **Read**: §§1–3, 4.1, 4.3, 5 and 6 in full; §4.4 in part (the estimates on pp. 12–14 skimmed); §4.2 (Jittorntrum–Osborne, pp. 9–10) not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: a known anomaly. Each barrier subproblem is warm-started at the previous approximate minimizer, and Wright (1993) showed that a full Newton step from there will very likely cross the constraint boundary (p. 3). The paper's claim is that this happens because standard Newton is one, asymptotically poor, member of a whole class of Newton methods (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Write stationarity of the barrier function as a primal-dual system in (x, λ) with (cᵢ + sᵢ)λᵢ = wᵢ (3.4)–(3.5). Eliminating Δλ gives a Newton matrix whose left-hand side depends on the chosen λ (3.7) (p. 5).
  - Standard Newton takes λ from the new weights and shifts (3.8). The alternative takes λ from the previous weights and shifts (3.9), so the dual residual is already small by the inner stopping rule (p. 6).
  - λ is not updated with Δλ; the system is only used to generate a different first correction (p. 5).
  - In the code the alternative is used only for the first step of each inner iteration, after which standard Newton resumes (p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f and cᵢ at least C² (p. 2). The paper is openly non-rigorous: all square systems are assumed nonsingular (p. 3). Weight and shift sequences are specific to each method: traditional w = μ (p. 7); Polyak and Lagrangian barrier with exponent α (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: order-of-magnitude arguments following Wright (1993).
  - Traditional barrier: after the standard step the active constraints are about (2 − μᵏ/μᵏ⁺¹)c_A, which is negative if μ is cut by more than half; after the alternative step they are about (μᵏ⁺¹/μᵏ)c_A, safely feasible (p. 8).
  - The barrier gradient after the alternative step is O((μᵏ)²/μᵏ⁺¹). One further Newton step then meets the stopping rule if μᵏ = o((μᵏ⁺¹)^{3/4}) (p. 9).
  - For the Lagrangian barrier the alternative is not crucial; its estimate (4.47) is only slightly better than (4.42) (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Code: Gould's HSL code VE14 for bound-constrained QPs, with three variants: traditional, Jittorntrum–Osborne, and Lagrangian barrier with α = 0.5 (p. 14). The inner solver is a backtracking line search on a convexified model factorised by MA27 (p. 15).
  - Problems: four large CUTE problems: BQPGAUSS (n = 2003, nonconvex), JNLBRNGA (n = 15625), OBSTCLBM (n = 15625), TORSION1 (n = 14884). The other large CUTE problems suited to direct methods are said to be variants of these (p. 16). Runs on a SUN Sparc 10, stopped when the projected gradient is below 10⁻⁶ (p. 16).
  - Measures: inner and outer iterations, CPU time, factorizations and backtracks (Tables 1–4, pp. 14–17).
  - Results: up to about 25% improvement, and significantly fewer backtracks for the two unshifted methods (pp. 16–17). Gains are not uniform: on TORSION1 the Lagrangian-barrier backtracks rise from 79 to 115 with the alternative (p. 17). These are the first reported results for Lagrangian barrier methods, which beat the unshifted methods in almost all tests (p. 17). Runs with more alternative steps were less successful and are not reported (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a reinterpretation plus a one-line algorithmic change, adopted as the VE14 default (p. 16), with similar signs in the unfinished VE19 (p. 17).
  - Limits: no rigorous theory (p. 3); four test problems, all bound-constrained QPs (p. 16).
  - Positioning: it explains Wright's (1993) difficulty (p. 3), transplants Gould's (1989) starting-point idea for the quadratic penalty (p. 5), and relates the system to McCormick's (1991) primal-dual methods (p. 6).
  - Writing: a short note that disclaims rigour up front (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence, the triad in miniature): a derivation, an HSL code option that became the default, and CUTE numbers (pp. 5–6, 14–17).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): Gould's warm-start device for the quadratic penalty is carried to barrier functions (p. 5).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): CUTE problems are used, but only four; the other large candidates are dismissed as variants (p. 16). The less successful extension is mentioned but its numbers are not shown (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *embed the standard method in a family, then pick another member* (pp. 3, 5–6). Related to the existing *parametrized family whose extremes are the rival classical methods*, but here the family is used to repair the standard method rather than to interpolate between rivals.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Rewrite a primal Newton system as a primal-dual system with a free dual estimate. Choose the estimate that makes the residual vanish asymptotically, and use it only for the first step after a parameter change (pp. 5–6, 16).
- Diagnose warm-start failures with a first-order Taylor estimate of the active constraint values after the step (p. 8).
- Report factorizations and backtracks as well as iterations and time, to show where a change in step quality acts (pp. 14–17).

**Quotes**:
- "the standard Newton method is actually an asymptotically inappropriate member of a whole class of Newton methods for the subproblem." (p. 3)
- "Our intention here is not to produce a completely rigorous theory, merely to indicate the possible advantages of viewing Newton's method" (p. 3)

### S113 · Global convergence of two augmented Lagrangian algorithms for optimization with a combination of general equality and linear constraints (CERFACS 1993, no DOI/arXiv; Technical Report TR/PA/93/26, read as FUNDP Report 93/01)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nick Gould, A. Sartenaer, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: FUNDP Report 93/01, 5 July 1993 (p. 2), the global half of a pair whose local half is S107. It extends CGT 1991 (SINUM, bounds only) to general linear inequalities, and uses the criticality measure of the SIOPT 1993 inexact-projection paper (S031) (pp. 3, 5). **Read**: §§1–4 in full; §5 statements, plus the proofs of Lemma 5.4 and Theorem 5.7; §6 algorithms and Theorem 6.2. The §6 proof details (pp. 23–24) were skimmed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: LANCELOT keeps only simple bounds outside the augmented Lagrangian and puts general linear inequalities inside it. That is inefficient when good techniques exist for handling linear constraints directly, especially at large scale (pp. 3–4). The aim is to keep linear constraints explicit at the subproblem level. This also gives flexibility for constraint sets that behave non-uniformly (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm**:
  - Subproblem: approximately minimize the augmented Lagrangian (1.4) over the polyhedron B defined by the linear inequalities (p. 3). Stop when the criticality measure π_k ≤ ω_k, where π is the magnitude of the best linearized decrease over B ∩ unit ball (2.8) (p. 5).
  - Algorithm 3.1 uses the first-order multiplier update. Algorithm 3.2 accepts any multiplier estimate, with a control on its growth (pp. 6–8).
  - §5 gives cheaper stopping tests: the projected gradient on the tangent cone of the *dominant* constraints, i.e. those within κ₄ω_k of being active (5.4) (pp. 14–15); sign-checked dominant multipliers from a least-squares solve (5.17) (pp. 17–18); and the reduced-gradient test with non-positive active multipliers (5.21), which LSNNO, VE09, VE14 and VE19 already use (p. 18).
  - §6: the equality constraints are split into q subsets with separate penalty parameters, disaggregated multiplier/penalty updates, and aggregated tolerance updates (pp. 20–22).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - AS1: B is nonempty. AS2: f and cᵢ are C² on B (p. 4).
  - AS3: the iterates lie in a closed bounded domain. AS4: J(x*)Z* has column rank at least m at every limit point, which generalizes the AS3 of the 1991 paper (p. 9).
  - The ℓ₂ norm in (2.8) is only for exposition; any uniformly equivalent norms work (p. 25).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Lemmas 4.1–4.2 are taken unchanged from the 1991 paper, because their proofs never touch what has changed (p. 9).
  - The new Lemma 4.3 bounds the reduced gradient, ‖Z*ᵀ∇Φ_k‖ ≤ κ₁ω_k. Its proof builds a feasible direction along ZZᵀ∇Φ, scaled by min{1, δ/α}, and compares it with π_k (pp. 9–11).
  - Lemma 4.5 uses continuity of the optimal value of a convex parametric problem (Fiacco) (pp. 11–12). The key Lemma 4.6 bounds the multiplier errors and the constraint violation (pp. 12–13). The proof of Theorem 4.7 is the 1991 proof (p. 14).
  - Theorem 5.3 is proved by revisiting the two places where test (3.1) was used (p. 17). Theorem 5.7 uses the Moreau decomposition (p. 19). Lemmas 5.1 and 5.4 and Theorems 5.2 and 5.7 do not depend on the form of the augmented Lagrangian (p. 20).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: global convergence is kept when linear constraints stay explicit, when practical stopping rules are used, and when penalties are partitioned (p. 25).
  - Limits: no numbers. The local analysis is deferred to the companion paper (p. 25).
  - Positioning: extends Conn et al. (1991), cites Fletcher (1987) for the advantage of the strategy (p. 4), and contrasts its multiple-penalty scheme with Powell's, which uses the ℓ₁ norm and updates multipliers only when all constraints are within tolerance (p. 20).
  - Writing: lemma headers credit their source, e.g. [Conn et al. (1991), Lemma 4.1] (p. 9). The least-squares multiplier estimate is flagged as an analytical device, not a computable quantity (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): the theory covers a class (two algorithms, four stopping tests) and is tied to the tests of existing codes (p. 18). LANCELOT's parameter values are quoted (p. 8).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence, step 4: handle cheap constraints exactly): linear constraints stay out of the augmented Lagrangian, so its Hessian sparsity is unaffected (pp. 4, 25).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the 1991 proof machinery is reused (pp. 9, 14), and the SIOPT 1993 convex-constraint trust-region algorithm serves as the inner solver (p. 8).
- Method 3 ⚠ (variant, pre-DFO): the condition that carries the theorem is a computable inner-iteration criticality test, and the paper shows which cheap tests imply it (pp. 5, 14–19).
- Existing candidate *theory for a device practitioners already use* ✅: convergence is proved for the stopping tests already in LSNNO, VE09, VE14 and VE19 (p. 18).
- Existing candidate *assumption audit → single-lemma extension* ✅: reuse the old proof and re-prove only the lemma the change touches (pp. 9, 14, 17).
- Existing candidate *inexact subproblem with a checkable certificate* ✅: sign-checked least-squares multipliers certify inner stationarity and bound the tolerated inexactness in one test (pp. 17–18).
- Existing candidate **Split by type, give each block its natural treatment** ✅: linear constraints go to the subproblem, nonlinear ones to the penalty function (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure subproblem criticality as the best linearized decrease over the feasible set ∩ unit ball, then prove that cheaper practical tests imply it (pp. 5, 14–19).
- Treat constraints within κω of being active as *dominant* in the stopping test, so it tolerates near-activity (p. 14).
- When you extend a published convergence proof, list which lemmas stay valid and why, and prove only the counterpart lemma (pp. 9, 14).
- Give separate penalty parameters to constraint subsets that behave differently, with per-subset multiplier updates (pp. 20–22).

**Quotes**:
- "In the worst case, global convergence is ensured by driving the penalty parameter to zero, in which case the algorithms essentially reduce to the quadratic penalty function method" (p. 8)
- "It is merely introduced as an analytical device." (p. 6)

### S107 · Local convergence properties of two augmented Lagrangian algorithms for optimization with a combination of general equality and linear constraints (CERFACS 1993, no DOI/arXiv; Technical Report TR/PA/93/27, read as FUNDP Report 93/20)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nick Gould, A. Sartenaer, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: FUNDP Report 93/20, dated 5 July 1993 like S113 (p. 2). It is the local companion of S113 and closes the gap to the bounds-only theory of 1991 (p. 4). **Read**: §§1–3 in full; §4 statements, the proof of Lemma 4.4 (pp. 14–16), Lemma 4.6 and the opening of the proof of Theorem 4.7 (pp. 18–19); the proofs of Theorems 4.8 and 4.9 skimmed; §§5–6 in full.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: complete the global theory of S113 with local properties: the rate of convergence and the boundedness of the potentially troublesome penalty parameters (p. 2), for the multi-penalty formulation (1.5) (p. 4). Preliminary experiments with general linear constraints are called encouraging, and fuller tests are promised once a robust linearly constrained code exists (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm**:
  - Same framework as S113. The inner stopping rule is the projected gradient on the tangent cone of the dominant constraints (3.5) (p. 7). It covers the tests in LSNNO, VE09, VE14 and VE19 (p. 7), and the stronger rule π ≤ ω² (p. 10).
  - The penalty reduction factor τ_{k,j} is τ for the subsets holding the largest penalty parameter and min(τ, μ_k) for the others (3.10) (p. 8). This balances the constraint violations gradually, not at every major iteration as in Powell (1969) (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - AS1–AS4 as in S113 (pp. 4, 10).
  - AS5: Lipschitz second derivatives at limit points. AS6: the reduced KKT matrix is nonsingular for every choice of weakly active constraints, described as a safeguard against small perturbations (p. 13).
  - Theorems 4.7–4.9 need the whole sequence to converge to a single limit point. This cannot be relaxed, by the 1991 counterexample (p. 24).
  - AS7: the reduced Hessian is uniformly positive definite; used for the second-order result (p. 24).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Active-set identification: *strongly active* constraints, whose removal would destroy criticality, are eventually dominant (Theorem 4.2, via the Moreau decomposition, p. 12). Under non-degeneracy the dominant set equals the active set (Corollary 4.3, pp. 12–13).
  - Error bounds (Lemma 4.4, pp. 14–16): there are finitely many possible dominant sets, so fix one along a subsequence (p. 14). Taylor-expand the gradient and the constraints, write a KKT-like system, split it into null-space and range-space parts, drop the block that the stopping rule makes vanish, and invert the reduced KKT matrix (AS6).
  - Penalty boundedness by contradiction: show that the multiplier-update step is eventually always taken (Theorem 4.7, pp. 19–21, following the 1991 technique). Then every subset's penalty is bounded away from zero (Theorem 4.8, pp. 21–23).
  - Rate: R-linear convergence with an R-factor governed by the final penalty parameter μ_min (Theorem 4.9, p. 23; the exponent is lost in the text extraction).
  - Second order: convergence to an isolated local solution under AS7 (Theorem 5.1, pp. 24–25).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. Preliminary experiments are only mentioned (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a local theory for general linear constraints that matches the bounds-only case (p. 25). It is claimed to be practical because its stopping rule covers existing packages (p. 25).
  - Limits: a single limit point is required (p. 24); implementable least-squares multipliers need non-degeneracy (p. 23); no numbers.
  - Positioning: Theorem 4.2 is called the direct generalization of Theorem 5.4 of the 1991 paper (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): a global/local companion pair that promises a numerical stage (p. 4), with theory tied to real codes (p. 25).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): the code and test legs are deferred until a robust linearly constrained algorithm is implemented (p. 4). Here the theory is published ahead of the code.
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): cheap linear constraints are handled exactly, outside the penalty function (pp. 4, 25).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the 1991 technique is reused for penalty boundedness (p. 19), and the 1991 counterexample shows an assumption is needed (p. 24).
- Existing candidate *promise-then-deliver sequel* ✅: S113's conclusion names the local analysis as the next step (S113 p. 25), and S107 carries the same date (p. 2).
- Existing candidate **Show an assumption is needed by a constructed counterexample** ✅ (variant: an earlier counterexample is reused) (p. 24).
- Existing candidate *theory for a device practitioners already use* ✅ (pp. 7, 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the active-set pattern can vary, use the fact that there are finitely many index sets and fix one along a subsequence (p. 14).
- Define *strongly active* constraints (removal destroys criticality) to get identification results without assuming strict complementarity (p. 12).
- Justify a restrictive assumption by pointing to an existing counterexample that fails without it (p. 24).
- Prove penalty-parameter boundedness by contradiction: show that the multiplier-update branch must eventually always be taken (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "the theory presented is directly relevant to practical computation, as the inner iteration stopping rule (3.5) covers the type of optimality tests used in available packages for linearly constrained problems." (p. 25)
- "To conclude this section, we note that the conclusions of Theorems 4.7, 4.8 and 4.9 require that the complete sequence of iterates converges to a unique limit point. This assumption cannot be relaxed." (p. 24)
