### S036 · Optimization of custom MOS circuits by transistor sizing (Proceedings of International Conference on Computer Aided Design 1996, DOI 10.1109/iccad.1996.569578)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: P. K. Coulman, R. A. Haring, G. L. Morrill, C. Visweswariah (IBM T. J. Watson and IBM Microelectronics, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: This is the first published account of JiffyTune (ICCAD '96, p. 1), two years before the IEEE TCAD 1998 JiffyTune paper that SKILL.md cites. LANCELOT is the engine (refs [14]–[16], p. 7), and CUTE [19] was used to plug MINOS into the tool (p. 2). **Read**: full text, pp. 1–7, including the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is the users' pain.
  - Manual transistor sizing is "slow, tedious and iterative", relies on designer intuition and has circuit simulation in the inner loop (abstract, p. 1; §1, p. 1).
  - Static-timing sizing (Elmore delay, posynomial, convex after a change of variables) does not work for full-custom circuits, has limited accuracy and suffers from false paths (p. 1). The accuracy figure is about ±25% "in our experience"; the "±25%" is displaced in the extraction (p. 1).
  - Dynamic tuning with SPICE in the loop is accurate but expensive (p. 1).
  - The authors see the static and dynamic approaches as complementary stages of the design flow (p. 1).
  - Problem statement: given a schematic, input signals, tunable transistors and requirements, find the widths (§2, p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: dynamic simulation plus gradient-based NLP. The paper says: "Our ability to compute gradients efficiently is crucial to the success of this approach" (p. 1).
  - Simulator: SPECS is on average 70× faster than the in-house SPICE-like AS/X (p. 2). It computes time-domain sensitivities by the direct and the adjoint method, and picks the adjoint method automatically when parameters outnumber functions by a factor of 5 (p. 3).
  - Glue layer: JiffyTune chain-rules the gradients, including parasitic capacitances submitted as internal sensitivity parameters, down to the independent widths (pp. 2–3).
  - Problem language: measurements; functions as linear combinations of measurements, each with a weight, target and relation (<, >, =, minimize); minimax groups; ratioing and grouping of transistors so that layouts stay regular (p. 2).
  - LANCELOT changes made for this application (p. 5):
    - noise-aware choice of the initial trust-region radius;
    - looser tolerances for feasibility, line-search discontinuities and bound activities;
    - stopping tied to the smallest meaningful step;
    - slacks of satisfied inequalities reset so that the equality holds exactly, used only when "such an update is consistent with the convergence theory" (fewer iterations; also p. 2).
  - Minimax is recast as an NLP with an extra variable z and constraints z − f_i(x) ≥ 0 (eqs (3)–(4), p. 5).
  - Interfaces in the Cadence and SLED schematic systems, with back-annotation of results onto the schematic (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - SPECS's piecewise-constant device models limit relative timing accuracy to about ±5% (symbol displaced in the extraction), so tuning is only accurate to that level (p. 3).
  - Given those models, the gradients are exact (p. 3).
  - Function and gradient values are "noisy": errors are small but well above machine precision (p. 5).
  - Area is modelled as the sum of tunable widths when there is no layout (p. 2). The user supplies the input patterns (p. 1).
  - Widths are continuous, so fixed cell libraries with discrete power levels are out of scope (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No theorem.
  - Convergence claims are inherited from CGT 1988/1991 and the inner-iterations paper [28] (p. 4).
  - The only analysis is a cost count: the direct method needs one sensitivity-circuit solve per parameter; the adjoint method needs one per function plus a convolution (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - A gradient benchmark: 144 MOSFETs; 36 functions × 168 parameters = 6,048 gradients; about 5 ms or less per gradient (Table 1, p. 3).
  - A case study on a 12-way priority decoder with five runs, Manual/HOT/COLD/DELAY/MINIMAX. Delays are re-measured by AS/X on the final design and normalized to 500 units for confidentiality. HOT improves the worst delay by 7.5% and area by 5.0% (Table 2, p. 6).
  - Adoption: 41 designers, about 1,200 sessions, 168 unique circuits, over 2,200 successful runs (p. 6).
  - No optimizer baseline is reported. MINOS was used only for comparisons and sanity checks (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a deployed application and software, not a new algorithm (§7, p. 6). The claimed change is that designers now specify the problem instead of solving it (pp. 6–7).
  - Limits: not applicable to discrete-cell designs (p. 6); accuracy is capped by SPECS (p. 3); recovery from "non-working circuits" is an open problem (p. 7).
  - Future (p. 7): event-driven convolution, parallel sensitivity runs, semi-infinite constraints for temperature and supply variation, using group partial separability in LANCELOT, mixed-integer transistor ordering, manufacturability.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ (evidence): the user's pain is stated in the designers' terms (p. 1). The interface is built into their own design systems (p. 5). Success is reported as adoption (p. 6), and the venue is the designers' conference (p. 1). This paper predates the 1998 TCAD evidence cited in SKILL.md.
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence), Heuristic 1 ✅: derivatives come from simulator sensitivities, not from DFO (pp. 1, 3). Structure is used through minimax reformulation (p. 5), ratioing and grouping (p. 2), and planned group partial separability (p. 7).
- Research Taste, mark 4 ✅ and Workflow D step 4 ✅: noise and the physical resolution of the variables (0.001 µm) forced changes to the trust-region radius and tolerances (p. 5).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): code and numbers without a new theorem. The theory still acts as a constraint on engineering changes: the slack update is used only when "such an update is consistent with the convergence theory" (p. 5).
- new-pattern candidate: **Validate with a higher-fidelity simulator than the one in the loop**. SPECS drives the optimization; the reported delays are AS/X predictions on the final design (pp. 2, 6).
- new-pattern candidate: **Make tacit requirements explicit, because the optimizer exploits every gap**. The tool greedily exploits any aspect the user leaves unspecified (p. 2). Without an area constraint, the result depends on the start point (p. 6). Requirements are stored with the circuit (p. 6).
- Cross-link to the c01 candidate **solver-agnostic evaluation layer**: the CUTE environment was used to integrate MINOS into JiffyTune (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If the simulator can return sensitivities, choose between the direct and the adjoint method from the ratio of parameters to functions with one fixed threshold (5× here), and let the tool decide automatically (p. 3).
- Turn a minimax objective into a smooth NLP with one extra variable z and the constraints z ≥ f_i(x) (p. 5).
- For noisy simulator outputs, set the initial trust-region radius, feasibility and bound tolerances, and the stopping step length in the application's physical units (the smallest meaningful move), not in machine precision (p. 5).
- Report a single benchmark as a small matrix of runs: manual baseline, warm start, cold start, reweighted objective and alternative formulation. Evaluate every final design with the reference simulator (Table 2, p. 6).
- Report adoption as users, sessions, distinct problems and runs (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "The designers’ focus shifts from solving the optimization problem to specifying it correctly and completely." (p. 1)
- "A trivial example is if movement of less than 0.001 microns in transistor width is considered negligible, it may be disastrous if an automatic choice of the initial trust region size produces a radius that is much smaller." (p. 5)

### S047 · Convergence properties of minimization algorithms for convex constraints using a structured trust region (SIAM Journal on Optimization 1996, DOI 10.1137/s1052623492236481)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. Gould, A. Sartenaer, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The CGST quartet in the LANCELOT period (IBM, RAL and Namur addresses, p. 1). The analysis follows the unstructured convex-constraint theory [14] and Toint's Hilbert-space theory [41] (pp. 7, 16). The conclusion aims the method at "the next release of LANCELOT" (p. 29). **Read**:
- read: pp. 1–17 (introduction, algorithm, Lemmas 1–4), the Lemma 5 statement and the start of its proof, Theorem 6 with its full proof, Corollaries 7–8, 10 and 11, the Theorem 9 statement and the first part of its proof, and §§4–6 with the acknowledgments;
- skimmed: the middle of the Lemma 5 proof (pp. 18–19) and the end of the Theorem 9 proof (pp. 23–24);
- not read: the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap in the whole literature.
  - "all algorithms that we are aware of use a single trust region radius", even when several functions are modelled (pp. 2–3).
  - In large partially separable problems each element depends on few variables. With many quadratic terms and a few highly nonlinear ones, the nonlinear terms needlessly cap every step (p. 3).
  - The obvious fix, a scaling matrix in the norm, is rejected. Existing theory needs uniformly bounded condition numbers, which forbids one element's radius from going to infinity while another stays modest (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: one trust-region radius per element function.
  - Each element region limits only the projection of the step onto that element's range subspace (2.6). The overall region is the intersection of the element regions (2.11) (pp. 5–6).
  - Sufficient decrease is rewritten for a very asymmetric region: (2.36) uses max[Δ_min,k, ‖s_k‖] (p. 10). The suggested implementation is two-stage: a Cauchy step in the small symmetric region of radius Δ_min,k, then further progress along the projected-gradient arc or by CG while (2.36) holds (pp. 10, 14).
  - Radii are updated per element (2.48)–(2.59). Elements are split into "meaningful" and "negligible" ones by the size of their model change relative to the overall predicted decrease (pp. 11–12).
  - Each element's misfit is measured against the overall predicted decrease divided by p, not against the element's own change. The more intuitive test (2.61) is rejected because elements can cancel each other (pp. 13–14).
  - Acceptance is the usual overall ratio test (2.43) (p. 11). Every rule reduces to the classical one when p = 1 (pp. 10, 14).
  - Kept: the Cauchy-point decrease philosophy and the convex feasible set handled through projections (pp. 8–9). Changed: the shape of the trust region and the radius bookkeeping.
  - Extensions in §5 (pp. 26–28):
    - a hybrid radius max{Δ_k, Δ_i,k} with a global radius (5.1);
    - an equivalent success test (5.4);
    - a weaker decrease condition (5.5);
    - uniformly equivalent norms (5.10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - AS.1–AS.4 (pp. 4–5): X is closed and convex with nonempty interior; f is bounded below and partially separable with invariant subspaces N_i; element gradients are bounded.
  - Models: m_i,k(x_k) = f_i(x_k) (2.7), and the element gradient error is at most κ1·Δ_min,k (2.8). The authors call this "quite weak" (p. 5).
  - AS.5: the reciprocals of a monotone bound on model curvature (2.16) sum to infinity. This is weaker than bounded Hessians and allows BFGS or SR1 growth (p. 7).
  - AS.6–AS.7: an abstract criticality measure, zero exactly at critical points and continuous in the gradient. It covers the measures (2.24)–(2.28) from the literature (pp. 7–8, 21). AS.8 is needed only for the full limit (p. 22).
  - The compatibility condition (2.41) is chosen because it is problem-independent. The sharper (2.71) would make the constants depend on p (p. 16).
  - Plain partial separability is chosen over group partial separability, which would make the link between local and global models harder (p. 29).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The skeleton follows the unstructured proofs [14], [41] (p. 16).
  1. Lemma 2 (coherence): if every meaningful element passes its fit test and every negligible element's true change is small, the iteration is successful (pp. 14–15).
  2. Lemma 3: element model error ≤ c1·(curvature bound)·Δ_i,k² (pp. 16–17).
  3. Lemma 5: while criticality stays above ε, Δ_min,k ≥ c3/(curvature bound). The proof takes the first iteration that breaks the bound and shows that the element with the smallest radius must have fitted well, so its radius could not have shrunk (pp. 17–20).
  4. Theorem 6 (liminf criticality = 0), inspired by Powell [35] (pp. 20–21):
     - the successful iterations make the sum of 1/(curvature bound) finite;
     - the remaining iterations are split into F1 (k ≤ r·S(k)) and F2;
     - F1 is bounded by the successful indices repeated r times;
     - F2 is bounded by a geometric series, because the product of the radii shrinks at every unsuccessful iteration (3.44)–(3.47);
     - together these contradict AS.5.
  5. Theorem 9: the limit over successful iterates under AS.8 (pp. 22–24).
  6. Corollary 11: with a persistent gradient error, as with finite differences, the limit points are critical up to a term proportional to that error (pp. 24–25).
  - Active-set identification is only sketched in four steps and deferred to [15] (pp. 25–26).
- **D5 Experiments**: None in the paper. One sentence reports preliminary tests with a modified LANCELOT on unconstrained CUTE problems, with no tables (p. 28).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: theoretical and algorithmic, a class of algorithms with global convergence (abstract, p. 2; §6, p. 28).
  - Limits, stated plainly: efficiency in practice "remains to see" (p. 28). Active-set identification is "seldom observed in practice before the very last iterations", which makes §4 "mainly of theoretical interest" (p. 26). Theorem 9 could not be proved under (5.5) (p. 27).
  - Future (pp. 28–29): the next LANCELOT release; a larger expected payoff for augmented Lagrangians and penalty functions, where scaling matters more; separate trust regions for the objective and the constraints.
  - The acknowledgment thanks a reader who twice caught an unsuitable definition of (2.36) (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): partial separability is built into the globalization device itself, with one radius per element (pp. 3, 5–6), not only into the linear algebra.
- Method 3 ✅ (evidence, pre-DFO root): model quality is an explicit condition tied to the trust-region radius, gradient error ≤ κ1·Δ_min,k (2.8), which gives an O(Δ²) value error (Lemma 3). The theorem uses only this property, not a particular model (pp. 5, 16–17). The shape matches the later fully-linear conditions in SKILL.md.
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): a framework theorem for a class (abstract, p. 2) and a LANCELOT prototype, but the numbers are one sentence (p. 28). The test companion is deferred to a future release.
- Existing candidate **Split by type, give each block its natural treatment** ✅: each element function gets its own radius (pp. 5–6).
- new-pattern candidate: **Reduce to the classical case as a built-in check**. Every generalized rule is stated together with its p = 1 reduction (pp. 10, 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a scalar mechanism (a radius, a penalty) becomes one per component, state for each rule what it reduces to with one component, and prove a lemma that the component tests jointly imply the global test (Lemma 2, pp. 14–15).
- Measure each component's model misfit against the total predicted decrease divided by the number of components, not against its own predicted change, so cancellation between components cannot break the global ratio test (pp. 13–14).
- A counting device for liminf proofs with many radii:
  - split the iterations by whether k ≤ r·(number of successes so far);
  - bound the first part by the successful indices repeated r times;
  - bound the second by a geometric series from the product of the radii (pp. 20–21).
- Keep the algorithm's constants problem-independent by accepting a slightly stronger compatibility condition, (2.41) instead of (2.71) (p. 16).
- Before adopting the obvious alternative, check whether the assumptions of existing theory (here, bounded condition numbers of scaling matrices) rule out the behaviour you want (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "allowing large steps in directions in which the model has proved to be adequate while restricting the movement in directions where the model seems unreliable." (p. 3)
- "Tests on unconstrained problems from the CUTE collection [1] have shown that the new method, although very comparable to LANCELOT in many cases, sometimes produces substantial improvements." (p. 28)

### S058 · On iterated-subspace minimization methods for nonlinear optimization (Linear and Nonlinear Conjugate Gradient-Related Methods 1996, no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. Gould, A. Sartenaer, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The CGST quartet (p. 1). The entry comes directly from LANCELOT experience (p. 3). The file prints no venue, and its title page is dated "January 16, 2018" (pp. 1, 3), so the venue comes from the batch list only. **Read**: main text pp. 1–17 in full, including Figure 1 and Tables 1–4. Of the appendix Tables 5–17 (pp. 21–33) only the captions were seen. The references were read in part.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - With LANCELOT, "it is the linear-algebra cost which tends to dominate" when functions are cheap, so the authors are willing to trade more function evaluations for less linear algebra (p. 3).
  - Linesearch and trust-region methods do multi-dimensional work on a model but probe the true function along one direction, which the authors call "wasteful" (p. 4).
  - They hold that small-scale unconstrained minimization with derivatives is effectively a solved problem (pp. 4, 9–10).
  - They correct a "popular misconception": large problems with exact second derivatives are common, as CUTE shows (p. 3).
  - Six research questions are listed up front (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: iterated-subspace minimization (ISM).
  - At each outer iteration, choose S(k) ∈ ℝ^{n×s(k)} with s(k) ≪ n and approximately minimize f(x + S y) over y (1.2)–(1.3). This is the multi-dimensional analogue of a linesearch (p. 5).
  - The subspace must contain a gradient-related direction, for global convergence, and a Newton-related direction, for the fast rate (p. 7).
  - The subspace is built from the preconditioned CG run that truncated Newton performs anyway (pp. 7–9): the preconditioned steepest-descent direction s0; q more conjugate directions, either the first ones or those with extreme Rayleigh quotients; and the truncated-Newton direction.
  - The dimension is either fixed (s = 10) or automatic: stop at the first CG iteration where the Rayleigh quotient rises after its initial decrease (p. 9).
  - The inner solver is BFGS started from the exact reduced Hessian, which costs nothing: it is diagonal by conjugacy, or an arrowhead matrix once the truncated-Newton direction is added. The first quasi-Newton step then equals the truncated-Newton step (p. 10).
  - Truncated Newton is the special case where the subspace minimization is a single linesearch along the truncated-Newton direction (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f ∈ C², n large, exact second derivatives available, and evaluations cheap relative to linear algebra (p. 3).
  - For convergence: f bounded below with a Lipschitz gradient; the Goldstein conditions (2.1) and an angle condition (2.2) on the subspace step (p. 6).
  - The preconditioner has a uniformly bounded condition number (p. 7). The Hessian is assumed positive definite in §3.1; in the tests the model is convexified (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No new theorem.
  - Global convergence is inherited from Dennis–Schnabel Theorem 6.3.3. The conditions are rewritten in subspace variables (2.3)–(2.4), so they can be checked inside the inner minimization (p. 6).
  - A relaxed version needs the conditions only at some intermediate inner iterate, provided the final inner point achieves a fraction τ of its decrease (2.5)–(2.7). The paper calls this "easy to show" and gives no proof (p. 7).
  - A first inner linesearch along the steepest-descent or truncated-Newton direction meets the conditions (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - A subspace-dimension sweep on FMINSURF: CPU time is flat for s from 9 to 40, within 10% of the best value at s = 11 (Figure 1, p. 11).
  - Main test: 34 large or hard unconstrained CUTE problems, with 32 reported and the exclusions named (pp. 14–15). Eight ISM variants are compared with LANCELOT (unpreconditioned and preconditioned), truncated Newton and L-BFGS-B, on total evaluations and CPU time (Tables 1–2) and on rank tables with ties at 5% or 0.5 s (Tables 3–4) (pp. 12–16).
  - Result: ISM needs more evaluations than LANCELOT but is competitive in CPU time, and ISM(p,f,a) is "the best, slightly" (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a class of methods, variants and numerical evidence (p. 16).
  - Future (pp. 16–17): a linearly constrained ISM (5.1)–(5.3); nonlinear constraints only through penalty or barrier subproblems; interaction between trust-region radii across successive subproblems.
  - The authors say "our understanding is far from complete" (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): mature small-scale software is reused inside a large-scale method. The conclusion says "it is possible to solve large-scale nonlinear optimization problems using methods designed for small-scale problems" (p. 16). The dog-leg ingredients are acknowledged (p. 7).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): it uses the public collection, both the authors' own and outside baselines, rank tables and a full appendix (pp. 13–16, 21–33). The cost unit, though, is CPU time, chosen deliberately because evaluations are cheap in this regime (p. 3). The principle of Method 2 step 1 (count what the user pays for) holds, but here it points to linear algebra, not evaluations.
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): heavy on code and numbers, light on theory; convergence is inherited (pp. 6–7).
- Existing candidate **Self-audit note on one's own solver** ✅: the problem comes from LANCELOT's own linear-algebra cost (p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: **Reuse the inner solver's byproducts**. CG directions that truncated Newton discards become the subspace (p. 8), and the conjugacy structure gives a free exact starting Hessian (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When an inner iterative solver (CG) produces directions and curvature, keep them to build a search subspace or a starting Hessian instead of keeping only the final aggregate direction (pp. 8, 10).
- Write the global-convergence conditions in the subproblem's own variables, (2.3)–(2.4), and relax them so that they need hold only at some intermediate iterate, plus a fraction-of-decrease rule for the final point (2.5)–(2.7), so any inner solver can be plugged in (pp. 6–7).
- A cheap rule for the subspace dimension: watch the Rayleigh quotients during CG and stop at the first rise after the initial decrease (p. 9).
- Code-name each variant by its design switches, e.g. ISM(p,f,a). Report every combination with totals plus rank tables that state the tie tolerance (pp. 12–16).
- Disclose how baselines were tuned (the truncated-Newton exponent was changed because TN results "deteriorate considerably" otherwise, footnote 3, p. 14). Also say when a comparator is more mature (footnote 4, p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "In this paper, we take the view that the above schemes are quite wasteful, given the amount of information that may have been accrued during the (approximate) minimization of the model." (p. 4)
- "LANCELOT is a much more sophisticated code than our ISM variants, because it is designed for solving generally constrained in addition to unconstrained problems, has been extensively tested and refined, and contains a number of safeguards that are not included in the simpler ISM codes." (p. 16)

### S080 · Methods for nonlinear constraints in optimization calculations (SCAN-9608055 1996, no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The CGT trio's invited presentation at the IMA conference on the State of the Art in Numerical Analysis (York, March 1996), for the OUP volume (p. 1). It revisits Powell's 1987 assessment of the same field (p. 2). **Read**: the full main text, pp. 1–17. The reference list (pp. 17–26) was not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The entry is to re-assess Powell's 1987 verdict that SQP is the future (p. 2).
  - The decade since was "a slightly sobering experience", especially for those implementing algorithms (p. 2).
  - The interior-point wave lets SQP be seen "in a new light" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea (the survey's argument)**:
  - The lens is coherence between the step and the merit function. Newton's method in unconstrained optimization both solves the optimality conditions and reduces the merit function; SQP directions are "not directly related" to any proposed merit function (p. 9).
  - SQP's three difficulties (pp. 9–10):
    1. that incoherence;
    2. poor fit to large n, where solving linear systems dominates;
    3. almost no inexact-Newton theory with constraints.
  - Active-set and interior-point methods are contrasted by which KKT conditions they keep satisfied. Active-set methods "hide" the complementarity nonlinearity inside a combinatorial problem; interior-point methods "confront the nonlinearity directly" (p. 10).
  - Penalty and augmented-Lagrangian methods get three advantages, which explain why many of the most successful large codes use them (p. 11):
    - the step is tied to the merit function;
    - the Hessians are nearly definite, so secant updates fit;
    - the linear systems are sparse and definite.
  - Their drawbacks are listed too: a sequence of problems, iterates that initially move away from the solution, and no use of linear constraints (p. 11).
  - The trio's own work is placed in this map (pp. 12, 14–15):
    - CGT 1991, bounds with an augmented Lagrangian;
    - the 1994 slack-variable note;
    - 1992b, one Newton step per subproblem;
    - CGST 1995, linear inequalities;
    - CGT 1988, bound-constrained trust regions;
    - CGST 1993, convex constraints.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Not applicable (survey). Smooth problems throughout, and equalities and inequalities are treated separately ("For simplicity", p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No theorems. The critique is in the first person: "in our opinion, the contortions that were necessary" to bend secant updates for positive-definite SQP underline the difficulties of that approach (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: None.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - It continues Powell (1987) (p. 2).
  - It is critical of positive-definite-Hessian SQP (p. 4).
  - It is balanced toward rivals: Murtagh–Saunders (MINOS) is still "pre-eminent" for large linearly constrained problems (p. 15).
  - The software section names CONOPT, LSGRG2, MINOS, LANCELOT, ETR and the Boggs et al. SQP code (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a survey plus an agenda. Solvable problem sizes grew from about 50 to about 20,000 unknowns in ten years, "primarily because of our better exploitation of problem structure" (p. 16).
  - Agenda (pp. 16–17):
    - interior-point methods for nonconvex problems;
    - a reappraisal driven by second derivatives and automatic differentiation;
    - the effect of strong nonlinearity;
    - how modern codes compare;
    - noise;
    - MINLP and global optimization;
    - factorization-free methods with approximate subproblem solutions.
  - Stated gaps: network optimization, optimal control, parallel and infinite-dimensional problems (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (stated, co-authored): the software section argues that designing and analyzing algorithms is mainly for enabling others to solve real problems, and that good software is one of the best ways to do so. ### S126 · A primal-dual algorithm for minimizing a nonconvex function subject to bounds and nonlinear constraints (Nonlinear Optimization and Applications 2 1996, no DOI)
**Title mismatch**: the listed title says "…bounds and nonlinear constraints". The file is Namur Report 96/9 (18 Nov 1996), titled "A primal-dual algorithm for minimizing a nonconvex function subject to bound and linear equality constraints" (p. 1). It treats only bounds and linear equalities, and says general nonlinear constraints would still be handled by the augmented Lagrangian (p. 3). The year and the first half of the title match, so the listed entry is probably a garbled record of this work, but no "nonlinear constraints" version was seen. This card describes the file. S054 (batch s2-06, the 1999 Kluwer chapter) has the same title as the file and a near-identical abstract (checked on its first page only); count them as one line of work.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: The CGT trio's first move toward primal-dual interior-point methods for nonconvex problems, motivated by LANCELOT's weaknesses (pp. 2–3). **Read**: pp. 1–15 (problem, directions, merit function, Lemmas 1–2, algorithm, comments, Lemma 3), the Theorem 7 statement and the opening of its proof (pp. 23–24), §3 variants, §4 experiments and §5 conclusion. Not read: the proofs of Lemmas 4–6 (pp. 16–23) and the references.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - LANCELOT has two drawbacks (pp. 2–3):
    1. augmenting linear constraints into the merit function may not be the best way to treat them;
    2. predicting the active bounds from the generalized Cauchy point works poorly when the problem is degenerate or nearly so.
  - Interior-point methods are less sensitive to degeneracy in linear programming (p. 3).
  - The gap: "remarkably little work" extends primal-dual methods to nonconvex problems. Bonnans–Pola need a strictly interior start and a convex model (p. 3).
  - Goals: one merit function; a guaranteed descent direction that is either the primal-dual direction or a closely related Newton direction; linear equalities handled explicitly without an initially feasible point (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Problem (1.1): min f(x) subject to Ax = b and x ≥ 0 (p. 2).
  - Primal-dual step: a Newton step on the perturbed KKT system (1.4), with μ = σ⟨x, z⟩/n (1.5), reduced to the KKT system (1.9) with G = H + X⁻¹Z (pp. 4–5).
  - Infeasible starts are allowed through one artificial scalar θ with Ax − b = θ r0 (1.11). A unit step on θ makes the equalities exact from then on (p. 5).
  - Alternative step: a Newton step for the shifted penalty f + (ρ/2)(θ + 1)² subject to (1.11) and x ≥ 0 (1.13), (1.17) (pp. 5–6).
  - One merit function serves both steps: the logarithmic penalty Φ = f + (ρ/2)(θ + 1)² − μ⟨log x, e⟩ (1.19) (p. 6).
  - Descent needs G to be second-order sufficient with respect to A, meaning that the augmented matrix is nonsingular with exactly m negative eigenvalues (1.26) (p. 7). Lemma 2 gives a threshold on ρ (1.33) (p. 8).
  - Step 2 switches between the two steps: take the primal-dual step if the slope test (1.44) holds, otherwise the Newton step (p. 12).
  - If needed, only NᵀGN (the reduced matrix) is convexified, not G (p. 10; comment 8, p. 14).
  - Step sizes (p. 11): separate step sizes in x and z with a fraction-to-boundary rule (1.50)–(1.51); θ kept nonnegative (1.53); an Armijo search on Φ (1.56).
  - Parameter updates (pp. 12–13): z is kept in a box (1.59); μ is reduced at μ-critical iterations, defined by dual feasibility, complementarity decrease and primal feasibility (1.60)–(1.63); ρ is increased only under (1.64).
  - The Newton step is a rank-one perturbation of the primal-dual system, so it is cheap to compute from the same factorization (comment 7, p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: AS1–AS5 (p. 4):
  - f ∈ C²;
  - f − μ⟨log x, e⟩ is bounded below for every μ > 0;
  - the gradient and Hessian are bounded on the positive orthant;
  - A has full rank;
  - a strictly interior feasible point exists.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Main result, Theorem 7: the iterations where μ decreases form an infinite set K. On K, complementarity, the dual residual and the primal residual all go to zero (2.75)–(2.78) (p. 23).
  - The proof assumes μ stays bounded away from zero and splits into cases: ρ bounded versus ρ → ∞, and primal-dual versus Newton steps (pp. 15–16, 23–24).
  - Lemma 1 bounds the reduced Hessian's eigenvalues through a Wilkinson perturbation argument (pp. 7–8).
  - A footnote gives a 3×3 counterexample showing that Lemma 2 fails if second-order sufficiency is weakened to second-order necessity (p. 8).
  - Lemma 3 bounds θ using the monotone decrease of the merit function (1.71)–(1.75) (p. 15).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - A Fortran 90 prototype for QPs. It uses MA27, whose inertia reports trigger the naive modification G + ‖G‖I (p. 27).
  - Test set: all the larger CUTE QPs, without preprocessing (p. 27). Baseline: the active-set code VE09. MINOS runs are mentioned but not reported, as "quantitatively similar" to VE09 (p. 28).
  - Failures are listed as more than 1000 iterations or as rank deficient (Tables 1–2, pp. 28–29).
  - Result: good on convex problems, "somewhat disappointing" on nonconvex ones, where negative eigenvalues are removed one at a time (p. 29).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a framework, a global convergence proof and preliminary numbers (p. 29).
  - Limits: naive handling of negative curvature; slow convergence with "massive" indefiniteness (pp. 29–30); an ill-conditioned KKT matrix (5.6), for which the normal equations are inappropriate (p. 30).
  - Future (p. 30): predictor-corrector steps; explaining why primal-dual methods should do better than primal ones globally, even for nonconvex problems; explaining why the central path matters.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): LP primal-dual interior-point machinery is carried to nonconvex NLP and hybridized with a Newton step on a shifted quadratic penalty (pp. 3–6). An inertia test from KKT theory is borrowed (p. 7).
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): framework, theorem, prototype and CUTE numbers with failures, in one report (pp. 11–29).
- Method 2 ✅ and Workflow C step 5 ✅: the larger CUTE QPs without preprocessing, an active-set baseline, a problem-type label per row and explicit failures (pp. 27–29).
- Existing candidate **Self-audit note on one's own solver** ✅: the entry is two named drawbacks of LANCELOT (pp. 2–3).
- Existing candidate **Necessity by constructed counterexample** (c02/S007) ✅: the 3×3 footnote example (p. 8).
- Existing candidate **Split by type** ✅: bounds through a log barrier, linear equalities handled explicitly, nonlinear constraints (future) through the augmented Lagrangian (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To allow infeasible starts for linear equalities, add one scalar θ with Ax − b = θ r0 and drive θ to zero. A unit step then gives exact feasibility from that point on (1.11)–(1.12), p. 5.
- If one search direction does not always descend on the merit function, compute a second direction from a rank-one perturbation of the same KKT system and switch between them by a slope test (Step 2, p. 12; comment 7, p. 14).
- Convexify only the null-space Hessian NᵀGN, detected by inertia from the factorization, so no modification is needed near a minimizer where the problem is locally convex (pp. 7, 14, 27).
- In result tables, label each problem convex, second-order sufficient or nonconvex, and report how many iterations used the fallback step and how many were modified. Failures can then be attributed to a cause (pp. 27–29).
- Check whether a lemma really needs its strong hypothesis with a tiny counterexample in a footnote (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Considering the vast literature on primal-dual methods for convex problems, there has been remarkably little work on extending these methods to the non-convex case." (p. 3)
- "Thus we were motivated to consider an interior point method in which linear constraints Ax = b are handled explicitly and simple bounds are handled via a logarithmic barrier term." (p. 3)

### S074 · On the number of inner iterations per outer iteration of a globally convergent algorithm for optimization with general nonlinear inequality constraints and simple bounds (Computational Optimization and Applications 1997, DOI 10.1023/a:1008667728545)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**:
- The file is Report 92/14 (20 Aug 1992, p. 1); the 1997 journal version was not seen.
- Companion papers: the equality-constrained version in the Dundee 1991 proceedings, Conn et al. (1992a) (p. 3, reference p. 26), and the Lagrangian barrier algorithm, Conn et al. (1992b), whose theorems it imports (pp. 2, 10, 12).
- File-level inconsistency: the reference-list entry for 1992b (p. 26) gives the title of the structured-trust-region report 92/11 (the preprint of S047). The text uses 1992b for the Lagrangian barrier algorithm.

**Read**: pp. 1–16 (introduction, notation, Algorithms 3.1–3.3, AS1–AS9, Theorems 4.1–4.3, the proof outline and item (i)) and pp. 25–26 (§5 general case, conclusions). The proof items (ii)–(vi) on pp. 16–25 were not read in detail.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Analyses of sequential penalty and barrier methods usually ignore inner-iteration effort (Fiacco–McCormick, Bertsekas). Gould 1989 and CGT 1992a are the exceptions (p. 3).
  - The direct trigger: while testing a prototype of LANCELOT Release B, the authors "often" saw only one SBMIN iteration per outer iteration (p. 3).
  - The question: under which assumptions does one inner iteration eventually suffice (abstract, p. 1)?
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Outer Algorithm 3.1 (pp. 3, 6):
    - the Lagrangian barrier function Ψ = f − Σ λ_i s_i log(c_i + s_i) (2.4), with shifts s_i = μ(λ_i)^α (3.4);
    - bounds enforced directly by projection;
    - multipliers updated when the constraint test (3.8) holds; otherwise the forcing parameter μ is reduced.
  - Inner Algorithm 3.2 is the CGT 1988 bound-constrained trust-region method: a generalized Cauchy point, then CG in the free variables (Algorithm 3.3) (pp. 7–11).
  - Three design choices make the result possible (p. 7):
    - a warm start from the previous solution, with the significantly dominated variables moved to their bounds (3.17)–(3.18);
    - an initial radius tied to the projected gradient, so the trust region does not interfere asymptotically (3.19);
    - B(k,0) any sufficiently good Hessian approximation, with exact second derivatives "often to be recommended".
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - The assumptions (pp. 2, 10, 12–13):
    - AS1: f and c are C².
    - AS2: the Jacobian of the active constraints, restricted to the free variables, has full rank.
    - AS3: the second derivatives are Lipschitz.
    - AS4: the KKT matrices are nonsingular for every admissible active set.
    - AS5 and AS7: two strict complementarity conditions.
    - AS6: a single limit point.
    - AS8: a Dennis–Moré-type accuracy condition on B(k,0).
    - AS9: a perturbed KKT matrix, nonsingular with m negative eigenvalues.
  - The tolerance exponents must satisfy (4.13) (p. 14).
  - The authors call the conditions "relatively strong" but "probably about as weak as is possible" (p. 3). They add that AS9 could be encouraged algorithmically (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Main result, Theorem 4.3: eventually one inner iteration completes each outer iteration, and each inner solution is the next starting point without adjustment (p. 14).
  - It imports Theorem 4.1 (limit points are KKT points) and Theorem 4.2 (the penalty parameter settles at μ_min, with geometric error bounds (4.6)) from 1992b (pp. 10, 12), then relabels the iterations so that μ is at its final value (p. 12).
  - The proof is a six-item checklist of events that hold for all large k (p. 14):
    1. the starting point's variable status is unchanged;
    2. the Cauchy point comes before the first breakpoint;
    3. one CG pass suffices;
    4. the step is accepted;
    5. the convergence test is met;
    6. the shifted start is redundant.
  - The key device for item (i): under strict complementarity, for each bounded variable exactly one of x_j and its gradient component tends to zero while the other tends to a positive limit. The threshold (4.21) separates the two and fixes each variable's status for all large k (p. 15).
- **D5 Experiments**: None in the paper. The motivation comes from prototype testing only (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a result about the work per outer iteration, extended to general bounds l ≤ x ≤ u in §5 (p. 25). The authors expect it to be "an important ingredient of release B of the LANCELOT package" (p. 25).
  - Limits: strong assumptions, a single limit point, and no numbers.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): the theory is written to explain behaviour seen in the authors' own code (p. 3) and is aimed back at the next release (p. 25). The loop runs code → observation → theorem → code.
- Existing candidate **anomaly-driven theory** (c01/S010) ✅, variant: the trigger here is a favourable regularity, not an anomaly (p. 3).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the analysis is of how two mature algorithms, a Lagrangian barrier outer loop and a bound-constrained trust-region inner loop, "mesh together" (p. 3).
- Existing candidate **Split by type** ✅: bounds are handled directly and general inequalities by the Lagrangian barrier (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a nested method, analyze the number of inner iterations per outer iteration, not only outer convergence. Design the warm start (move dominated variables to their bounds) and the initial trust-region radius so the asymptotic regime is reached at once (pp. 3, 7).
- Structure a long asymptotic proof as a numbered list of events that must all hold for large k, then prove each one (p. 14).
- Under strict complementarity, pair each variable with its gradient or multiplier component and define a threshold that separates the two sequences; this fixes the active set for all large k (p. 15).
- Say explicitly which behaviour is proved and which is only observed. Here the package shows the behaviour even when the assumptions fail (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "It was often apparent that only a single iteration of the inner iteration subroutine SBMIN was ultimately required for every outer iteration of our sequential Lagrangian barrier program." (p. 3)
- "While the conditions required in this paper to turn this observation to a proven result are relatively strong (and we feel probably about as weak as is possible), the package frequently exhibits the same behaviour on problems which violate our assumptions." (p. 3)

### S102 · A numerical comparison between the LANCELOT and MINOS packages for large-scale constrained optimization (SCAN-9711063 1997, no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: I. Bongartz (ProMIRA Software), N. I. M. Gould, M. A. Saunders (Stanford, co-author of MINOS), Ph. L. Toint (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: It follows the LANCELOT options study (Conn et al. 1996) and reuses its methodology choices (p. 6). The complete per-problem results are in the separate Report 97/14 (p. 12), which is S120 in batch s2-06. The cover page says "large-scale nonlinear optimization" and the inner title "large-scale constrained optimization" (pp. 1–2). **Read**: the full main text, pp. 1–18. References after p. 19 were not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - LANCELOT's options had been studied, but little had been published on MINOS's numerical performance, "and even less" on how the two packages compare (p. 3).
  - The difficulty of a fair comparison is made the explicit problem. Five difficulties are listed (p. 3):
    1. an unbiased test set;
    2. the choice of efficiency measure;
    3. which options to use;
    4. stopping and memory settings;
    5. how to present the data.
- **D2 Core idea (the design of the comparison)**:
  - Test set: 913 problems derived from 704, namely 112 LP (mostly NETLIB), 225 QP, 300 unconstrained or bound-constrained, 58 linearly constrained and 218 nonlinearly constrained problems, from CUTE (pp. 7–8). Each variable-dimension problem appears in only two sizes, one small and one large, to avoid bias (p. 7).
  - Problem characteristics are shown up front: dimension, m/n, degrees of freedom, nonlinearity ratios (Figures 1–3, Table 1, pp. 8–10).
  - Measures: reliability, function evaluations and CPU time. Iteration counts are rejected because the two packages define iterations differently (p. 10).
  - Settings: default versions only, with both specification files printed and iteration caps made effectively infinite (Figures 4–5, p. 11).
  - Symmetric failure accounting (p. 12): a reported failure at a correct solution counts as a success; a reported success at a wrong point counts as a failure.
  - Efficiency is compared only on "coherently solved" problems, whose optimal values agree within 1%. LPs and QPs are excluded from the evaluation counts (pp. 13–14).
  - Reporting: totals, then win/tie/loss counts with the min, quartiles and max of the per-problem difference. Results are split by degrees of freedom (< 50 or ≥ 50, from MINOS's superbasic count) (pp. 14–18).
- **D5 Experiments** (the results):
  - Reliability for LANCELOT vs MINOS (Table 3, p. 13): LP 65% vs 100%, QP 93% vs 90%, unconstrained/bound-constrained 97% vs 88%, linearly constrained 97% vs 97%, nonlinearly constrained 87% vs 79%.
  - Failure causes: MINOS fails mostly by arithmetic error, LANCELOT by lack of progress or time (Table 4, p. 13).
  - Function evaluations favour LANCELOT, and the gap grows with the degrees of freedom (Tables 5–8, pp. 14–16). One outlier (LAUNCH) is shown to swamp the totals (footnote 6, p. 14).
  - CPU time favours MINOS on LPs and on problems with few degrees of freedom, and LANCELOT on problems with many (Tables 9–12, pp. 16–18). Named extreme cases: BIGBANK, CORKSCRW, GRIDNETB, MSQRTALS (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Both algorithms are described neutrally (pp. 3–6).
  - A plausible cause of MINOS's weaker reliability on nonlinear constraints is given: its step-length heuristic has no convergence proof (p. 6).
  - The authors' own package's LP weakness is reported first (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: a benchmark and a methodology. The claim is that the two packages "complement each other" (p. 17).
  - Limits: defaults only, so the conclusions hold only for the defaults. A variant-by-variant comparison would be a major exercise (p. 18).
  - Advice: practitioners should try both (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (strong evidence): a very large public set (CUTE plus NETLIB); cost units of function evaluations and CPU time, not iterations (p. 10); two tiers of problem class; failures reported by cause (pp. 7–13).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the benchmark is co-authored with the author of the rival package, Saunders (p. 2), and both schools are described fairly (pp. 3–6).
- Research Taste, warning sign 6 ✅: the claim is worded as "complement each other" (p. 17), and the authors' own weakness (LANCELOT on LPs) is stated plainly (p. 12).
- Method 2 step 5 ⚠ (variant): defaults only. Per-option analysis is delegated to the earlier LANCELOT options study (p. 18).
- new-pattern candidate: **Rival's author as co-author of the benchmark** (p. 2).
- new-pattern candidate: **Symmetric failure accounting against known solutions** (pp. 12–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Check every reported outcome against known solutions. Count reported failures at correct points as successes and reported successes at wrong points as failures, and give both counts per package (p. 12).
- Restrict efficiency comparisons to "coherently solved" problems (optimal values within 1%) and state how many remain (pp. 13–14).
- Report win/tie/loss counts plus the min, quartiles and max of per-problem differences. Show how one outlier changes the totals (pp. 14–15).
- Stratify results by a structural feature that the algorithms' design says should matter (degrees of freedom < 50 vs ≥ 50) (pp. 15–18).
- Print each solver's exact specification file. When the solvers define iterations differently, make the iteration caps effectively infinite (p. 11).
- Use one small and one large instance of each variable-dimension problem so that no family is overweighted (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "In our experience, this is essential for a valid assessment of software reliability and performance. Smaller test sets are more likely to introduce unwanted bias and make the statistical results less useful." (p. 7)
- "One immediately notices the disappointing reliability of LANCELOT on linear programs, especially given the perfect reliability of MINOS on such problems." (p. 12)
