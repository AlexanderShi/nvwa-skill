### S236 · SIAG/OPT Views-and-News (SIAG/OPT Views-and-News 18(2) 2007, no DOI or arXiv id)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none for the essay. It is single-author, written from IBM T. J. Watson Research Center (p. 1). The issue's Scholar author string (Conn, Resende, Anstreicher, Vicente) lists the authors of the other items. **Position in trajectory**: written seventeen years after the move to IBM (p. 2, p. 7). It looks back on circuit tuning (JiffyTune line, 1990s–2002 award), the IBM–CMU mixed-integer nonlinear programming project (BONMIN), and the then-recent start of petroleum work (pp. 5–6), while the DFO book (IDFO, listed as 2008, to appear) was nearly done (p. 7, ref. [6]). **Read**: the essay in full (pp. 1–8, including references). For the rest of the issue, see the source notes.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The essay was commissioned. The issue's theme is *Optimizers in Industry* (p. 1). The editor says he asked two optimizers who spent much of their careers in industry to describe their experiences and thoughts. He presents Conn's article as the experience of a continuous optimizer in circuit design and petroleum engineering (p. 23). Conn frames it as a personal view but an honest one (p. 1). The essay itself answers two questions: what an industrial research lab offers a mathematician compared with a university (§2, pp. 2–3), and how his projects there began (§§3–5, pp. 3–7). Three entry routes are described:
  1. **Circuit tuning: through a user's small question** (p. 3). An electrical-engineering colleague at Watson asked Conn's advice on minimax optimization. Conn gave brief discussions and pointers, and the colleague did the rest himself. Later, a colleague of his organized an IBM-wide internal meeting on circuit tuning. Because of the earlier encounter, Conn was suggested as a relatively approachable mathematician and invited. He attended about half of it. Asked for his opinion, he said the users were running 1960s algorithms in the 1990s, and was asked to present 1990s algorithms. This began the collaboration (p. 3).
  2. **Mixed-integer nonlinear programming: through a management call, pointed at the user's next problem** (p. 4). Management asked for projects suited to an IBM–CMU collaboration. Conn chose MINLP, a topic he had been thinking about for a while. It was catalysed by circuit tuning, because future circuit technology would add discrete variables (low, regular or high threshold-voltage assignment, footnote 2) to the continuous widths then being tuned (p. 4).
  3. **Petroleum: a deliberate move once the first project was routine** (pp. 5–6). Once circuit tuning had become an accepted daily tool, he wanted to make a splash in a new area (p. 5). IBM prefers its researchers to work on applications of interest to IBM (p. 5). The entry vehicle was IBM's first-of-a-kind projects: exploratory collaborations with a customer on a hard problem, with a small initial investment from the customer, which may or may not lead to longer engagements (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: when you sit in on users' meetings, compare the vintage of the algorithms they use with the current state of the art, and offer to present the newer ones. That gap is the entry (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: pick the next research topic from the structural change you can see coming in your current user's problem (continuous to mixed discrete-continuous), before the users ask for it (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea (the essay's theses)**:
  1. **Industry and university are complementary** (p. 2). An industrial research lab like Watson offers things a university does not, and the other way round. The decline of industrial *research* labs, as opposed to development labs, is bad for science (p. 2).
  2. **Basic research needs protection** (p. 2). He calls basic research an important luxury (footnote 1: arguably a necessity, but seen as dispensable when budgets are tight). It cannot thrive if short-term gains are the single most important objective, and it needs extended periods of uninterrupted thought (p. 2). His largest lowlight is the pressure, real or perceived, to finish things quickly that are adequate but could be better (p. 2).
  3. **Applications feed theory** (p. 3). At IBM there is no shortage of applied projects. Although the customer may seem too important, a successful project's results really matter. The problems are challenging and can catalyse innovations in basic research, and they motivate learning new things (p. 3).
  4. **An applied optimization success needs three ingredients** (p. 4). The circuit project could not have been as successful without state-of-the-art optimization. But without the electrical engineers and the familiar interfaces it would not have got off the ground (p. 4). Software that works in an environment the users are comfortable with outranks a factor-of-two algorithmic gain (p. 4; see Quotes).
  5. **Derivatives decide the tool chain** (p. 3). The in-house simulator was fast (about seventy times faster than a more accurate SPICE-like simulator), reliable, based on piecewise-constant approximations, and it gave reasonably accurate derivative approximations. The outside alternative was only promised: it did not exist, would use piecewise-linear approximations and would give no derivatives. Higher management believed linear approximations beat constant ones and did not see what derivatives mean for optimization (p. 3).
  6. **Success needs intimate collaboration** between end-users, domain experts (engineers, physicists, geologists) and the mathematicians (p. 5). Colleagues who lead in the application but are broad enough to talk mathematics make it possible to contribute as a neophyte in the application (p. 5).
  7. **Applying optimization is almost a duty** (p. 5). There are so many challenging, economically important applications that would benefit from the intelligent use of state-of-the-art optimization that he feels it is almost a duty to contribute in that applied way (p. 5).
  8. **Formulation matters, and matters more in the nonlinear case** (p. 5). Mathematically equivalent formulations are not practically equivalent. For MILP there is a sophisticated understanding of good formulations; for MINLP formulation seems even more critical but is far less understood (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: when defending a tool choice to non-specialist decision makers, argue from what the *optimizer* needs (derivatives, speed, reliability), not from the approximation order outsiders judge by, and let the domain experts carry the argument (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Assumptions & conditions (what he says his account depends on)**:
  - **Watson is atypical.** Working at T. J. Watson is not a typical industrial position (p. 2).
  - **Scale of resources.** The circuit project needed investment, commitment and a breadth of skills that would have been hard to find together anywhere but a place like Watson. It would not have happened in an academic environment (p. 4). He says, hedged with *I may be incorrect in this*, that for real, complex applications combined with applied mathematics, no university can match a research centre like his (p. 5).
  - **People.** The project needed world-class circuit designers and electrical engineers with the confidence to know they were right and, eventually, to persuade others (pp. 3–4). It also had unfailing support from immediate management; the conflicts were higher up (p. 3).
  - **An in-house simulator with derivatives** (p. 3).
  - **Money and funding.** When he left Canada he held the largest NSERC grant in numerical analysis in the country ($165,374 for three years). Within a year at IBM he had a three-year DARPA grant of $917,809 (p. 2).
  - **Costs he names.**
    - Hierarchical management; political correctness, intellectual property and legal issues can be real obstacles, though IBM consented to publication of the essay (p. 3).
    - Greater short-term variance in a private company, and agendas that can seem short-sighted (p. 3).
    - Worse computing support (hardware and software) than at a university, and harder travel and exchange visits (p. 3).
    - Travel promised at hiring was never realised because of IBM's decline in the 1990s; living costs were higher than expected (p. 2).
  - **Teaching.** Teaching at the right level and to good students is very useful for research, but it is a constant demand (p. 2). He taught a course at Yale for three years and was about to build a group of doctoral students when Yale's Operations Research department was closed (p. 2).
- **D4 Argument technique (no proofs or theorems)**:
  - **Structure.** The argument is built from cases. After the personal scene-setting (§1) and the environment (§2), three sections classify projects by how application-driven they are: an application (§3), not primarily an application (§4), and the present (§5). Each gives origin, obstacles and outcome (pp. 3–7). §2 lists highlights and lowlights side by side (pp. 2–3).
  - **Evidence.** Claims are backed by concrete figures and named outputs:
    - the two grant amounts (p. 2);
    - the roughly seventy-fold simulator speed-up and the eighteen-month fight for the project's survival (p. 3);
    - millions of dollars gained by IBM and an IBM award in spring 2002 (p. 3);
    - papers in premier journals, several patents and a best-paper award, ref. [2] JiffyTune (p. 4);
    - BONMIN built within little more than a year (p. 5).
  - **Mathematical content** is brief and non-technical:
    - Circuit tuning is set up as tuning transistor widths. Wider transistors are generally faster but use more power and area and load the previous stages (p. 4).
    - MINLP is hard for four stated reasons (p. 5):
      - undecidability even for a linear objective with one polynomial equation in ten nonnegative integer variables (ref. [8]);
      - the solution is typically not on the boundary, so the convex hull of the feasible region is not enough;
      - nonconvexity makes global optimization an issue for bounds;
      - the subproblems are hard.
    - Seismic matching is posed as an inverse problem that is inherently ill-posed and computationally difficult (p. 6).
    - History-matching derivatives were derived as forward and adjoint methods in one algebraic framework (p. 6).
    - Maintenance scheduling is posed as simulation-based optimization (pp. 6–7):
      - failures are random events whose distributions are estimated by the company's experts;
      - simulated scenarios give a predictive distribution of the schedule's cost (maintenance costs roughly known and fixed, shutdown costs random);
      - the schedule is optimized with derivative-free techniques (refs. [5] CST 1997 and [9] Powell's NEWUOA developments).
  - Transferable technique: for decisions under random failures, simulate failure scenarios using expert-estimated distributions to get a predictive cost distribution, then optimize the decision with a derivative-free method (p. 7). No results are reported, so this is a formulation pattern, not validated practice.
  - Transferable technique: derive forward and adjoint derivatives of a simulator inside one algebraic framework, so both come from the same derivation (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments (project outcomes reported; no numbers, tables or plots)**:
  - **Circuit tuning.** The tool automated circuit tuning and worked on far larger circuits than earlier techniques. It used LANCELOT (ref. [3]) and now uses IPOPT (ref. [10]). It improved both the designs and the designers' productivity, and is deployed as a standard IBM tool for all custom circuits (p. 4). Before it, tuning was slow, tedious, manual and error-prone (p. 4).
  - **Seismic matching.** They inverted for basin modelling, rock physics and seismic attributes, matching amplitude as well as phase (p. 6).
    - The synthetic model was deliberately sized: realistic enough to expose the challenges of applying state-of-the-art optimization, but not so large that progress would slow to a crawl (p. 6).
    - The result was a very respectable fit on the synthetic model. They were less successful on a shallow deltaic model offshore West Africa (ref. [7]) (p. 6).
    - The conclusion is hedged: reasonably confident that the approach is feasible for realistic problems given modern industrial computing (p. 6).
  - **History matching.** A benchmarking study compared three automatic history-matching tools on two problems supplied by the petroleum company (p. 6).
    - Problem 1: a simple set of synthetic problems testing robustness and efficiency, where all tools can be applied in exactly the same way. Result: good to excellent matches for all wells.
    - Problem 2: water flooding close to a real field application. Result: qualitatively similar to commercial software, which he finds remarkable given a very elementary implementation.
    - His conclusion: the results form a good basis for further work (p. 6).
  - **Maintenance scheduling.** Work in progress on a single facility, to be generalised to several dependent facilities. No results yet (pp. 6–7).
  - Transferable technique: size the synthetic test model on purpose: realistic enough to expose the real difficulties, small enough to keep iteration fast. Then run a real case and report where it did worse (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: when benchmarking against commercial tools, use a partner-supplied synthetic set on which every tool is applied identically, plus one near-real case, and word the claim as *similar* when that is what the data show (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Where he stands.** Against the fashion for development labs, he argues for industrial *research* labs and urges readers to strengthen them (p. 2, p. 7). He compares industry with universities on both sides and says he knows no place with a more industrious or brilliant group of mathematical-sciences colleagues than Watson (p. 2).
  - **Rivals.** Outside CAD vendors are criticised on two grounds: their tools were not yet built and would have given no derivatives (p. 3), and buying them would have forced IBM to disclose aspects of its circuits, improving tools the vendors then sold to competitors (p. 3).
  - **Credit to others.**
    - The electrical engineers and designers (pp. 3–4).
    - Jon Lee, who leads the MINLP project; Conn calls his own contributions minimal (p. 5).
    - The geophysics colleague who leads that field (p. 6), and the petroleum company's research group leader and data (p. 6).
  - **Citations.**
    - His own books: LANCELOT [3], Trust-Region Methods [4] and IDFO [6], called his third book since joining IBM (p. 7).
    - JiffyTune [2], BONMIN [1] and IPOPT [10] (pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] In a users' meeting, date the algorithms they run against the current state of the art, and offer to present the newer ones. The gap is your entry (p. 3).
- [D1] Choose the next topic from the structural change you can see coming in your partner's problem, such as discrete variables entering a continuous tuning problem, and start the basic research before they ask (p. 4).
- [D1] Write down explicit selection criteria before entering a new area: under-researched, rising importance, strength in-house, a complementary partner, applications at hand (p. 4, p. 6).
- [D2] Choose the simulator or model by what the optimizer needs (derivatives, speed, reliability), and make that argument to decision makers through the domain experts (pp. 3–4).
- [D2] Put the solver behind interfaces the users already know, and count that work as part of the project. He says a twice-as-good algorithm fails without it (p. 4).
- [D5] Size the synthetic test model deliberately (realistic enough to expose the difficulties, small enough to iterate), then run a real case and report where it did worse (p. 6).
- [D5] Benchmark against commercial tools on a partner-supplied synthetic set applied identically to every tool, plus one near-real case. Say *similar* if that is what you see (p. 6).
- [D4] For schedules under random failures, simulate scenarios from expert-estimated failure distributions to get a predictive cost distribution, and optimize the schedule with a DFO method (p. 7).
- [D8] Seed a new area by releasing an open-source package together with test problems as early as possible (p. 5).
