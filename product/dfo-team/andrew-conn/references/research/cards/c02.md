### S007 · A globally convergent Lagrangian barrier algorithm for optimization with general inequality constraints and simple bounds (Math. Comput. 1997, DOI 10.1090/s0025-5718-97-00777-1)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in research trajectory**: the inequality-constraint twin of CGT's 1991 *SINUM* augmented-Lagrangian paper ("This paper is a companion to previous work of ours on augmented Lagrangian methods", p. 3). It first appeared as FUNDP Technical Report 92/07 in 1992 (ref. 12, p. 28), five years before journal publication. It sits between LANCELOT A (1992, the augmented-Lagrangian code it compares itself with, p. 7) and Conn's DFO turn (S009).
**Read level**: main text pp. 1–32 read in full. Appendix A (p. 33), B.1 (pp. 34–36) and B.2 (pp. 36–38) read. From B.3 and C.1–C.6 (pp. 38–52) only the proof structure was read, i.e. the opening and closing of C.3 (pp. 44, 47) and the construction in C.4 (pp. 47–51). The inequality chains of C.1, C.2, C.5 and C.6 were not read in detail; the formulas there are garbled in the extraction.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a *numerical-analysis diagnosis of a revived method*. Classical log-barrier methods (Frisch; Fiacco–McCormick) were abandoned once Lootsma and Murray showed that the barrier Hessian's condition number blows up as µ→0 (p. 4). Karmarkar-era interior-point methods revived barriers, and the authors observe that the early-1970s lessons "seem to have been forgotten" in the rush to extend them to nonlinear programs (p. 4). For penalty functions, the ill-conditioning was fixed almost at once by augmented Lagrangians, which move the singularity by shifting the constraints (p. 5). The authors find it "rather strange" that the same device was not applied to barriers (p. 5). The problem is stated as min f(x) s.t. c_i(x) ≥ 0, l ≤ x ≤ u, solved by sequentially minimizing the Lagrangian barrier function Ψ(x,λ,s) = f − Σ λ_i s_i log(c_i + s_i) inside the box (eq. 1.4, p. 3). Seven practical reasons follow (pp. 7–8): active-set identification as in LP interior-point methods, less sensitivity to degeneracy than LANCELOT's gradient-projection augmented Lagrangian, numerical evidence from others on bound-constrained problems, no convexity or self-concordance assumptions, infeasible starts allowed by the shifts, easy combination with [11] for equalities, and no slack variables for large-scale problems.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: One-line insight: treat the log barrier exactly as the quadratic penalty was treated. Shift the constraints by s_i = µ λ_i^{αλ} (eq. 3.4, p. 12) and update the multipliers by the first-order formula λ̄_i = λ_i s_i/(c_i + s_i) (eq. 2.3, p. 9). Then µ need not go to zero, and the barrier's singularity stays away from the solution (pp. 5–6, 19–20). Motivating identity: the constraints are equivalent to s_i log(1 + c_i/s_i) ≥ 0, and Ψ is their classical Lagrangian up to a constant (eqs. 1.13–1.14, p. 6). The footnote on p. 6 notes that (1.8) and the Taylor expansion of (1.14) are similar.
  - *What changed vs Polyak's modified barrier and Gill et al.'s shifted barrier* (p. 6): the weights are tied to the multipliers and the shifts to µλ^{αλ} with αλ > 0. That choice gives Q-superlinear convergence of the inactive multipliers, where Polyak proves only a linear rate (p. 16). Its cost is that Polyak's method (αλ = 0) is excluded (p. 16).
  - *What stayed the same*: the outer-iteration skeleton of the 1991 augmented-Lagrangian paper. One test (3.8) on a scaled complementarity measure decides between updating the multipliers with µ kept (step 3) and reducing µ with the multipliers kept (step 4). Inner and outer tolerances ω_k and η_k are powers of µ (Algorithm 3.1, pp. 12–13). The test is described as "merely to detect when the penalty parameter is small enough for us to move from a globally convergent to a locally convergent regime" (p. 13). The authors also show that (3.8) is the same test as the 1991 one, rewritten as ||λ̄_k − λ_k|| ≤ η_k/µ_k (eq. 3.17, p. 15).
  - *Simple bounds are kept out of the barrier*, because a bound may be needed for a problem function to be well defined, and "such a constraint is commonly called a hard bound" (p. 8). They are handled by a projected-gradient inner test ||P(x_k, ∇Ψ_k)|| ≤ ω_k (3.5, p. 12). Any bound-constrained solver may be used for the inner iteration (projected-gradient methods, the CGT 1988 trust-region method, Nash–Sofer), so (3.5) acts as an inexact stopping rule (p. 14). The results survive a diagonally scaled test (8.12) (p. 27).
  - *Feasible restarts*: when µ is reduced, the next inner iteration needs c(x) + s_{k+1} > 0. §7 builds an auxiliary problem min ξ s.t. c(x) + ξ s_{k+1} ≥ 0 with a known feasible point (x_k, ξ = τ^{-1}). The solve is stopped as soon as ξ < 1, so an ordinary barrier method "need never enter its potentially dangerous asymptotic phase" (pp. 23–24). Alternatives with m or |C1| extra variables are given (pp. 24–25).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: AS1: f and c are C² on B (p. 3). AS2: the set of multipliers L(x*,0;x*,F1) is bounded (p. 15). AS3: the Jacobian of the active constraints restricted to the floating variables has full rank (p. 16). AS4: the second derivatives are Lipschitz (p. 17). AS5: the KKT-type matrices (5.3) are nonsingular for every admissible active/floating combination (pp. 17–18). AS6: single limit point (p. 19). AS7 and AS8: strict complementarity for the constraints and for the bounds (pp. 19–20). AS9: uniform positive definiteness of the barrier Hessian on floating variables (p. 21). There is no convexity or self-concordance assumption (p. 7). Simplifications the authors say are for exposition or proof: bounds reduced to x_i ≥ 0 (p. 8, generalized in §8.1, pp. 25–26); ω* = η* = 0 in the analysis (p. 15); appendix proofs given only for one parameter instance (αω = βω = 1, αη = 5/6, βη = 1/6, αλ = 1/2), with the general case in TR 92/07 (pp. 8, 33). AS6 is shown to be necessary for Theorem 5.3 by counterexample (Theorem 5.4, p. 20). The reliance on AS7 in Theorem 6.1 could be relaxed only under a condition whose truth "is not known" in general (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: This is an asymptotic analysis without complexity bounds, and the proofs sit in the appendices (p. 8).
  - *Global convergence (Thm 4.2, p. 16; proof B.2, pp. 36–38)*: case split per constraint, first c*_i ≠ 0 (with (1a) µ bounded away from 0 or (1b) µ → 0), then c*_i = 0. Driver identity: λ_i − λ̄_i = c_i λ̄_i / s_i (eq. 2.5, p. 9). Lemma B.1 (pp. 34–36) shows µ_k λ_{k,i}^{3/2} → 0 whenever µ_k → 0. The proof sums the multiplier updates between successive penalty reductions and bounds each by η_k. Multiplier boundedness comes from AS2 plus Fiacco's perturbation result (Lemma 4.1, pp. 15–16). Stationarity with respect to the bounds comes from the dominated/floating partition of the variables (eqs. 2.10–2.13, Lemma 2.1, pp. 10–11).
  - *Error bounds (Thm 4.3, p. 17; Lemma 5.1, p. 18)*: least-squares multiplier estimates (4.2) are differentiable under AS3 (p. 16). Lemma 5.1 removes the "a posteriori" term ||x_k − x*|| by using the nonsingular matrix (5.3) (p. 18).
  - *Penalty parameter bounded away from zero (Thm 5.3, p. 19; C.3, pp. 44–47)*: by contradiction. Assume step 4 runs infinitely often; then Lemma 5.1's bounds and the exponent conditions (5.13)–(5.15) force test (3.8) to hold at every late iteration, so step 4 runs only finitely often. The authors show the exponent conditions can be met by giving one concrete tuple (αω = 1, αη = 0.75, βω = 1, βη = 0.25, p. 20).
  - *Necessity counterexample (Thm 5.4, p. 20; C.4, pp. 47–51)*: a problem with two KKT points and distinct multipliers. An admissible cycle of iterates, with an "extra" iterate near the second KKT point, makes the test fail repeatedly, so µ → 0 when AS6 is dropped.
  - *Rates (Thm 5.6, Cor. 5.7, p. 21)*: inactive multipliers converge Q-superlinearly; active multipliers and x converge R-linearly, with the R-factor a power of µ_min.
  - *Second order (Thm 6.1, p. 22)*: decompose ∇²Ψ into H_ℓ + AᵀDA (6.1) with diagonal D whose inactive entries vanish.
- **D5 Experiments**: None in the paper. The authors say so: no detailed numerical evidence, and how inner solvers mesh with Algorithm 3.1 is not yet studied (p. 27). The only numbers are anecdotal. A "rather rudimentary implementation" solved over 90% of about 1000 problems, sometimes more efficiently than LANCELOT A, and the HSL code VE14 (bound-constrained QP) is cited as a success (p. 27). Motivation reason 3 rests on others' experiments (Conn et al. [17]; Nash et al.; Breitfeld–Shanno) (p. 7). Appendix A warns that parameter choice "appears to be significantly more critical than for augmented Lagrangian approaches" (p. 33).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper continues and corrects the barrier tradition: Frisch, Fiacco–McCormick, then the Lootsma/Murray ill-conditioning results, then Karmarkar and Gill et al. (p. 4). The explicit criticism is aimed at the interior-point community's forgetting (p. 4), not at named individuals. The closest relatives, Polyak's modified barrier and Gill et al.'s shifted barrier, are restated in the authors' own framework (eqs. 1.10–1.11, p. 6). Differences are stated on technical grounds: rates, and the exclusion of αλ = 0 (p. 16). Jittorntrum–Osborne are credited as the first move in this direction (p. 5). Polyak is thanked for conversations at Yorktown Heights (p. 27). The theorems are tied to CGT [11] one by one ("analog of", "in the spirit of", "direct analog of", pp. 11, 16, 17, 20, 21). The paper also contrasts itself with its own LANCELOT A on degeneracy and slack variables (pp. 7–8).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution is stated as two results in the abstract: global convergence, and a "potentially troublesome penalty parameter is bounded away from zero" (p. 3). A numbered list of seven motivations ends with "ample justification, both from a practical and theoretical point of view" (p. 8). An intuition paragraph follows the dense algorithm: "Although it might appear quite complicated, the idea behind Algorithm 3.1 is rather simple" (p. 13). The main text holds statements and the appendices hold proofs, "to keep the development of the theory as coherent as possible" (p. 8). Hedging is moderate and specific: "It is unclear how Algorithm 3.1 behaves when αλ < 1, in the absence of AS7" (p. 20); "We caution the reader" that µ bounded away from zero is not sufficient (p. 20); "it is not known if such a result holds in general" (p. 22). The authors decline to state more general tolerance-update rules because the conditions "seemed rather complicated and are unlikely to provide more practical updates" (p. 27).
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: The contribution is theory plus algorithm: global convergence, multiplier error bounds, µ bounded away from zero, rates, second-order limits, feasible-restart procedures, and a composite barrier/augmented-Lagrangian function Θ for mixed equality and inequality constraints (eq. 8.7, p. 26). The composite's convergence is "no worse" than either parent (p. 27). The limitations are concrete: no numbers (p. 27); the fit between inner solver and outer loop is not analysed (p. 27); restarts after a µ reduction may be inefficient (p. 27); AS7 is needed for well-conditioning near the limit (p. 20); αλ < 1 without AS7 is open (p. 20); parameter sensitivity (p. 33). Future work named: the inner-solver issue is "currently" being considered (p. 27). The number of inner iterations per outer iteration is analysed in TR 92/14 (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the paper's core move is to transplant the augmented-Lagrangian shift/multiplier device from penalties to barriers (pp. 5–6). It keeps the proven outer-loop machinery of [11] and then hybridizes both into Θ (p. 26).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): here the twin is a theory–theory pair (1991 augmented Lagrangian, then 1992/1997 Lagrangian barrier) rather than theorem plus code plus test. The paper openly lacks a numerical study (p. 27), and the only numerical support is anecdotal (p. 27). Method 1, step 1 (write the algorithm as a framework) is strongly evidenced. The inner solver is left open behind a termination test (p. 14), and Algorithm 3.1 is a "general algorithmic framework" (p. 12).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): simple bounds are handled exactly and kept out of the barrier (pp. 3, 8), slack variables are avoided for large-scale problems (p. 8), and equalities are added by structure (p. 26).
- Method 3 ⚠ (variant, by analogy): what is certified here is inner-solve accuracy (ω_k, η_k as powers of µ) rather than a model. The convergence theorem holds for any inner algorithm that meets the test (pp. 14, 16).
- new-pattern candidate: **Mirror the predecessor's architecture**. The paper deliberately copies the structure of its own earlier paper, theorem for theorem, so the two methods combine and their proofs are reused (pp. 8–9, 26–27).
- new-pattern candidate: **Recover a forgotten lesson**. The motivation comes from re-reading 1970s numerical analysis that the current fashion ignored (p. 4), and from asking why a known device was never transplanted (p. 5). This also appears in S009 (Winfield, p. 6).
- new-pattern candidate: **Show an assumption is needed by a constructed counterexample**. The authors build an admissible iterate sequence that breaks the conclusion when the assumption is dropped (Thm 5.4, pp. 20, 47–51).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Remove a method's intrinsic ill-conditioning by *shifting* the singularity with multiplier-weighted shifts (s = µλ^{α}), so the parameter that caused the ill-conditioning can stay bounded. Then prove it stays bounded (pp. 12, 19–20).
- Design a two-regime outer loop with one switching test. Update the multipliers when a scaled complementarity measure is below η_k; otherwise shrink µ. Set the inner and outer tolerances as powers of µ, write the exponent conditions out, and give one concrete tuple that satisfies them (pp. 12–13, 20).
- Specify the subproblem solver only through an inexact termination test (projected gradient ≤ ω_k, and allow diagonal scalings with bounded condition number). The theorem then covers every solver that passes the test (pp. 14, 27).
- To prove a parameter stays bounded away from zero, assume the reduction step fires infinitely often and use error bounds to show the acceptance test must eventually hold every time (C.3, pp. 44–47).
- Prove that an assumption cannot be dropped by constructing an admissible cycle of iterates between two KKT points with distinct multipliers (Thm 5.4, pp. 20, 47–51).
- For restarts, pose a phase-1 problem that has a known feasible point (x_k, ξ = 1/τ) and stop it as soon as ξ < 1. The auxiliary solve then never enters its ill-conditioned asymptotic phase (pp. 23–24).
- To keep the appendix readable, prove one concrete parameter instance, point to the technical report for the general case, and state that the instance is not a recommended setting (pp. 8, 33).

**Quotes**:
- "We have intentionally kept our development as close as possible to that of Conn et al. [11] in order to emphasize the unity between our approaches to shifting an exterior and interior penalty function" (p. 8)
- "Nor have we provided any detailed numerical evidence that the approach taken here is effective on general problems." (p. 27)

### S009 · Recent progress in unconstrained nonlinear optimization without derivatives (Mathematical programming 1997, DOI 10.1007/bf02614326)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K. Scheinberg, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in research trajectory**: Report 97/12 (16 Feb 1997), written for Conn, Scheinberg and Toint's invited semi-plenary at ISMP97, Lausanne (p. 2). It is the programme statement of the CST DFO line: it follows Conn–Toint 1996 (numerical results) and presents CST 1997 (Powell festschrift, the first convergence theorems) (p. 6). It lists the open problems that the later regression/underdetermined, structured and software work took up (p. 17).
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–20, including the reference list).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is practitioner demand. The problem is unconstrained smooth min f(x) where ∇f cannot be computed (eq. 1.1, p. 3). In "the applications presented to the authors" f is expensive, and derivatives are missing because f is a physical/chemical/econometric measurement or, "more commonly", the output of a large simulation whose source code is effectively unavailable. Proprietary simulation packages make this increasingly common (p. 3). The introduction then sorts the user's options (pp. 3–4). (i) Direct search (Nelder–Mead, PDS): little user effort, but smoothness is poorly exploited, so it needs many evaluations. (ii) Automatic differentiation: not applicable to a black box. (iii) Finite differences with quasi-Newton: "useful and sometimes surprisingly efficient". (iv) Model the function itself, which is the paper's focus. Case (iv) is backed by Powell's per-evaluation rate argument. The secant method gives rate ≈1.618 per function value; Newton with one extra value spent to estimate the derivative gives only √2 (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: One-line insight: put a quadratic interpolation model inside a trust-region framework, and make the *geometry of the interpolation set* an explicit, measurable object managed by the algorithm (pp. 7–9). The paper names three ingredients (pp. 7–9): (1) the model is fixed by interpolation m_k(y) = f(y) on Y with |Y| = (n+1)(n+2)/2, which needs poisedness; the interpolation determinant δ(Y) must be nonzero (eqs. 3.3–3.5, pp. 7–8); (2) an accepted point enters Y and some point must leave, chosen to keep the geometry good (p. 8); (3) if no progress is made, the geometry is checked and improved *before* the radius is blamed, since with exact derivatives Taylor's theorem guarantees that a smaller radius cures non-progress, whereas here the model itself may be inadequate (pp. 8–9).
  - The outline algorithm (pp. 9–10) encodes this. Step 4 shrinks Δ only "If ρk < η1 and Y was adequate in Bk when sk was computed"; otherwise Δ is kept. Step 5 moves to the best point in Y whenever ρ̂_k ≥ η0, so decreases found during geometry steps are not wasted (p. 10). "any such evaluation should be exploited if at all possible" (p. 10). Models less than fully quadratic, or even less than fully linear, are allowed early on, so the first few values are used at once (p. 10).
  - Two geometry measures are compared. Powell's uses Lagrange fundamental polynomials: the factor by which |δ(Y)| changes when y− is replaced by y+ equals |L(y+, y−)|, independent of the basis (p. 11), and checking adequacy costs 2(p−1) constrained quadratic maximizations (p. 12). CST's uses Newton fundamental polynomials built by procedure CNP (p. 14). Their pivots, computed anyway while building the model, measure poisedness, and the exact test ≠ 0 becomes a pivot threshold θ (p. 14). The paper stresses that the interpolant is unique for a poised Y. The new basis changes only the procedures for improving geometry and including x⁺_k, not the model (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is smooth and nonlinear, there are no constraints, and ∇f and ∇²f are unavailable (p. 3). The cost of evaluating f dominates all other costs (p. 12). The interpolation error bound needs a bounded d-th derivative (eq. 5.4, p. 15). Adequate geometry is defined operationally in two ways. For the Lagrange approach: all points within 2Δ and |δ| cannot be doubled by moving one point (Powell's "good", p. 11), later refined to (4.4)–(4.5) (p. 12). For the Newton approach: |Y| ≥ n+1, all y ∈ B_k, and Newton-polynomial values bounded by κ1 > κ0 (eqs. 5.7–5.9, p. 16). The slack κ1 > κ0 is "merely intended" to make the conditions attainable in view of Theorem 1 (p. 16). The authors admit the outline is "admittedly broad and simplistic" and has no stopping test (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No proofs; this is an expository survey. It states Theorem 1 (from CST 1997, Theorem 2): for any x_k and Δ_k one can build Y inside B_k with every Newton fundamental polynomial bounded by κ0 on B_k, and κ0 does not depend on x_k or Δ_k (p. 15). It reproduces the Sauer–Yuan/Sauer path-sum error bound. That bound makes |f(x) − m(x)| proportional to ||f^{(d)}||_∞/(d+1)! times a sum over paths of products of step lengths and Newton-polynomial values. The paper notes those values are byproducts of computing the generalized finite differences (eq. 5.4, p. 15). It also records that a finite number of replacements y+ = argmax |N_i^{[ℓ]}(x)| restores adequacy (eq. 5.10, p. 16). Global convergence is deferred to CST 1997 (p. 16). (Citation note: the error-bound property is attributed to "Sauer and Xu" on p. 15, while the same Math. Comp. 64 paper is cited as "Sauer and Yuan (1995)" elsewhere, including the reference list on p. 20. This looks like a name slip in the preprint.)
- **D5 Experiments**: None. The paper points to "Preliminary numerical results" in Conn–Toint 1996 and Powell 1996 (p. 12), and to "encouraging numerical results" in Conn–Toint 1996 (p. 6). The computational trade-offs are declared "currently ongoing" work (p. 17).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: A historical review credits Powell (1964, conjugate directions) as "one of the first to systematically explore" DFO (p. 5). It rehabilitates Winfield's 1969/1973 interpolation-plus-trust-region method as a very early trust-region method, predating Powell (1970), that "went mostly unnoticed" (pp. 5–6). The decisive difference from Winfield is Powell's (1994b) geometry-preserving update (p. 6). Powell's approach is described as "very elegant, if also potentially expensive" (p. 17). The CST approach is positioned as the one with a convergence theory and possible computational savings (pp. 13, 17). Nobody is dismissed. Every predecessor is restated in the same trust-region vocabulary. Footnotes track *software* (Powell's VA04/VA24 in the Harwell Subroutine Library, PRAXIS, COBYLA) and each code's interface to CUTE (pp. 5–6). Approximation theory (de Boor–Ron; Sauer–Yuan; Sauer) is imported for poisedness and error bounds (pp. 8, 14–15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The purpose is stated modestly: "not to provide a detailed inverstigation of an algorithm, but rather to introduce some basic concepts" (sic, p. 4). Concepts are taught by tiny counterexamples: six points on a line, or on a circle, do not determine a 2-D quadratic (pp. 7–8); Rosenbrock's valley x2 = x1² shows how geometry deteriorates (p. 8, fn. 6). Figure 1 shows the block structure for cubic interpolation on a grid (p. 13). Footnotes carry caveats, for example "This, of course, does not prevent the algorithm recording the best point found so far" (p. 7, fn. 4). The closing is upbeat but pinned to three things: theory, algorithm design and user demand (p. 18).
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: This is a conceptual and expository contribution: a unifying algorithm class plus two concrete geometry-management schemes. The open questions are concrete (p. 17): (a) the trade-off between "best possible geometry" and linear-algebra cost as the relative cost of f changes; (b) initial models when |Y| < p (design of experiments, thin-plate splines); (c) the theoretical meaning of pivot values; (d) larger n via structure, either by dropping monomials x_i x_j where the Hessian is known to be sparse or by partial separability with element models and a structured trust region; (e) noise, where interpolation should give way to approximation and geometry must be combined with "sufficient sampling"; (f) software, since the methods' potential depends on "high quality software" (pp. 17–18). The limitations are explicit: no numbers, and an outline that is "broad and simplistic" (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ (evidence, and the origin statement): geometry is made an explicit, measurable condition (adequate in B_k, pp. 9–10, 16). It is backed by an attainability theorem (Thm 1, p. 15) and a bounded-replacement restoration procedure (p. 16). Radius reduction is conditioned on certified geometry (Step 4, pp. 9–10), which prefigures the criticality/model-improvement logic of Method 3, step 3. The survey already counts the cost of geometry checks (2(p−1) maximizations, p. 12), which supports Method 3, step 5. Wording note: the abstract says "geometrical quality" (p. 3), whereas SKILL.md paraphrases it as geometric quality. The phrase "fully linear" here means only |Y| ≥ n+1 (pp. 10, 16), not the later error-bound definition.
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): DFO is entered only after AD and finite differences are ruled out for the target users (pp. 3–4). Structure (Hessian sparsity, partial separability) is the first named route to larger problems (p. 17). One nuance counts as a variant ⚠: Powell's per-evaluation argument (p. 4) is a reason to prefer modelling f over *estimated* derivatives, even when finite differences are feasible.
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): trust-region machinery is transplanted into DFO (p. 7). Multivariate approximation theory (Sauer–Yuan, de Boor–Ron) is imported (pp. 8, 14–15). A rival scheme (Powell's Lagrange polynomials) is presented alongside the authors' own rather than against it (pp. 11–12, 17). Coupling with direct search is noted (p. 4, fn. 1).
- Method 1 ✅ (stated evidence; no practice in this paper): theory is announced as done (CST 1997), numbers are pointed to elsewhere (p. 12), and software is declared the condition for the methods' potential to be realized (pp. 17–18).
- Method 2 ✅ (weak evidence): the survey tracks which DFO codes have CUTE interfaces (pp. 5–6).
- Method 4 ✅ (weak evidence): the demand comes from "applications presented to the authors" (p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: **Recover a forgotten lesson**. The authors rehabilitate an overlooked precursor (Winfield) as the origin of the idea and name what it lacked (geometry) (pp. 5–6). The same move appears in S007 (p. 4).
- new-pattern candidate: **Survey as a research agenda**. The paper closes with an itemized list of open problems (p. 17): cost versus geometry, initial models, structure, noise/regression and software. Each matches a later Conn–Scheinberg paper topic (not verified in this batch).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Justify a derivative-free design by *convergence rate per function evaluation*, not per iteration (secant ≈1.618 vs Newton-with-finite-difference √2; p. 4).
- Open an application-driven paper with a four-way triage of the user's options (direct search, AD, finite differences, model-based) and the specific reason each fails for the target user (pp. 3–4).
- In any model-based trust-region method, shrink the radius only if the model was certified adequate when the step was computed; otherwise repair the model and keep the radius (Step 4, pp. 9–10).
- Measure model quality with quantities the model construction already produces (Newton-polynomial pivots with a threshold θ), so the check costs no extra evaluations (pp. 14–15).
- Define a quality condition with a constant strictly looser than the one an existence theorem guarantees (κ1 > κ0), so the condition is always attainable and restorable in finitely many steps (p. 16).
- Explain a degeneracy with the smallest counterexample (six points on a circle for quadratic interpolation in the plane, pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "This simple example indicates that it may not be optimal to use function values to compute explicit derivative approximations." (p. 4)
- "Finally, the authors acknowledge that the potential of the methods outlined in this paper will only be fully realized when associated high quality software will become available to users." (p. 17)
