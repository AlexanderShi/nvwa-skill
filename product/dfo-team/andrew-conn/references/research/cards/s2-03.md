### S103 · Intensive numerical tests with LANCELOT (Release A): the complete results (FUNDP 1992, no DOI/arXiv; Report 92/15, updated 1995)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nick Gould, Ph.L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: This is the LANCELOT period of the CGT trio, with Conn at IBM T.J. Watson (p. 1). It is the data appendix, published as a separate report, of the numerical-experiments paper [3] (Report 92/16, p. 86). SKILL.md Method 1 lists that paper as the 1996 testing twin of LANCELOT. **Read**: prose in full (pp. 1–3, 18, 35, 52, 69, 86). The tables (pp. 3–17, 19–34, 36–51, 53–68, 70–85) were scanned and tallied by script, not read row by row.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Target class: problems (1)–(3), with smooth f and c, equality and inequality constraints, and simple bounds. The package is intended for problems where n and/or m are large (p. 2).
  - Purpose: the report is archival. Performance on 943 problems extracted from CUTE is discussed in [3], and this report provides, for future reference, the complete per-run results behind that discussion (p. 2).
  - The algorithms are not defined here. The reader is sent to [3] for the definitions (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & design of the report**: The design is a four-metric grid over 943 problems × 21 algorithmic variants.
  - Section 2 gives the problem characteristics: n, m, CUTE id, and counts of free, fixed and bounded variables, of linear and nonlinear objective groups, and of linear and nonlinear equality and inequality constraints. It also gives a *best value*, the lowest objective at a stationary point over all successful runs (pp. 2–3).
  - Sections 3–6 give minor iterations (pp. 18–34), gradient evaluations (pp. 35–51), cumulative CG iterations (pp. 52–68) and CPU seconds (pp. 69–85).
  - The 21 variant columns are default, scaling, mltf, semltf, noprc, diagonal, band(0), band(1), band(10), expband, seprc, gmpsprc, munksg, appGCP, l2norm, accBQP, bfgs, dfp, psb, sr1 and fdg (p. 19). The labels are defined only in [3], which was not read.
- **D3 Assumptions & run protocol** (p. 18):
  - Iteration limit: 1000.
  - Workspace limit: three million integers and/or doubles.
  - CPU limit: 18000 s on a DECstation 5000/200.
  - CPU times were measured under f77 3.0-2 without optimization and rounded to the nearest second (p. 69).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none. The report contains no theory and no discussion. All analysis is in [3] (pp. 1–2).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Failure taxonomy* (p. 18). Every unsuccessful run is coded, and all five cases count as failures:
    - |E| arithmetic error;
    - |M| workspace exceeded;
    - |F| no feasible point found;
    - |I| iteration limit reached;
    - |S| step comparable to machine precision;
    - |T| time limit reached.
  - *Wrong-minimum flag*: brackets mark a successful run whose final objective differs from the best value by more than 0.01% relative (p. 18). Example: BDEXP n = 500–5000 is bracketed for most variants (p. 19).
  - *Scaling families*: the same problem appears at several sizes, e.g. ARTIF with n = 12 to 5002 (p. 3).
  - *My own tally* of the tables (the report gives no summary):
    - Instances: 943 rows, 398 distinct problem names, and 228 instances with n ≥ 1000. The largest n is 15625 (JNLBRNG1) and the largest m is 31373 (NGONE) (pp. 3–17).
    - Successful terminations per variant, including bracketed runs (pp. 19–34): default 865, seprc 878 (the highest) and dfp 608 (the lowest; 227 of its failures are |I|).
    - Coverage: 920 of 943 problems are solved by at least one variant. The 23 problems solved by none are exactly the rows whose best value is 0.1000000D+26, which appears to be a sentinel (e.g. AGG, p. 3 vs p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: an infrastructure and reproducibility product. It is the complete raw evidence for a software paper, published as its own report (pp. 1–2).
  - Limits:
    - The variants are not defined in the report (p. 2).
    - No function-evaluation count is tabulated separately. The cost units are minor iterations, gradient evaluations, CG iterations and CPU time (pp. 2, 18).
    - No noisy variants and no engineering tier appear (pp. 2–86).
  - Positioning: the report cites only CUTE [1] (still in preparation, 1992), the LANCELOT book [2] and the analysis report [3] (p. 86).
  - Writing: two paragraphs of prose and 80+ pages of tables. The report describes itself as a record kept for future reference (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): this is the data layer of the LANCELOT testing twin. The report is explicitly the basis of the discussion in [3] (pp. 1–2), which is the numerical-experiments companion to the LANCELOT book [2] (p. 86).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): the shared public collection is CUTE, with 943 instances (p. 2). Per-option results are reported for all 21 variants, failures included (Method 2 step 5; pp. 18–85).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): the cost units are iterations, gradient evaluations, CG iterations and CPU time, which is natural for a derivative-based solver (Method 2 step 1 asks for function evaluations). There is no noisy tier and no engineering tier (Method 2 steps 2–3) (pp. 2, 18).
- New-pattern candidate: *archival complete-results report*. The per-run data are published as a separate citable report, so the analysis paper can stay short while every number remains checkable (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Publish a companion data report with every problem × every variant × every cost measure, plus a problem-characteristics table (n, m, bound and constraint types, best value), and cite it from the analysis paper (pp. 2–17).
- Code each failed run by cause (arithmetic error, memory, infeasible, iteration limit, tiny step, time limit), and state that all of them count as failures (p. 18).
- Flag runs that converged, but elsewhere: compare each final objective with the best value over all variants (relative tolerance 0.01%) and mark the difference, instead of counting such runs as plain successes (pp. 3, 18).
- Report several cost units side by side, and state the machine, the compiler, the optimization level and the rounding of the timings (pp. 18, 69).

**Quotes**:
- "These results constitute the basis on the discussion and analysis presented by the authors in [3]." (p. 1)
- "It is the purpose of the present report to provide, for future reference, the complete set of results on which this discussion is based." (p. 2)

### S155 · Discontinuous piecewise differentiable optimization I: Theory (INRIA 1992, no DOI/arXiv; Research Report RR-1694, HAL inria-00076929)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Marcel Mongeau · **Position in trajectory**: This is a nonsmooth-optimization line parallel to LANCELOT. Conn was at T.J. Watson and Mongeau at CRM Montréal (p. 4). The work grows out of Mongeau's Waterloo PhD thesis [30] (1991, p. 40); the text itself still calls the work a thesis (p. 7). It generalizes Conn's 1973 ℓ1 exact-penalty algorithm [8] (p. 7). **Read**: full text (pp. 4–38, including all proofs and the conclusion); references skimmed (pp. 38–41).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Problem (1): inf f(x) subject to f_i(x) = 0 (i ∈ E) and f_i(x) ≥ 0 (i ∈ I), where f and the f_i are possibly discontinuous piecewise linear functions (p. 4). A local infimum is sought (p. 6).
  - Entry: generalize Conn's own treatment of the ℓ1 exact penalty, a special continuous piecewise-linear problem, to general discontinuous piecewise-linear functions (p. 7). The penalty (3) with decreasing μ reduces the constrained problem to unconstrained piecewise-linear minimization (p. 7).
  - Stated gaps in the existing methods (pp. 6–7):
    - Subgradient methods converge slowly.
    - Bundle methods need a more sophisticated implementation.
    - Structure-exploiting methods (minimax, ℓ1) are faster but special.
    - Smoothing methods (Zang; Imo–Leech) are numerically unstable and costly to evaluate.
    - Practitioners use heuristics.
  - Motivating applications (cited from the literature, p. 7): VLSI floorplanning, plant layout, batch production, switching regression, hydro-electric discharge allocation and fixed-charge problems.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Decomposition (Definition 1, p. 11). Near x̂, f equals a smooth part f(x̂) + g^T(x − x̂) plus a sum of single-ridge terms, namely Σ v_i min(0, a_i^T(x − x̂)) over the active ridges.
  - Theorem 1 (p. 13): the decomposition exists and is unique when the active ridge gradients are linearly independent.
  - Proposition 1 (pp. 16–17): builds the decomposition from |A|+1 cell gradients instead of 2^|A| (p. 18).
  - Optimality (Theorem 2, p. 22). The point is a *dead point* (the reduced restricted gradient is zero, so g = Σ u_i a_i), and 0 ≤ u_i ≤ −v_i holds for every activity. This is a multiplier rule per activity, obtained from single-dropping directions (pp. 12, 21).
  - Algorithm (pp. 22–23), of active-set and projected-gradient type:
    - Take d = −P g.
    - At a dead point, obtain u by least squares and drop one activity along ±P_{−i} a_i.
    - Line-search from breakpoint to breakpoint, updating the directional derivative (22)–(23) and using a heap (pp. 23–24).
  - Kept from Conn 1973: the projection and active-set logic. Changed: the explicit restricted-gradient formula for the ℓ1 penalty (7) becomes a general decomposition (pp. 13, 19–20).
  - Discontinuous case (§4, pp. 27–35):
    - Work with the lower semicontinuous envelope (p. 27).
    - Faults are defined through *soaring* directions (pp. 27–28).
    - Only step 4 changes: soaring single-dropping directions are ignored (pp. 27, 35).
  - Nonlinear case (§5, pp. 36–38): only a sketch. It uses a first-order decomposition (Definition 3), ε-active ridges, horizontal plus vertical steps in the Conn–Pietrzykowski manner, and a Coleman–Conn-type second-order step.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Oracle: the ridges (a_i, b_i), the active set and each cell gradient c^P are available at any point. The authors stress that this is more information than a bundle method assumes (p. 11).
  - Algorithm assumptions: f is decomposable at every iterate and breakpoint, and the active ridge gradients are linearly independent at dead points (p. 22). In the discontinuous case, all points are non-contact points and the fault sets F⁺ and F⁻ are available (pp. 31, 35).
  - Deferred to Part II [13]: degeneracy and contact points (pp. 8, 20, 35).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Decomposition theorem: a change of variables y = Lx turns the active ridges into coordinates. Continuity then forces each component of the cell gradient to depend only on sign(y_i), which yields h(y) = y^T d^{full} + Σ (d_i^∅ − d_i^{full}) min(0, y_i) (pp. 14–15).
  - Uniqueness: tested along the directions P_{−k}a_k (p. 16).
  - Finite convergence (Theorem 3, p. 26) is a counting argument:
    - Non-dead steps only add activities, at most n in a row.
    - At a dead point, exactly one activity is dropped.
    - f is constant on a dead point's sign class, and f decreases monotonically, so no sign vector σ ∈ {−1,0,1}^|R| can recur. Finitely many σ exist, so the algorithm terminates.
  - Discontinuous case: the proofs are reused almost verbatim (the proof of Theorem 4 is said to follow that of Theorem 1 exactly, pp. 32–33; Theorem 5, pp. 34–35).
  - A necessary and sufficient decomposability condition (13) is stated. Its proof is omitted, and the condition is called impractical (p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments**: none in Part I. Implementation and numerical results are deferred to Part II (pp. 4, 38). The authors report that in their experience decompositions are easy to construct in practical problems (p. 18, citing [13]).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: theory plus an algorithm for continuous and discontinuous piecewise-linear functions (p. 38).
  - Limits, stated specifically:
    - Degeneracy, contact points, the constrained case, the implementation and the numbers are all in Part II (p. 8).
    - The nonlinear extension is only sketched, with a linear rate expected for the first-order version (p. 37).
  - Future work: compare with bundle methods, composite nonsmooth methods and Clarke's nonsmooth analysis (p. 38).
  - Positioning: generalizes Conn [8], Conn–Pietrzykowski [14], Coleman–Conn [6, 7] and Conn–Cornuéjols's facility-location projection method [11]. The last is shown to fit the framework with *nested ridges* (pp. 21, 24). It contrasts with Fourer's piecewise-linear simplex (p. 7).
  - Writing:
    - One running toy example, the square-base pyramid (2), is reused for every definition (pp. 6, 10, 18).
    - Intuition comes before proof (p. 14).
    - The two-part plan is announced in the abstract (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): Conn's 1973 ℓ1 exact-penalty projection algorithm is transplanted into a new problem class, and the new algorithm is presented as a generalization of his algorithm (p. 7). The later Conn–Cornuéjols facility-location method is shown to be an instance (p. 24).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the method deliberately asks for more structure (ridges, cell gradients) than a bundle-method oracle provides. The authors argue that structure-exploiting nonsmooth methods beat general bundle methods (pp. 6–7, 11).
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): the triad is planned as paired publications from the start. Part I is theory; Part II covers degeneracy, implementation and numerical results (pp. 4, 38).
- Method 3 ⚠ (variant, by analogy): a checkable geometric condition carries the theorem. Linear independence of the active ridge gradients guarantees that the local model (the decomposition) exists and is unique (Theorem 1, p. 13) and underlies the optimality test (p. 22). The degenerate case is deferred rather than handled (p. 20).
- Method 4 ⚠ (variant): the applications are listed from the literature (p. 7), not taken from a user.
- New-pattern candidate: *smooth + single-kink local decomposition*. Write the nonsmooth function locally as a linear term plus one min(0, ·) term per active kink, then read off a multiplier interval per kink (pp. 11, 22).
- New-pattern candidate: *minimal-change extension*. Extend to the harder class so that only one algorithm step and no proof skeleton changes (the lower semicontinuous envelope; only step 4 changes; the proofs are said to go through exactly as before) (pp. 27, 32, 35).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a piecewise function, prove that a canonical local form (linear part + Σ v_i min(0, a_i^T(x − x̂))) exists under a regularity condition. Then derive the optimality test and the descent directions from it, one kink at a time (pp. 11–13, 21–23).
- Prove finite termination by showing that the iterates never revisit a sign pattern of the active constraints while f strictly decreases (p. 26).
- To handle discontinuities, replace f by its lower semicontinuous envelope and forbid only the directions that jump up. Everything else in the continuous algorithm is kept (pp. 27–28, 35).
- In an exact line search along a piecewise-linear path, pop breakpoints from a heap and update the directional derivative at each breakpoint instead of sorting all of them (pp. 23–24).

**Quotes**:
- "(Hence, we are assuming that more information on the structure of the objective function is available than, for example, in a bundle method, which assumes that only one element of the subdifferential is known at any point.)" (p. 11)
- "We have hence set a framework for an algorithm dealing directly with discontinuities that could be involved in a non-differentiable optimization problem." (p. 38)

### S031 · Global convergence of a class of trust region algorithms for optimization using inexact projections on convex constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 1993, DOI 10.1137/0803009)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: N.I.M. Gould, A. Sartenaer, Ph.L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: This is the theory layer of the CGT programme, with Conn at IBM T.J. Watson (p. 1). It generalizes the 1988 bound-constrained trust-region theory [9] to general convex sets (pp. 3, 59). The report number is 90/4 (p. 2), which dates the work to the start of the LANCELOT period. **Read**:
- Read: abstract and introduction (pp. 1–4); the problem and assumptions (pp. 4–7); Algorithm 1 and its remarks (pp. 9–13); §3 in full with proofs (pp. 13–24); the RS and GCP algorithm statements (pp. 25–29); the §5 assumptions and the Theorem 26, 27 and 35 statements (pp. 34–37, 46); the Theorem 36 statement (p. 48); §7.1, §7.3–7.5 and the conclusion (pp. 50–58); Appendix C (p. 66).
- Skimmed or not read: proofs of Theorems 23, 24, 35 and 36 skimmed or not read; Lemmas 28–34 not read (pp. 38–46); §7.2 Theorems 37–38 and Appendix A not read (pp. 51–53, 61); Appendix B glossary not read (pp. 62–65).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Problem: min f(x) over a nonempty closed convex set X (pp. 4–5).
  - Entry: earlier convex-constraint trust-region theories rely on the explicitly computed Euclidean projected gradient (p. 3).
  - New features (p. 3):
    - uniformly equivalent arbitrary norms;
    - approximate gradients;
    - inexact *projected gradients*;
    - finite identification of the binding (in)equalities without partitioning X into faces.
  - Motivation: a polyhedral norm turns the projection into an LP. Network problems have cheap null-space bases from spanning trees, and multiperiod models have staircase matrices (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (pp. 9–10) is a framework of conditions.
  - Model: the model matches f at x_k (2.12), and its gradient error satisfies ‖g_k − ∇f(x_k)‖ ≤ κ₁Δ_k (2.13) (p. 6).
  - Generalized Cauchy Point (GCP), conditions (2.30)–(2.35):
    - it is feasible and lies within ν₂Δ_k;
    - it achieves a fraction μ₃ of χ_k(t_k), the best linearized decrease in a ball of radius t_k;
    - it satisfies Goldstein-type conditions (2.33) and (2.35), or has a length comparable to Δ_k (2.34).
  - Step: any feasible step inside the trust region that gives a fraction μ₄ of the GCP's model decrease (2.38).
  - Kept: the ratio test and the radius update (pp. 9–10). Changed: the repeated projections (convex nonlinear programs) are replaced by possibly inexact LPs (p. 10).
  - Choosing m_k = f makes the theory cover linesearch methods too (p. 10).
  - A model GCP procedure is given: bisection on a restricted piecewise-linear path that follows the boundary of X (RS and GCP algorithms, pp. 25–28).
  - Extensions:
    - an LP simplex or interior-point solver, stopped early through a weak-duality bound (§7.1, pp. 50–51);
    - linear equality constraints (§7.2, pp. 51–53);
    - noisy objective values (§7.5, pp. 57–58).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Global convergence:
    - AS.1: the level set is compact (p. 4).
    - AS.2: ∇f is Lipschitz (p. 4).
    - AS.3: the norms are uniformly equivalent (p. 7).
    - AS.4: Σ 1/β_k diverges, where β_k bounds the model curvature along the GCP and trial steps (pp. 19–20).
    - AS.5: β_k[f(x_k) − f(x_{k+1})] → 0 (p. 22).
  - Identification of the active set:
    - AS.6: the GCP also nearly minimizes the linearization over its own active constraints (p. 35).
    - AS.7: the step keeps the GCP's active constraints (p. 36).
    - AS.8: X is described by C¹ inequalities h_i (p. 37).
    - AS.9: LICQ.
    - AS.10: Dunn's nondegeneracy, −∇f ∈ ri N(x*).
    - AS.11: the gradient errors vanish asymptotically. The authors admit this assumption is not the weakest and is used to simplify the exposition (p. 37).
  - Whole-sequence convergence: AS.12 (f is C²) (p. 48).
  - AS.9 is called a rather strong constraint qualification (p. 53).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Criticality measure: χ_k[x] = |min ⟨∇f(x), d⟩| over x + d ∈ X and ‖d‖_(k) ≤ 1 (3.2) (p. 13).
  - Theorem 4 (pp. 13–14): the measures are equivalent across norms. The proof sandwiches the unit balls between two ℓ₂ balls and uses the fact that χ(t)/t is nonincreasing (Lemma 2, whose proof rescales the t₂-optimal step by t₁/t₂ and uses convexity, p. 8).
  - Theorem 9 (p. 17), Cauchy-type decrease: m_k(x_k) − m_k(x_k + s_k) ≥ c₃ χ_k min[1, Δ_k, χ_k/(1 + ω_k^C)], using a curvature measure along the step, i.e. a Rayleigh quotient (3.29).
  - Lemma 12 (pp. 19–20): Δ_k ≥ c₅/β_k, proved by contradiction at the first index where the bound fails.
  - Theorem 14 (p. 21): lim inf χ_k = 0, via Powell's lemma (Lemma 13) and AS.4.
  - Theorem 17 (pp. 22–24): lim χ_k = 0 over successful iterations, by a two-subsequence argument that uses AS.5.
  - Corollary 19 (p. 24): limit points are as critical as the accuracy of g_k allows.
  - Theorem 35 (p. 46): the active set is identified finitely.
  - Theorem 36 (p. 48): the whole sequence converges under second-order sufficiency.
- **D5 Experiments**: none. Typical constant values are listed, but the remaining constants are left to extensive testing (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution: theory plus a model algorithm (p. 58).
  - Limits:
    - The GCP algorithm is a feasibility model, not an efficient implementation. The inner solver, t₀ and special cases are left open (p. 28).
    - AS.9 and AS.11 are strong (pp. 37, 53).
    - Identification without LICQ is only weak, and a geometric version is ongoing research (p. 54).
  - Future: an application to network flow-conservation constraints is under study (p. 58).
  - Positioning: extends [4] (Burke–Moré–Toraldo), [9] (CGT 1988), [20] and [29] (Toint), in a more general setting (p. 3). The inexact-gradient device is taken from [29] (p. 54).
  - Critique of Carter's fixed relative accuracy [6]: a two-variable constrained example (7.28)–(7.29) shows that the required accuracy depends on the geometry of X, so an adaptive condition like (2.13) is needed (pp. 54–55).
  - Writing:
    - The new features are listed as bullets (p. 3).
    - Each algorithm condition is motivated in numbered remarks (pp. 10–13).
    - The authors choose the familiar vocabulary of classical optimization over convex-analysis prerequisites (p. 7).
    - A glossary and a one-page summary of assumptions are appended (pp. 4, 62–66).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ (evidence, pre-DFO precursor): model accuracy is an explicit, algorithm-independent condition that carries the theorem. Condition (2.13) bounds the gradient error by κ₁Δ_k, i.e. first-order accuracy proportional to the radius, which is the Taylor-like bound of Method 3 step 1. The theory is proved once for any model that satisfies (2.12)–(2.13) and the curvature conditions (pp. 6–7, 10, 19–21).
- Method 3 / Workflow D ✅ (evidence): §7.5 turns the rule that noise must be small against the predicted decrease into a theorem. With E_k = (f(x_k) − m_k(x_k))/(m_k(x_k) − m_k(x_k + s_k)), Theorems 14 and 17 survive if |E_k| stays below a modest bound (≈ 0.1) (pp. 57–58).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): this is the theory-only member. The GCP procedure is only a model (p. 28), the constants are left to testing (p. 13), and the application is still to come (p. 58). The 1995 report date on a Report 90/4 (p. 2) shows the slow theory side of the triad.
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence, taste #2): the theorem covers a relatively abstract algorithmic class, with conditions rather than a code (p. 10). Linesearch methods are covered too (p. 10).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the design aims to exploit cheap null-space bases in network and staircase problems through polyhedral norms and LPs (pp. 3, 29, 50–51).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the 1988 bound-constrained trust-region theory is carried to general convex sets. LP machinery (simplex bases, weak duality, interior-point duality gaps) is imported into the Cauchy-point computation (pp. 3, 50–51).
- New-pattern candidate: *assumption audit → single-lemma extension*. Locate the one place where an assumption is used ((2.12) appears only in Lemma 11), then generalize by patching that inequality (noisy f) (p. 57). The same move appears for AS.6: proofs called entirely similar, with χ_k replaced by χ_k^C (p. 36).
- New-pattern candidate: *inexact subproblem with a checkable certificate*. Replace an exact operator (projection) by a fraction-of-optimum condition and stop the inner solver once a duality bound certifies that fraction (pp. 9, 50–51).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a subproblem is expensive (projection onto X), require only a fraction μ₃ of the optimal linearized decrease χ_k(t). Then certify that fraction cheaply, e.g. by stopping a simplex method when ⟨g, d_ℓ⟩ ≤ −μ₃ × (the best dual objective so far) (7.12) (pp. 9, 50–51).
- Let the norm vary by iteration, and prove once that the criticality measures are uniformly equivalent (Theorem 4). The norm then becomes a free design choice, e.g. ℓ∞ or polyhedral norms that turn subproblems into LPs (pp. 7, 13–14, 29).
- Make the accuracy requirement on approximate gradients proportional to the trust-region radius (‖e_k‖ ≤ κΔ_k), not a fixed relative accuracy. Use a small constrained example to show why the fixed version fails (pp. 6–7, 54–55).
- Before generalizing a convergence proof, list which lemma uses which assumption, then extend by modifying only those lemmas. Here noisy f needs only a bound on the ratio of the noise to the predicted decrease (p. 57).
- After the algorithm statement, add numbered remarks that justify each condition, and put every assumption in a one-page appendix (pp. 10–13, 66).

**Quotes**:
- "Of course, this only describes a relatively abstract algorithmic class." (p. 10)
- "We emphasize that this GCP Algorithm is only a model, intended to show feasibility of our approach" (p. 28)
