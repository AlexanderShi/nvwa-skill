### S018 · Nonlinear programming via an exact penalty function: asymptotic analysis (Mathematical Programming 1982, DOI 10.1007/bf01585100)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. F. Coleman · **Position in trajectory**: Coleman was at Argonne and Conn at Waterloo (p. 3–4). This is the asymptotic half of a two-report pair. Its companion, the global analysis (CS-80-31, cited as [4]), has the global convergence proof and the numerical results (p. 4, p. 16). The exact-penalty line goes back to Conn–Pietrzykowski 1977 ([7], p. 16). The future-work paragraph (p. 15) announces the quasi-Newton sequel, which is S021. **Read**: full text including the references (pp. 3–17), from page images. The text file is empty (see source notes).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper enters through a gap in the rival method.
  - Recursive quadratic programming (RQP) has fast local behaviour but is not a robust global procedure. The authors judge that none of the attempts to globalize it ([5], [6], [9], [12], [14]) is yet entirely satisfactory (p. 4).
  - The authors' own exact-penalty method [4] already had both global and fast local convergence, with numerical support and a global convergence proof. This paper sets out to establish the superlinear properties rigorously and to compare the method directly with RQP (p. 4).
  - A second, technical entry: a geometric reading of the RQP search direction near a solution shows unnecessary computation and storage (p. 4–5).

  The problem is min f(x) subject to φ_i(x) ≥ 0, with f and φ_i continuously differentiable (1.1) (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: split a method's theory into a global paper and an asymptotic paper. The asymptotic paper analyses only the simple local algorithm that the global method reduces to near a solution (p. 4, p. 7, p. 14).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Rewrite the RQP step in range-space and null-space form and remove the part that does not matter.
  - Near x*, with the active set identified (the Powell [15] setting), the RQP step is d = h + v (p. 5, (2.5)–(2.7)).
    - The vertical step v = −A(AᵀA)⁻¹Φ(x^k) is a Newton-like step on the active constraints, using exact information at x^k (2.9) (p. 6).
    - The horizontal step h = −Z(ZᵀBZ)⁻¹Zᵀ(∇f + Bv) approximates the constrained Newton step for the Lagrangian, using only approximate gradient information at x^k + v^k (2.10)–(2.12) (p. 6).
  - The authors ask whether ZᵀBZ is a good approximation, and whether Zᵀ(∇L + Bv) is (p. 6). Powell [15] had shown that the accuracy of ZᵀBv hardly matters for a 2-step superlinear rate, so the term is dropped: h̄ = −Z(ZᵀBZ)⁻¹Zᵀ∇f (2.13) (p. 6).
  - The vertical step is then taken *after* h̄. The constraint values are re-evaluated at x^k + h̄^k, giving v̄ = −A(AᵀA)⁻¹Φ(x^k + h̄^k) (2.15)–(2.16), because the global convergence argument of [4] needs those evaluations. A is not recomputed, so no factorization changes (p. 7).
  - Stated consequences: h̄ + v̄ is not the QP solution, and only the projected Hessian ZᵀG_L Z is ever needed (p. 7).
  - Algorithm 1 (local) is exactly what the global method of [4] reduces to near a solution (p. 7–8). Multipliers come from least squares via QR (p. 8).
  - Changed relative to RQP: the ZᵀBv term is dropped; the tangential step comes first and restoration second; the n×n Lagrangian Hessian approximation is replaced by an (n−t)×(n−t) projected one. Kept: Powell's active-set-identified setting and unit steps.
  - Transferable technique: write the competitor's step in range/null-space form, sort each term by whether it uses exact or approximate information, and drop the term the existing theory says is unimportant (p. 5–7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Standing assumptions (p. 8): A) f and φ_i are C²; B) the second-order sufficiency conditions (Fiacco–McCormick) hold at x*; C) the iterates lie in a compact set W; D) the active constraint gradients are linearly independent on W.
  - Lemma 1 adds uniform bounds b1‖y‖² ≤ yᵀZᵀBZy ≤ b2‖y‖² (2.17) (p. 9).
  - The rate results assume x^k → x* (Theorem 1, p. 10) and ZᵀBZ → Z*ᵀG_L(x*,λ*)Z* (Theorem 2, p. 11; Theorem 3 (vi), p. 12). They also assume the active set has already been identified (p. 5).
  - The authors flag two gaps between theory and implementation:
    - the algorithm statement does not reflect the actual implementation, which is treated in [4] (Note (i), p. 8);
    - meeting the projected-Hessian hypothesis needs an expensive method such as gradient differencing along the columns of Z_k near x* (p. 15).
  - The negative result in §4 adds its own simplifications: exact Hessians and concave φ_i (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The rate is proved first, assuming convergence; local convergence comes second. The devices:
  - **Step bounds** (Lemmas 1–3, p. 9):
    - ‖h̄‖ ≤ L1‖x^k − x*‖, from ∇L(x*,λ*) = 0 and a bounded (ZᵀBZ)⁻¹;
    - ‖v̄‖ ≤ L2‖x^k − x*‖, using φ_j(x^k + h̄^k) = φ_j(x^k) + O(‖h̄^k‖²), which holds because h̄ is tangential;
    - hence ‖x^{k+1} − x*‖ ≤ L3‖x^k − x*‖.
  - **Separable 2-step criterion** (p. 10): the error is split as x − x* = A w + Z u (2.19)–(2.20). By Lemma 4, if ‖w^{k+1}‖/‖x^k − x*‖ → 0 and ‖u^{k+1}‖/‖x^{k−1} − x*‖ → 0, then ‖x^{k+1} − x*‖/‖x^{k−1} − x*‖ → 0. The proof is given as a single word.
  - **Theorem 1**: the range component converges superlinearly in one step. The device is the mean-value form φ_j(x^k) = ∇φ_j(ξ_j)ᵀ(x^k − x*) and an error matrix E_k → 0 (p. 10–11).
  - **Theorem 2**: the null component converges 2-step superlinearly. The devices are a Taylor matrix G̃_L with ∇L = G̃_L(x − x*) and error matrices Ẽ_k and Ē_k that tend to 0 under projected-Hessian convergence (p. 11–12). Theorem 3 combines the two with assumptions i)–vii) (p. 12).
  - **Local convergence by 2-step contraction** (Lemmas 5–6 and Theorem 4, p. 12–13): ‖A w²‖ ≤ ¼‖x⁰ − x*‖ and ‖u²‖ ≤ ¼‖x⁰ − x*‖ give ‖x² − x*‖ ≤ ½‖x⁰ − x*‖. The even iterates converge; the odd ones follow from Lemma 3.
  - **Negative result for the rival** (p. 14–15): with the penalty p(x) = f(x) − (1/μ)Σ min(0, φ_i(x)), exact Hessians and concave constraints, take x' with φ_i(x') = 0. The RQP direction d then gives p(x' + d) − p(x') = −½dᵀ[G_f + Σ(1/μ − 2λ'_i)G_{φ_i}]d + o(‖d‖²). If λ'_i ∈ (0, 1/(2μ)), one can build simple convex examples in which the bracket is negative definite. The penalty then increases at points arbitrarily close to x*. (This is the phenomenon later called the Maratos effect; the report does not use that name.)
  - Constants are not made explicit.
- **D5 Experiments**: none in this paper. Numerical results are in [4] (p. 4). The implementation behind them is described: rank-2 updating far from the solution, then a switch to gradient differencing near it (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper continues the authors' own [4] (global analysis) and [3] (second-order conditions for the exact penalty) (p. 4, p. 16).
  - Powell [15] provides the setting (active-set identification, the formula for d) and the key tool (2-step superlinearity with an inaccurate ZᵀBv) (p. 5–6).
  - Murray–Wright [14] are credited with also ignoring ZᵀBv at times (p. 7).
  - Han [12], [13] is criticized directly (p. 14): global and superlinear convergence do not mesh in his method. Superlinearity needs unit steps, a unit step need not decrease the penalty, and so the full algorithm must switch from a slow global method to a fast local one with no assurance of convergence.
  - The Chamberlain–Pedersen–Powell technique for forcing convergence [6] appears only in the list of globalization attempts (p. 4, p. 16).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Short: 12 printed pages.
  - The algorithm is *derived* from a geometric reading of the rival's step, not postulated (p. 4–7).
  - Assumptions are lettered A)–D) (p. 8), and the step from the separable lemma to the theorem is dismissed in one word (p. 10).
  - The concluding section is organized as a question about the rival: can RQP have both properties? It answers no with a construction, even in the convex case (p. 14–15).
  - Hedging is light and phrased as expectation (the full quasi-Newton version is expected to be developed, p. 15).
  - One slip: the future-work paragraph refers to an Algorithm 2 that does not appear in this report (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical: local convergence and a 2-step superlinear rate for the local phase of the Coleman–Conn exact-penalty method, and a proof by example that the RQP direction can increase the exact penalty arbitrarily close to x* (abstract p. 3; p. 12–15). The limit is concrete: the rate depends on projected-Hessian approximations converging to the true projected Hessian, which in practice needs gradient differencing near x* (p. 15). Future work (p. 15): a full quasi-Newton implementation, made plausible by Powell's result that projected-Hessian approximations need only be accurate along the search directions (a parallel to Dennis–Moré). S021 is that sequel.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the triad is spread over two reports. The global analysis with numbers is [4]; the local theory is here (p. 4, p. 16). The theorem is tied to the code by a reduction argument: the global method becomes Algorithm 1 near x* (p. 7, p. 14). The implementation's switch to gradient differencing near x* is exactly what satisfies Theorem 2's hypothesis (p. 11, p. 15). So the Workflow B checkpoint (the theorem must be about the algorithm actually run) is respected, but this paper itself has neither code nor numbers.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: Conn's exact penalty (the 1977 line, [7]) is combined with the rival school's fast local step, and Powell's RQP result is reused as a proof tool (p. 4–7, p. 14).
- Method 5 ✅ weak evidence: the structure of the constraint manifold is used to avoid the full Lagrangian Hessian and to avoid refactorization. Only the projected Hessian is needed, and A is not recomputed (p. 4, p. 7, p. 15).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (precursor): the rate theorem is carried by an explicit model-accuracy condition, ZᵀBZ → Z*ᵀG_L Z* (Theorem 2, p. 11). The code enforces it by switching the model builder to finite differences near x* (p. 15). The condition concerns Hessian accuracy, not sample-set geometry.
- New-pattern candidate: *global-to-local reduction*: prove that the global method reduces to a simple local algorithm near the solution, then analyse only that algorithm (p. 7, p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *counterexample against the rival in the conclusion*: show by construction that the competing method's step lacks the property yours has (p. 14–15).
- New-pattern candidate: *promise-then-deliver sequel*: the future-work paragraph names the next theorem and the prior result that makes it plausible. S021 delivers it two years later (p. 15; S021 p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: *rate first, convergence second*: prove the rate assuming x^k → x*, then prove local convergence separately (p. 8–13; also S021 p. 17–26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Write the competitor's step as range-space plus null-space parts and ask, for each part, whether it uses exact or approximate information. Remove the part the theory says is unimportant (p. 5–7).
- [D4] Split the error into range and null-space components, and use a separable criterion: a one-step rate on one component and a two-step rate on the other give a 2-step superlinear rate (Lemma 4, p. 10).
- [D4] Prove local convergence by a 2-step contraction: bound each component by ¼ of the old error to get ½ overall, and handle the odd iterates with a linear bound (p. 12–13).
- [D4] Build a local negative result for a rival: expand your merit function along the rival's step, choose multipliers so the quadratic form is negative definite, and exhibit points arbitrarily close to the solution where the merit function increases (p. 14–15).
- [D8] End with a future-work paragraph that names the next theorem and the published result that makes it plausible (p. 15).

### S021 · On the local convergence of a quasi-Newton method for the nonlinear programming problem (SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis 1984, DOI 10.1137/0721051)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: T. F. Coleman · **Position in trajectory**: Coleman was now at Cornell and Conn at Waterloo (p. 1, p. 3). The paper is a direct extension of the local Newton method of Coleman–Conn 1982a,b (p. 6), cited as *Math. Programming* 24, to appear (p. 27). It delivers the quasi-Newton version announced as future work in S018 (S018 p. 15). **Read**: full text including the references (pp. 1–28), from page images. The text file is empty (see source notes).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an asymmetry between the unconstrained and constrained cases.
  - Unconstrained: DFP and BFGS have two properties, local Q-superlinear convergence and positive-definite Hessian approximations (p. 5).
  - Constrained: the authors know of no truly Q-superlinear quasi-Newton method for the nonconvex case (p. 5).

  Each prior attempt is checked against this gap (p. 5–6):
  - Powell 1978 gives sufficient conditions for a 2-step Q-superlinear SQP rate, but proves only R-superlinear convergence for his modified BFGS;
  - Han 1976 is Q-superlinear only in the convex case;
  - Boggs–Tolle–Wang 1982 give conditions, but no update is known to satisfy them;
  - Tanabe 1981 proposes projected updates without convergence results.

  The diagnosis, given as the authors' opinion (p. 6): these methods carry a full n×n positive-definite approximation, although only the projection of the Lagrangian Hessian needs to be positive definite at the solution. Gill–Murray 1974 had recurred a projected approximation for linear constraints only (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: build the gap statement as a list of prior methods, each paired with the exact property it lacks (R- vs Q-superlinear, convex only, conditions without an update, no theory) (p. 5–6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Recur a symmetric positive-definite (n−t)×(n−t) matrix B that approximates Z*ᵀ∇²L(x*)Z*, using a projected DFP or BFGS update (abstract p. 3; (1.5), p. 7).
  - The method (3.1)–(3.8) (p. 13):
    - h = −Z_k B_k⁻¹ Z_kᵀ∇f_k and x⁺ = x^k + h;
    - s = Z_kᵀ(x⁺ − x^k) and y = Z_kᵀ[(∇f(x⁺) − C(x⁺)λ^k) − ∇f_k];
    - B is updated by projected DFP (3.5);
    - v = −C_k(C_kᵀC_k)⁻¹c(x⁺) and x^{k+1} = x⁺ + v;
    - λ^{k+1} is the least-squares multiplier.
  - The secant pair is formed from the horizontal step alone, in reduced coordinates.
  - The vertical step uses c at x + h (1.6), not at x (1.4). All results hold with either choice (p. 7, p. 13). (1.6) is chosen because, in the discrete-Newton case, it guarantees that an exact penalty function decreases near x* (Coleman–Conn 1982b). The authors state that this is not yet proven for quasi-Newton B (p. 7).
  - DFP is analysed so as to follow Broyden–Dennis–Moré 1973 and Dennis–Moré 1974. The results also hold for projected BFGS (3.5') (p. 12–13).
  - Kept: the DFP/BFGS formulas and the Dennis–Moré analysis. Changed: the dimension of the matrix, the update data (reduced steps, and a gradient difference shifted by the multipliers), and the order of steps (horizontal step first, then restoration).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Equality constraints c_i(x) = 0, with f and c_i C² on an open convex set D (p. 8).
  - The singular values of C(x) are uniformly bounded above and below on D (p. 8).
  - Z(x) is a Lipschitz function with ZᵀZ = I and CᵀZ = 0; its computation follows Coleman–Sorensen (p. 8–9).
  - Second-order sufficiency holds at x*. By eigenvalue continuity, this makes the two-argument projected Hessian H(x,y) uniformly positive definite near (x*,x*) (p. 9).
  - Hessians are bounded and Lipschitz on a D of small radius (p. 9), and finite convergence does not occur (p. 10).
  - The local theorems require x⁰ close to x* and B₀ close to H* in the weighted norm (Theorems 3.14 and 3.15, p. 24, p. 26).
  - Convexity is not assumed (p. 26). Inequality constraints are covered only once the active set is identified (p. 26). The smooth-Z(x) requirement is flagged as needing a careful QR implementation (p. 26).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The analysis has two layers.
  - **(i) An algorithm-independent sufficient condition** (Theorem 2.1, p. 10). Suppose x^k → x*, ‖x^{k+1} − x*‖ = O(‖x^k − x*‖), and the step-equation residuals r_L = Zᵀ∇f + H_k Zᵀδ and r_c = c + Cᵀδ satisfy ‖r_L‖ + ‖r_c‖ = o(‖Zᵀ∇f‖ + ‖c‖) (2.5). Then ‖x^{k+1} − x*‖ = o(‖x^{k−1} − x*‖).
    - The proof has three parts: the range component w, then the null component u, whose error splits into four terms T1–T4, then the combination (p. 10–12).
    - The authors call the conditions slightly more general than Powell's, because no algorithm class is presupposed (p. 7, p. 12). They place them in the spirit of Dembo–Eisenstat–Steihaug (p. 7).
  - **(ii) Verification for this algorithm.**
    - Perturbation bounds for λ and for the projected Hessian, polynomial in ‖x^k − x*‖ (Lemmas 3.0–3.2, p. 13–15).
    - Lemma 3.3: ‖My − M⁻¹s‖ ≤ ⅓‖M⁻¹s‖ with M = H*^{−1/2} (p. 15–16). This is the one hypothesis needed by Dennis–Moré's bounded-deterioration lemma, which is reproduced as Lemma 3.4 in the weighted Frobenius norm ‖Q‖_M = ‖MQM‖_F (p. 16–17).
    - Theorem 3.5: the Dennis–Moré-type condition (3.11), ‖(B_k − H*)Z_kᵀ(x^{k+1} − x^k)‖/‖x^{k+1} − x^k‖ → 0, holds provided Σ‖x^k − x*‖ < ∞ (p. 17–18).
    - Lemma 3.6 checks (2.5) for the method (p. 18–19), and Theorem 3.7 gives the rate (p. 20).
  - **Local convergence**, proved afterwards:
    - step bounds and a linear bound (Lemmas 3.8–3.9, Corollary 3.10, p. 20–21);
    - a quadratic range component, ‖w^{k+1}‖ ≤ K9‖x^k − x*‖² (Lemma 3.11, p. 21–22);
    - ‖u^{k+1}‖ ≤ ¼‖x^{k−1} − x*‖ (Lemma 3.12, p. 22–23), giving a 2-step contraction by ½ (Corollary 3.13, p. 23).
  - **Summability** (Theorem 3.14, p. 24–25): proved by induction. The Banach perturbation lemma (Ortega–Rheinboldt) keeps ‖B_k⁻¹‖ ≤ 2γ, and the deterioration increments form a geometric sum (3.26)–(3.27). The main result is Theorem 3.15 (p. 26).
  - A small device: hᵀv = 0 gives ‖x⁺ − x^k‖ ≤ ‖x^{k+1} − x^k‖ (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The conclusion states that the method's practical performance is unknown and will be the subject of future work (p. 26).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper extends the authors' own 1982a,b papers (p. 6).
  - Rivals are placed by precise gap statements, not by polemic: Powell, Han, Boggs–Tolle–Wang and Tanabe (p. 5–6).
  - Gill–Murray 1974 is credited for the linear-constraint case (p. 6).
  - The borrowing from Broyden–Dennis–Moré 1973 and Dennis–Moré 1974 is announced in the roadmap (p. 7) and labelled wherever it is used: a reproduced lemma (p. 16), results that follow almost directly from Dennis–Moré (p. 17), and the local-convergence proof, which borrows heavily from Broyden–Dennis–Moré (p. 23).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The abstract names the pivotal feature and sets the word projection in bold (p. 3).
  - A roadmap follows the motivation (p. 7).
  - The standing assumptions are set in bold inside the prose (p. 8–10).
  - The diagnosis of earlier methods is flagged as opinion (p. 6).
  - A design choice whose justification is proved only for a simpler case is disclosed together with its motivation (p. 7).
  - The conclusion is half a page with three open problems (p. 26).
  - About 19 pages of theory and no numbers (p. 5–26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical: local 2-step Q-superlinear convergence, without convexity, for a quasi-Newton method that keeps a positive-definite approximation to the projected Hessian only (p. 26). The limits are specific:
  - the result is local only, and the rate is 2-step rather than 1-step;
  - there is no numerical experience (p. 26);
  - it is not known how to modify projected approximations when the active set changes (p. 26);
  - the penalty-decrease property is unproven for quasi-Newton B (p. 7).

  Future work: practical performance, implementation, a line search and globalization (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✗ contradiction (partial): a theorem-only paper whose practical performance is declared unknown (p. 26). This contradicts the SKILL.md Research Taste claim, under warning sign 2, that a theorem with no code and no numbers never occurs in the confirmed record. - Method 6 ✅ evidence: the unconstrained Dennis–Moré / Broyden–Dennis–Moré quasi-Newton machinery is transplanted into the null space of a constrained problem (p. 7, p. 16–17, p. 23).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the paper exploits the fact that only the projected Hessian must be positive definite, and so approximates a smaller matrix (p. 6).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the rate is carried by a model-accuracy condition *along the steps* ((3.11), p. 17–18), obtained from bounded deterioration, not by convergence of the whole matrix. This weakens the hypothesis S018 needed (S018 p. 11, p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *two-layer rate analysis*: an algorithm-independent sufficient condition in terms of step residuals, then a separate lemma verifying it for the specific method (Theorem 2.1 and Lemma 3.6, p. 10, p. 18–19).
- New-pattern candidate: *promise-then-deliver sequel* (S018 p. 15 → this paper, p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: *rate first, convergence second* (Theorem 3.7 before Theorems 3.14–3.15, p. 20–26).

### S008 · Global convergence of a class of trust region algorithms for optimization with simple bounds (SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis 1988, DOI 10.1137/0725029)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: the first paper of the CGT trio, with affiliations at Waterloo, Harwell and Namur (p. 1). It was received on 25 April 1986 (p. 1). Its testing twin S011 was received on 6 October 1986 (S011 p. 2), and both appeared in April 1988. S008 cites the twin as "reported elsewhere [5]" (p. 2, p. 28). It is the theoretical base on which the SR1 anomaly of S011 and later the LANCELOT line build. **Read**: full text, including the second-order section §3.3 and the references (pp. 1–28). Formulas were checked against rendered PDF pages 6, 7, 11, 14 and 16.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is practical frequency plus a theory gap.
  - Bound-constrained minimization is "probably one of the most common types of constrained optimization problems encountered in practical applications" (p. 1). Its difficulty is combinatorial: finding the active set (p. 1).
  - Bertsekas's projection method [1] has a rather complete theory, but it is unclear how to extend that theory to nonconvex problems while keeping global convergence (p. 1).
  - Trust regions give global convergence on nonconvex unconstrained problems (Moré's survey [12]), and extensions to equality constraints [3], [4], [7], [15], [16] and to linear inequalities [8] exist (p. 1–2).

  The stated purpose is a general global convergence theory for a trust-region algorithm for nonconvex problems with simple bounds. It merges the unconstrained ideas of [12] and [14] with the bound treatment of [1] (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: find a result that is proved for a restricted class (here convex problems), and carry a globalization framework from a neighbouring problem class into it (p. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Generalize the Cauchy point.
  - Scale the gradient, D_kᵀD_k w_k = g_k (7). Follow the projected polygonal path P[x_k − t w_k], which satisfies a norm-increasing property (8). The generalized Cauchy point (GCP) is the first local minimizer of the model along this path inside the trust region (9)–(11), shown in Fig. 1 (p. 3).
  - Any feasible trial step is accepted if it achieves a fixed fraction β1 of the GCP model decrease (12) and stays in an enlarged trust region (13) (p. 4). This generalizes Moré's conditions [12] (p. 4).
  - The step is deliberately *not* required to be the quasi-Newton step −B_k⁻¹g_k, unlike Powell [14], because that step can be costly for large n (p. 4).
  - Diagonal scalings D_k are kept for invariance (diagonal changes of variables are the only ones that preserve the structure of the bounds) and to allow preconditioned conjugate gradients (p. 4).
  - An ℓ∞ trust region is allowed, so that the region is a box aligned with the bounds (p. 4).
  - The ratio test and radius update follow Moré (p. 4–5).
  - All iterates are feasible, which helps with "hard" constraints, where f or ∇f is undefined outside the bounds (p. 5).
  - Kept: Moré's trust-region mechanism and Powell's weak Hessian condition. Changed: the Cauchy point is taken along a projected path; there is no Newton-step requirement.
  - Transferable technique: define the minimal *checkable* step property (a fraction of the decrease at a cheap reference point) and let every implementation choice above it stay free (p. 3–4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The assumptions form a ladder; each result names the subset it uses.
  - AS.1: the intersection of the level set with the feasible region is compact with nonempty interior. AS.2: f is C² (p. 2).
  - AS.3: the scalings are diagonal with bounded inverses (p. 5).
  - AS.4: Σ 1/b_k = ∞, with b_k = 1 + max_{i≤k}‖D_i^{−T}B_iD_i^{−1}‖ (30)–(31) (p. 6). The authors justify it as the "weakest possible" condition of this kind, by Powell's counterexample. BFGS on convex problems satisfies it, as does a safeguarded SR1 (see [5]) (p. 7).
  - AS.5: bounded D_k. AS.6: lim b_k[f(x_k) − f(x_{k+1})] = 0 (p. 17).
  - AS.7: strict complementarity, together with the condition (155) that the bounds active at the GCP stay active at the trial point (p. 19).
  - AS.8–AS.10 are needed for second-order results: a step that captures an eigenvector of B_k, and Hessian approximations that reflect negative curvature (p. 25).
  - The authors flag that the algorithm is "obviously a theoretical algorithm", with no stopping rule and no step details (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: A three-part plan, announced in advance (p. 7).
  - **(1) Sufficient decrease on a piecewise quadratic** (Lemmas 2–6, p. 8–13).
    - Work in the scaled space (p. 7–8).
    - Between breakpoints the path is linear. The slope of q_k(t) is bounded by −α_k² + 2t c2²‖B_k‖ (75) (p. 11), which gives q_k(t) ≤ f(x_k) − ½α_k²t on an explicit interval (p. 9–11).
    - Lemma 3 bounds the path parameter below by a quantity depending only on h_k and the problem (p. 8–9).
    - The bound-constrained analogue of the Cauchy condition (32) is f(x_k) − m_k(x_k + s_k) ≥ ½c3[h_k^s]² min([h_k^s]²/b_k, Δ_k) (79) (p. 11). The criticality measure is h_k = ‖P[x_k − w_k] − x_k‖ (29), shown in Fig. 2 (p. 6).
  - **(2) Global convergence** (p. 14–19).
    - Lemma 7: if criticality stays above ε, then Δ_k ≥ c4/b_k (102). The proof takes the first iteration r at which (c5σ1² + b_k)Δ_k falls below a threshold, shows that iteration r−1 was very successful, so Δ_r ≥ Δ_{r−1}, and derives a contradiction (p. 14–15).
    - Theorem 8, lim inf h_k = 0, uses a counting argument. Iterations are split into J1 = {k ≤ pS(k)} and J2 = {k > pS(k)}, where S(k) counts successful iterations and p is chosen so that γ2γ1^{p−1} < 1. Σ_{J1} 1/b_k is bounded by repeating successful indices p times; Σ_{J2} 1/b_k is bounded by the geometric radius bound (126). Both sums are finite, which contradicts AS.4 (p. 15–16).
    - The authors note that this proof differs from Powell's, while the second statement follows Powell closely (p. 14).
    - Theorem 11 gives lim h_k = 0 under AS.5–AS.6 (p. 18–19).
  - **Active set** (p. 19–22). Under AS.7, Lemma 12 (connected sets of limit points share an active set) and Lemma 13 (once picked up, a bound stays active) lead to Theorem 14: the active set is identified finitely. The asymptotics are then those of an unconstrained method (p. 22).
  - **(3) Second order** (p. 22–27).
    - Theorem 15: the whole sequence converges if B_k is uniformly positive definite on the free subspace and the Hessian there is nonsingular (p. 22–24).
    - Theorem 17: some limit point has a Hessian that is positive semidefinite on the free subspace. The proof uses a Rayleigh-quotient condition on B_k (204) (p. 25–27).
- **D5 Experiments**: none here. Preliminary numerical results "are reported elsewhere [5]" (p. 2), and the GCP's efficient computation is deferred to [5] (p. 4). [5] is S011.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper builds on Bertsekas [1] for bounds, Moré [12] for the trust-region framework and step conditions, and Powell [14] for the weak Hessian condition and the global convergence argument (p. 2, p. 7, p. 14).
  - Gay [8] is contrasted on cost: it may need several linear-system solves per step, whereas this method uses a line search along a piecewise-linear arc plus one refinement (p. 27).
  - A Harwell library bound-constrained code (the text file reads "V08") is praised as "remarkably efficient" but said to lack the theoretical foundation this paper provides (p. 27).
  - Criticism of others is limited to cost and missing theory.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Two figures give the key constructions geometrically: the GCP (p. 3) and the criticality length h_k (p. 6).
  - Every assumption is followed by a comment on what it means and who else uses it (p. 2, p. 5, p. 7, p. 17).
  - The authors warn against over-reading their own results: one must be "cautious in interpreting" lim inf h_k = 0 as convergence (p. 16), and the second-order result does not ensure a local minimizer (p. 27).
  - The conclusion opens with "We believe that the theory presented in this paper is interesting for several reasons" and lists them (p. 27).
  - Hedging is light and attributed ("seems to us", p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory for a *class* of methods: global convergence to critical points, finite active-set identification, and second-order results for bound-constrained trust-region methods, under weak Hessian conditions (p. 27). It is also a design suited to large problems (projected search plus truncated preconditioned CG, and partially separable updating) (p. 27). The limits are named:
  - it is a theoretical algorithm (p. 5);
  - a true limit (not only lim inf) needs AS.5 and AS.6 (p. 17);
  - the true limit for a_k cannot be obtained because a_k is discontinuous (p. 19);
  - the second-order point need not be a minimizer (p. 27).

  Future work: linear and nonlinear constraints (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the theory/testing twin with S011, cross-cited, submitted six months apart and published in the same month (p. 1–2, p. 28; S011 p. 2–3).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence for step 1 and Taste mark 2: the theorem covers a class. Any step with a fraction of the GCP decrease qualifies; B_k is free subject to AS.4; the scaling D_k is free subject to AS.3 (p. 4, p. 6–7, p. 27).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: unconstrained trust-region theory (Moré, Powell) is transplanted to bounds and merged with Bertsekas's projection (p. 2, p. 14).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: bounds are handled exactly by projection, iterates stay feasible (useful for "hard" constraints), and only structure-preserving diagonal scalings are used (p. 4–5). Partial separability is flagged for large problems (p. 27).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (precursor): the theorem is carried by a checkable certificate that the code must enforce, the fraction of the GCP model decrease (12)–(13) (p. 4, p. 7). The model itself is left almost free (AS.4). This is the same interface role that geometry plays in the later DFO theory, but here it certifies the step, not the model.
- New-pattern candidate: *generalize the Cauchy point*: define a cheap reference step whose decrease every admissible step must match up to a constant; the whole convergence theory then rests on that one inequality (p. 3–4, p. 7, p. 11).
- New-pattern candidate: *graded assumption ladder*: AS.1–AS.10, each added only for the stronger result that needs it (p. 2, p. 5–6, p. 17, p. 19, p. 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Define the generalized Cauchy point as the first minimizer of the model along the projected (scaled) gradient path within the trust region. Accept any feasible step with a fixed fraction of its decrease (p. 3–4).
- [D3] Justify a weak condition as the weakest possible by citing the counterexample that breaks any weaker one, and list which common updates satisfy it (p. 7).
- [D4] Minimal-counterexample device for radius bounds: take the first iteration at which radius × curvature bound falls below a threshold, and show that the previous iteration must have been very successful, a contradiction (Lemma 7, p. 14–15).
- [D4] Counting argument for Σ1/b_k: split the iterations by whether k ≤ pS(k), and bound one part by monotonicity and the other by a geometric radius bound (p. 15–16).
- [D4] Use a projected-step length h_k = ‖P[x_k − w_k] − x_k‖ as the criticality measure, and draw it (p. 6).
- [D4] Prove finite active-set identification under strict complementarity, then import the unconstrained rate theory unchanged (p. 19–22).

### S011 · Testing a class of methods for solving minimization problems with simple bounds on the variables (Mathematics of Computation 1988, DOI 10.1090/s0025-5718-1988-0929544-3)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. I. M. Gould, Ph. L. Toint · **Position in trajectory**: the testing twin of S008 ([3] here). Its SR1 observation (p. 20) is the anomaly that S010 (1991, batch c01) turned into a theorem. Its closing research programme, an augmented Lagrangian with bound-constrained subproblems (p. 24), is the LANCELOT plan. **Read**: full text (pp. 2–24), all ten tables (pp. 13–22) and the references (pp. 31–33). The appendix of test problems (pp. 24–31) was skimmed: the problem names, definitions, bounds and the variant rules were read, but the solution vectors were not checked.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The paper states its entry in its own words: to "demonstrate the viability" of the methods whose convergence was studied in the previous paper (p. 3). Its positioning points (p. 2–3):
  - active-set methods change the active set slowly and need many iterations to correct a bad initial choice, a drawback for large problems (p. 2);
  - bent-line-search methods (Bertsekas, Dembo–Tulowitzki) change it fast, but their theory covers convex problems only (p. 2);
  - for SQP-type subproblem methods, "We know of no experience with this class of method." (p. 3).

  The scope is deliberately small: small dense problems, the largest with 45 variables, with large-scale software postponed (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: give the testing companion a single stated job (viability of the analysed class) and a declared scope, so that the reader knows what the numbers can and cannot show (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The paper turns the theoretical algorithm into a concrete one while staying inside the theory.
  - The ℓ∞ trust region is intersected with the bounds to give a box (2.5) (p. 5).
  - The GCP is redefined: the first minimizer along the projected steepest-descent path in the box, so the search may continue along the trust-region boundary. The authors check that "Our existing theory covers the new definition" (p. 5).
  - The GCP is found by breakpoint recurrences needing one sparse matrix–vector product and two inner products per breakpoint (3.4)–(3.5). In the worst case each column of B is accessed once (p. 7–8).
  - Conjugate gradients then run on the free variables and stop when (p. 5–6):
    - (i) a relative residual test η_k = min(0.1, ‖ĝ‖^{1/2})‖ĝ‖ is met (2.8);
    - (ii) a bound is hit, which also covers negative curvature, keeps feasibility and avoids restarts;
    - (iii) an iteration limit is reached.
  - Hessian growth is safeguarded by ‖B^(k)‖ ≤ c1 + k c2 (2.10), which implies the global-theory condition (2.9) (p. 6).
  - No line search is ever used (p. 3).
  - The full algorithm is stated step by step (p. 8–10). The only varying component is B^(k), with five options (p. 10–11):
    - exact Hessians;
    - BFGS and DFP, skipped when curvature ≤ 10⁻⁸ (5.1);
    - PSB, which allows indefinite matrices and needs no growth safeguard;
    - SR1, skipped when the correction norm exceeds 10⁸.
  - Transferable technique: when turning a theory paper into code, re-check each deviation (here the new GCP definition) against the existing theorems and say so in the paper (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - f is sufficiently smooth (p. 2).
  - Strict complementarity is needed for active-set identification. It is violated on purpose in the DEGEN problems (p. 5, p. 12; appendix p. 24–25).
  - The superlinear rate needs an inexact-Newton residual condition and B^(k) accurate along the steps (Dembo–Eisenstat–Steihaug, Dennis–Schnabel) (p. 6).
  - The skip thresholds 10⁻⁸ and 10⁸ are "arbitrary" in theory but should in practice "reflect problem scalings" (p. 11).
  - The problems are reasonably well scaled, which is why the choice of B⁽⁰⁾ did not matter (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: no new proofs. The paper checks that its algorithmic deviations remain inside the theory of [3] (p. 5, p. 19–20). It offers an explanation for SR1's success as an explicitly unproven conjecture: trust-region methods need an accurate model on a larger subspace than the Newton direction (p. 20).
- **D5 Experiments**: This section is the core of the paper.
  - **Test set** (p. 11; appendix pp. 24–31):
    - 25 problems from 23 families (BVP and VAR at two sizes each), drawn from Moré–Garbow–Hillstrom, Hock–Schittkowski and Toint, generalized or chained to n = 8–45;
    - each problem is run twice. **U** adds −100 ≤ x_i ≤ 100, or tighter bounds to prevent overflow. **C** adds x*_i + 0.1 ≤ x_i ≤ x*_i + 1.1 for odd i, around the U solution, and starts from the projection of the U starting point;
    - "degenerate" variants (DEGENROSE, DEGENSING) add bounds that break strict complementarity (p. 24–25);
    - the text announces "forty six test problems" (p. 10), whereas the tables list 25 × {U, C} = 50 runs per method, and p. 23 speaks of "the 50 tests".
  - **Environment** (p. 11): IBM 3084Q at Harwell; WATFIV-compiled Fortran 77 prototype; CPU timings are meant for relative comparison only.
  - **Settings** (p. 11): B⁽⁰⁾ = I; Δ⁽⁰⁾ = 0.1‖ĝ⁽⁰⁾‖; μ = 0.25, η = 0.75, γ0 = 0.5, γ2 = 2.
  - **Reporting** (p. 11–12):
    - per run: n, it. (= function evaluations), de. (= gradient evaluations), c.g. iterations, final projected-gradient norm, "cas" (the last iteration at which the active set changed), time, and "usk" (skipped updates);
    - stop when ‖ĝ‖ < 10⁻⁶;
    - failure when the iteration count exceeds max(20n, 600) for U or max(10n, 300) for C, or when Δ < 10⁻¹⁶, marked ">".
  - **Summaries**:
    - Table 5.6 gives iteration and time ratios relative to the exact-Hessian version (p. 18);
    - Tables 5.7–5.8 give counts of rank positions 1–5 per method, with failures counted separately (p. 12, p. 19).
  - **Baselines** (p. 12): the NAG line-search, active-set codes E04LAF (modified Newton) and E04KAF (BFGS), run with default tuning to 5 correct decimals and counted in function/gradient pairs (fde.). Harwell VA13AD gave similar results that were not reported.
  - **Findings**:
    - exact Hessians are best, and their use is recommended "whenever they are available" (p. 12);
    - SR1 is the best updating scheme. Its approximations on the free variables converged, or nearly converged, to the true values on almost every problem, and its update was almost never skipped (p. 20);
    - BFGS is "surprisingly disappointing" relative to SR1 in the trust region. Its only failure (HOSC45) comes from insisting on positive definiteness on an indefinite problem (p. 20);
    - DFP is the most unreliable, and PSB converges more slowly than expected (p. 22);
    - against NAG, the exact version needs fewer function evaluations on 43 of the 50 tests and fewer gradient evaluations on 44. SR1 against E04KAF wins on 46 and 47 tests (p. 23).
  - **Failure reporting**: the DEGENSING failure is diagnosed as degeneracy interacting with the stop-on-bound CG rule. It is fixed by allowing CG restarts, which the theory permits (Table 5.10, p. 19–20, p. 22).
  - The cost unit is stated explicitly: function and gradient calls (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper relies on its own theory [3] and on Moré [18].
  - CG truncation follows Steihaug [27] and Toint [29].
  - Brayton–Cullum are credited with the use of SR1 in a line search and "ingenious techniques" to make it stable (p. 20).
  - The received wisdom is challenged directly: updates in good repute for line searches are not necessarily best in a trust region (p. 23).
  - Earlier reports of poor BFGS behaviour (Powell [24]) are cited in support (p. 22).
  - The NAG baseline comes from the rival active-set/line-search school (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The abstract is modest: "The results are encouraging" (p. 2). The introduction announces that both comparisons "yield unexpected results" (p. 3).
  - Surprises are flagged as such ("Somewhat more surprisingly", p. 20). The one explanation offered is labelled a conjecture, with its difficulty stated (p. 20).
  - The paper ends with explicit recommendations (p. 23) and a research programme (p. 23–24).
  - The tables take 10 of the 32 journal pages (pp. 13–22).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and experimental: a concrete GCP-plus-CG implementation of the S008 class, and evidence on which Hessian option works in a trust region (p. 23). The limits are concrete:
  - small dense problems only (p. 3);
  - prototype timings (p. 11);
  - no theory for SR1's advantage (p. 20, p. 23);
  - degeneracy slows convergence (p. 12).

  Future work:
  - large-scale problems with partial separability, low-rank element Hessians and preconditioners (p. 23–24);
  - general nonlinear programming by solving a sequence of bound-constrained augmented-Lagrangian subproblems, with an expected trade-off between subproblem accuracy and overall convergence (p. 24).
