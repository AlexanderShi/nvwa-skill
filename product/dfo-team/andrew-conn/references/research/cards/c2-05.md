### S012 · Global convergence of general derivative-free trust-region algorithms to first-and second-order critical points (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2009, DOI 10.1137/060673424)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K. Scheinberg, L. N. Vicente · **Position in trajectory**: Conn and Scheinberg were at IBM T. J. Watson and Vicente at Coimbra (p. 1). This is the convergence capstone of the CSV geometry programme. For the construction of fully-linear and fully-quadratic models it points to the two 2008 geometry papers [4, 5] and the 2009 book [6] (p. 2, p. 29). It revisits the authors' own CST 1997 [3] and the CGT book *Trust-Region Methods* [2]. The other three papers of this batch cite it as the theorem they rely on (S025 ref [5]; S066 ref [9]; S075 ref [18]). **Read**: full text (p. 1–29), including every proof, the acknowledgement and the reference list. There are no appendices.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap in the literature, which the paper sorts into two categories (p. 2). One category holds trust-region DFO methods aimed at practical performance (Marazzi–Nocedal [7], Powell 2003 [12]) that had no convergence theory. The other holds methods with proven global convergence that were impractical, and the examples given are the authors' own CGT book [2] and CST 1997 [3] (p. 2). The paper sets out to give a framework in the spirit of the first category with the guarantees of the second (p. 2). The abstract names three new issues (p. 1):
  - global convergence when iterates are accepted on simple decrease;
  - how the trust-region radius is maintained at the criticality step;
  - global convergence to second-order critical points.

  The problem is unconstrained min f(x), with f once or twice continuously differentiable and bounded below, and derivatives unavailable (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key insight is that the convergence proof needs only two things. The first is a fraction of Cauchy decrease (Assumption 2.1), plus a fraction of eigenstep decrease for second order (Assumption 2.2) (p. 5–6). The second is that the models come from a *fully-linear* (or *fully-quadratic*) class. A class qualifies if it meets Taylor-like error bounds with uniform constants and if a *model-improvement algorithm* exists that, in a finite and uniformly bounded number of steps, either certifies a model or produces a certified one (Def. 3.1, p. 7–8; Def. 3.3, p. 9). The models need not be polynomials (p. 2). Changes relative to [2] and [3] (p. 2–3, Algorithm 4.1 on p. 13–14):
  1. One trust region both limits the step and defines the sampling region. Powell's [12] two regions are rejected as more complicated (p. 2).
  2. The criticality step (Algorithm 4.2, p. 14) runs when ‖g_k^icb‖ ≤ ε_c. The model is improved on balls of radius α^{i−1}Δ_k^icb until Δ̃_k ≤ μ‖g̃_k‖, and then Δ_k = min{max{Δ̃_k, β‖g̃_k‖}, Δ_k^icb} is set (p. 13–14). This forces Δ_k → 0, which gives a natural stopping criterion (p. 2).
  3. Acceptance on simple decrease: a trial point is accepted if ρ_k ≥ η1, or if ρ_k ≥ η0 and the model is fully linear, with 0 ≤ η0 ≤ η1 < 1 (Step 3, p. 13–14).
  4. Models need to be certified only within a finite, uniformly bounded number of iterations. Iterations with uncertified models can still move the iterate (p. 3). The model-improvement step (Step 4) runs only when ρ_k < η1 (p. 14).
  5. In the first-order algorithm the radius update after a success is free, as long as the radius does not decrease (Step 5, p. 14). In the second-order algorithm the radius is forced to increase only when Δ_k < βσ_k^m, not on every success as in the derivative-based theory (Algorithm 6.1, p. 22; p. 3, p. 21).

  Kept from the classical theory: the ratio test, Cauchy decrease and the proof skeleton of [2] (p. 5, p. 16–21). Iterations are classified as successful, acceptable, model-improving or unsuccessful (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The assumptions:
  - Assumption 3.1: ∇f is Lipschitz on the enlarged level set L_enl(x0), the union of balls B(x; Δmax) over x in L(x0). The enlargement is needed because sample points may lie outside L(x0) (p. 7).
  - Assumption 3.2: ∇²f is Lipschitz on the same set, for second order (p. 9).
  - Assumption 5.1: f is bounded below (p. 16).
  - Assumption 5.2: the model Hessians are uniformly bounded, imposed for simplicity of presentation (p. 16).

  The constants in Lemmas 3.2 and 3.4 are enlarged without loss of generality so that full linearity (or the fully-quadratic property) carries over to larger balls (p. 8–9). The authors state what the framework does not cover:
  - there is no local-rate analysis, so models built from n+1, 2n+1 or (n+1)(n+2)/2−1 points are treated alike (p. 3);
  - fully-quadratic models need on the order of n² points, so for large n only the first-order theory applies, with underdetermined models (p. 3);
  - requiring x_{k+1} = x_k at model-improving iterations is needed only for the lim-type result (Remark 5.1, p. 21).

  The definition knowingly admits impractical classes: M = {f} itself, and finite-difference Taylor models rebuilt from scratch at each x and Δ (p. 8). The paper then shows that practical classes exist: Λ-poised interpolation and regression models (p. 11–13).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: A classical trust-region skeleton adapted to certified models. First order (p. 15–21):
  - **Lemma 5.1** (p. 15–16): the criticality loop ends unless ∇f(x_k) = 0. Otherwise ‖∇f(x_k)‖ ≤ (κ_eg + 1/μ)α^{i−1}Δ_k^icb → 0.
  - **Lemma 5.2** (key, p. 16–17): if the model is fully linear and Δ_k ≤ min{1/κ_bhm, κ_fcd(1−η1)/(4κ_ef)}‖g_k‖, the iteration is successful, because |ρ_k − 1| ≤ 4κ_efΔ_k²/(κ_fcd‖g_k‖Δ_k).
  - **Lemma 5.3** (p. 17): ‖g_k‖ ≥ κ1 implies Δ_k ≥ κ2. The proof uses the floor Δ_k ≥ min{β‖g_k‖, Δ_k^icb} set by the criticality step.
  - **Lemma 5.4** (p. 17–18): with finitely many successes, at most N model-improving iterations occur in a row, so Δ shrinks at least once every N iterations. A three-term triangle inequality then gives lim ∇f = 0.
  - **Lemma 5.5** (p. 18): Δ_k → 0 in all cases. On successes the decrease is at least η1(κ_fcd/2)min{ε_c, Δ_k/μ}·min{…}, which must be summable. Other radii are at most γ_inc times the last successful radius. Powell suggested this lemma (p. 28).
  - **Lemmas 5.6–5.7** (p. 18–19): liminf ‖g_k‖ = 0, and the model gradient transfers to the true gradient, because the criticality step gives ‖∇f(x_k) − g_k‖ ≤ κ_egμ‖g_k‖.
  - **Theorems 5.8–5.9** (p. 19–21): liminf, then lim. The lim proof takes subsequences k_i < ℓ_i and bounds ‖x_{k_i} − x_{ℓ_i}‖ ≤ Σ Δ_j ≤ (2/(η1κ_fcd))(f(x_{k_i}) − f(x_{ℓ_i})) (p. 20).

  Second order (p. 21–28):
  - σ^m = max{‖g‖, −λ_min(H)} replaces ‖g‖ throughout (p. 21).
  - **Proposition 7.1** bounds the eigenvalue perturbation, |λ_min(∇²f) − λ_min(∇²m)| ≤ κ_ehΔ, by a Rayleigh-quotient argument in each direction (p. 23). **Lemma 7.2** gives |σ − σ^m| ≤ κ_σΔ (p. 24).
  - **Lemma 7.4** splits into a gradient case and a curvature case (p. 24–25).
  - **Theorem 7.11** proves the lim-type result using the forced radius increases on S+, which give a geometric series Σ Δ_j ≤ (γ_inc/(γ_inc−1))Δ_{j*} (p. 28).

  **Lemma 3.4** (p. 9–10) shows that bounds holding on a small ball also hold on larger balls, by integrating along the ray from θs to s. There are no complexity bounds; they are not a goal of the paper.
- **D5 Experiments**: none. Practicality is argued in prose, without numbers (p. 11–13):
  - pivot-based Λ-poisedness maintenance [4] needs to replace only one point per iteration;
  - bad-pivot replacements are said to be rarely needed in practice (p. 12);
  - the linear-algebra cost can reach O(n⁶) per iteration, against O(n⁴) for Powell's Lagrange-polynomial updating (p. 12–13);
  - 2n+1-point minimum-Frobenius-norm models can be kept fully linear (p. 13).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues [3]: the criticality step comes from there, and the proof of Lemma 5.1 is said to be very similar to [3, Lemma 5.iii] (p. 15). It also corrects the authors' own book. [2, pp. 321–322] said the second-order DFO analysis follows simply from the derivative-based case. The paper answers that the adaptation is not as trivial as one might expect (p. 3). Credit is given to others:
  - Powell, for an analogue of the criticality step [12] (p. 15);
  - Powell again, for the improved Step 1, for Lemma 3.2 and for Lemma 5.5 (p. 28);
  - Thomas [13] for lim-type results, and Yuan [14] for the cycling example that motivates the radius provision (p. 2).

  Neither camp is criticized; the paper classifies them (p. 2). There are only 14 references (p. 29).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contributions appear first in the abstract and then as a list of design features in the introduction (p. 1–3). A roadmap follows (p. 3), and then a table of κ constants with mnemonic subscripts (p. 4). Hedging is light and attributed to the authors: the priority claim for second-order analysis is limited to what they are aware of (p. 3), and the value of the abstraction is given as their opinion (p. 13). Scope statements are candid (no local rates, p. 3; impractical members of the class, p. 8). A parenthetical note observes that a criticality step in derivative-based methods would also give the lim result, but might spoil fast local rates (p. 2). About two thirds of the text, pages 13–28, is algorithms and proofs. The practicality discussion sits on p. 11–13.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical and algorithmic: first- and second-order lim-type global convergence for a class of methods that covers interpolation and regression models (Theorems 5.9 and 7.11, p. 19–21, p. 27–28). The limits are stated concretely:
  - no local rates (p. 3);
  - the theory cannot distinguish models by their number of sample points (p. 3);
  - fully-quadratic models need O(n²) points (p. 3);
  - O(n⁶) linear algebra can dominate when n is around 100 and evaluations are cheap (p. 12);
  - bounded model Hessians are assumed (p. 16).

  There is no future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ strong evidence; this is the defining paper. The model-improvement algorithm is part of the definition of a model class (Def. 3.1, 3.3, p. 7–9), and the analysis is independent of the sampling technique (abstract, p. 1). This supports steps 3–4 of Method 3.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: a theory-only paper. The code and numbers belong to the geometry papers and the book [4–6]. Implementation costs are discussed (p. 11–13), but no runs are reported. Method 1's triad is spread across several publications here, not paired.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: derivative-based trust-region convergence machinery ([2], Thomas [13], Yuan [14]) is carried into DFO, and the paper insists the carry-over is not trivial (p. 2–3).
- Research Taste mark 2 ✅ (theory covers a class): the framework is described as general enough to cover a wide class of DFO methods (p. 3), and the models need not be polynomials (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: *close the theory–practice gap from the theory side*. Relax the analysed algorithm towards what codes actually do (simple decrease, one radius, uncertified models allowed to move the iterate, one point replaced per iteration), and pay for it with a more complicated proof (p. 2–3, p. 12, p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *delimit an abstraction by naming its trivial members*, then prove that practical members exist (p. 8, p. 11–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Define the model interface as error bounds plus the existence of a finite, uniformly bounded improvement procedure. Then any model builder that meets the interface inherits the theorem (Def. 3.1, p. 7–8).
- [D2] Classify iterations (successful, acceptable, model-improving, unsuccessful) so that iterations with uncertified models can still move the iterate without breaking the proof (p. 15).
- [D4] Couple the radius to the stationarity measure at small gradients (Δ_k ≤ μ‖g_k‖) to turn model-gradient convergence into true-gradient convergence: ‖∇f‖ ≤ (κ_egμ + 1)‖g‖ (Lemma 5.7, p. 18–19).
- [D4] Prove Δ_k → 0 from two facts: the decreases on successful iterations are summable, and the criticality step bounds ‖g_k‖ below by min{ε_c, Δ_k/μ} (Lemma 5.5, p. 18). The same fact gives the stopping test.
- [D4] State a *small radius ⇒ successful* lemma with an explicit threshold and reuse it in every later proof (Lemma 5.2, p. 16–17; Lemma 7.4, p. 24–25).
- [D4] Extend error bounds from a small ball to a larger one by integrating the derivative error along the ray from θs to s (Lemma 3.4, p. 9–10).
- [D4] For a lim-type second-order result, force radius increases only when Δ_k < βσ_k^m, and bound the path length by a geometric series (p. 22, p. 28).
- [D7] Give a table of constants with mnemonic subscripts before the analysis (p. 4).

- "The abstraction highlights, in our opinion, the fundamental requirements for obtaining the appropriate convergence results." (p. 13)

### S025 · A derivative-free algorithm for least-squares minimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2010, DOI 10.1137/09075531x)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: H. Zhang (Louisiana State University), K. Scheinberg (Lehigh University); Conn at IBM T. J. Watson (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The first structured follow-up of CSV 2009, which it cites as [5] (p. 21). It was received on 8 April 2010 and accepted on 15 October 2010, with NSF support (p. 1). The local convergence analysis is in a companion paper, Zhang–Conn [29] (p. 3, p. 22). A bound-constrained successor, DFBOLS, is announced (p. 21). **Read**: full text (p. 1–22), including every proof and the reference list.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min Φ(x) = ½‖F(x)‖², where the residuals f_i are C² but have no available derivatives. They come from simulations, experiments or legacy codes, and the goal is to use as few function evaluations as possible (p. 1). The paper surveys three DFO classes (p. 1–2):
  - direct search, which needs many evaluations;
  - finite differences with quasi-Newton, which is not robust under noise;
  - model-based methods, which Moré–Wild [20] found frequently superior, although the first two are still widely used in engineering.

  The entry is structure. Build one model per residual rather than one for Φ, as CST 1998 [4] did for constraints (p. 2). Because evaluations dominate the cost, extra models cost storage but no extra evaluations (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: One interpolation model m_i per residual, all on a common sample set Y of 2n+1 points and all updated with Powell's minimum-Frobenius-norm technique. Updating the coefficients costs O(mn), building the models costs O(mn²) per iteration, and an occasional change of base point costs O(mn³) (p. 2, p. 16–17). The local model is φ(y, s), with c_φ = ½‖m(y)‖² and g_φ = J(y)ᵀm(y). Its Hessian switches by regime (p. 5):
  - J(y)ᵀJ(y) (Gauss–Newton) if ‖g_φ‖ ≥ κ_H¹;
  - J(y)ᵀJ(y) + κ_H³‖m(y)‖I if ‖g_φ‖ < κ_H¹ and c_φ < κ_H²‖g_φ‖;
  - the full J(y)ᵀJ(y) + Σ m_i∇²m_i otherwise.

  The typical values are 1, 1 and 0.01 (p. 5). The middle term regularizes steps along the null space of the Jacobian, which keeps quadratic convergence on zero-residual problems (p. 5–6). The paper calls the whole model an adaptive version of Levenberg–Marquardt (p. 6). The DFLS algorithm (p. 6–8):
  - The model is built at a base point y_k but centred at x_k, which avoids an O(mn³) shift at every iteration. The base point moves when ‖d_k‖ ≤ 0.1‖x_k − y_k‖ (p. 8–9).
  - Two radii: Δ_k, and a lower bound ρ_k on the sampling radius (p. 6–8).
  - Step 3 is a safety step. When ‖d_k‖ < ρ_k/2 away from criticality, no function evaluation is spent (p. 7–8).
  - Simple decrease (r_k > 0) is accepted (p. 7).
  - The radius update is keyed to ‖d_k‖, with thresholds 0.1 and 0.7 (p. 7).
  - The radius is reduced only when the failure cannot be blamed on geometry (p. 9).
  - After a success the new point replaces the sample point farthest from the base point, with no poisedness check (p. 9).

  One tension: the introduction says a single, adaptively updated radius can serve both purposes (p. 3), yet the algorithm section admits that Δ_k and ρ_k in some sense play the roles of Powell's two regions (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The assumptions:
  - Assumption 2.1: at most |Y| − 1 point substitutions make any set Λ-poised, which the paper calls not unduly restrictive (p. 4);
  - Assumption 3.1: F is C² and the level set is bounded (p. 10);
  - Assumption 3.2: the model Hessians are uniformly bounded (p. 10).

  In the code, Assumption 3.2 is enforced by a safeguard: restart from the minimum-Frobenius-norm model when the accumulated Hessian grows too large (p. 11). Lemma 3.4 shows that it holds automatically for minimum-Frobenius-norm models (p. 11). The constants depend on n, Λ, F and the model-Hessian bound (p. 10). For local analysis the paper points to local error-bound conditions, which are weaker than a nonsingular Jacobian (p. 3); that analysis is not in this paper.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The trust-region analysis is standard, with the model-accuracy step done per residual:
  - **Lemma 3.1** gives fully-linear bounds for Λ-poised interpolation, taken from the book [7, Thm 5.4] (p. 9).
  - **Lemmas 3.2–3.3** give Lipschitz and model bounds on the convex hull of the enlarged level set (p. 10–11).
  - **Lemma 4.1** (Powell's Cauchy decrease, p. 12) and **Lemma 4.2** (the criticality step terminates, proved in [5]; p. 12) are reused without proof.
  - **Lemma 4.3** (key, p. 12–13): |r_k − 1| ≤ ε once Δ_k ≤ c‖g_φk‖. The bound behind it is Φ(s) − φ(y, s−y) ≤ ‖F(y)‖‖∇F(y) − J(y)‖‖s − y‖ + c1‖s − y‖². The per-residual Jacobian error enters multiplied by the residual norm (4.3).
  - **Lemma 4.4** (p. 13–14): the radius stays bounded away from zero while ‖g_φk‖ ≥ δ1. The proof is by contradiction and uses the interlocking updates ρ_{k_i} = ρ_{k_i−1}/10 and Δ_{k_i} = ρ_{k_i−1}/2.
  - **Lemma 4.5** (p. 14–15): through the criticality step, g_φ → 0 implies ∇Φ → 0.
  - **Theorem 4.6** (p. 15–16): liminf ‖∇Φ(x_k)‖ = 0.

  This is only a liminf result, weaker than the lim result of S012. The proofs are described as following the standard technique, modified for this setting (p. 12–13).
- **D5 Experiments**: Setup and results:
  - Baselines (p. 16): LMDIF, Moré's finite-difference Levenberg–Marquardt code, and NEWUOA, Powell's unstructured interpolation code.
  - DFLS was built inside the NEWUOA framework, partly to make the comparison meaningful (p. 16). It uses NP = 2n+1 points.
  - All codes are Fortran (F77) on an IBM ThinkPad T40, with default parameters and an initial radius of 1.0 (p. 17).
  - Test set (p. 17): the Moré–Wild set of 53 problems, drawn from 22 CUTEr nonlinear least-squares problem types.
  - Convergence test (5.1): Φ(x0) − Φ(x_k) ≥ (1−τ)(Φ(x0) − Φ_L) (p. 17).
  - Budget: 50 simplex gradients, i.e. 50(n_p+1) evaluations. Data profiles at τ = 10⁻¹, 10⁻³, 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁷ (p. 17–18).
  - Smooth set: final radius 10⁻⁸, finite-difference step 10⁻⁷ (p. 18).
  - Noisy set (p. 19): deterministic noise (1 + ε_N π(x))Φ(x), where π is a Chebyshev cubic applied to a trigonometric function. It is chosen for reproducibility, with ε_N = 10⁻³. The final radius and finite-difference step are set to max{√(10⁻³Φ*), 10⁻⁷}, i.e. to the noise level.

  Results. At loose tolerances all three codes solve more than 90% of the problems within 50 simplex gradients (p. 18). At τ = 10⁻⁷ the order is DFLS > LMDIF > NEWUOA (p. 18). DFLS reaches τ = 10⁻⁷ on about 88% of the smooth problems within about 22 simplex gradients (p. 19). On the noisy set DFLS is always first and the least affected, and LMDIF overtakes NEWUOA at tight tolerances (p. 19). The authors suggest that interpolation methods filter noise by accumulating information over iterations (p. 20). Not reported: CPU times and per-problem failures. The memory overhead is given analytically as (m−1)(1 + n + n(n+1)/2) (p. 17). Two figure legends contain typos (LIMDIF, DMDIF; p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself among the three DFO classes (p. 1–2). It builds on the CSV framework and poisedness papers [5–8] (p. 3–4, p. 6) and on Powell's NEWUOA and minimum-Frobenius-norm machinery [22–24] (p. 2, p. 6). NEWUOA's details are praised as carefully and intelligently chosen (p. 16). Benchmarking follows Moré–Wild [20] (p. 17). The local-convergence literature is cited for error-bound conditions (Fan–Yuan, Yamashita–Fukushima, Hager–Zhang; p. 3). No competitor is criticized. The baselines are described by what each lacks: NEWUOA does not use structure, and LMDIF uses finite differences instead of interpolation (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract opens with a framework-for-a-class claim (p. 1), followed by a roadmap and a table of constants (p. 3). Rationale paragraphs follow the algorithm and explain each step (p. 8–9). Results are hedged (*seems*, *perhaps not surprising*, p. 18–19), and the conclusion calls the experiments preliminary (p. 20). The conclusion argues from two features: an efficient and reliable method needs both interpolation and structure, and DFLS has both (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an algorithm, a global convergence theorem and numerical evidence (p. 20–21). The limits are specific:
  - only a liminf global result, with local convergence deferred to [29] (p. 3, p. 20);
  - O(mn²) extra storage and arithmetic (p. 17);
  - the ranking of the codes depends on noise and required accuracy, to the point that the algorithm of choice can change (p. 20).

  Future work: bound constraints, already done as DFBOLS (p. 21).

**Method links**:

- Method 3 ✅ evidence: Λ-poisedness, the criticality step and fully-linear bounds come from CSV (p. 4, p. 6, p. 9, p. 12). ⚠ variant in practice: successful iterations skip the poisedness check to save linear algebra (p. 9). S012 allows this.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: theorem, code and data profiles in one paper, with a companion local-convergence paper [29] and a follow-on code, DFBOLS (p. 3, p. 21).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence, updated: Moré–Wild data profiles, deterministic noise and a budget counted in simplex gradients (p. 17–19). This is the modern replacement that the SKILL.md limitation of Method 2 anticipates. ⚠ Only one tier, with no engineering instance.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the derivative-based Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt model is transplanted into DFO and combined with Powell's NEWUOA machinery (p. 5–6, p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *baselines as ablations*. Pick each baseline so that it lacks exactly one of your method's two ingredients (NEWUOA lacks structure, LMDIF lacks interpolation), so that the comparison is an ablation (p. 16, p. 21).
- New-pattern candidate: *implement inside the strongest baseline's code base*, so that the difference isolates your change (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *plug-in convergence*. Cite the framework's lemmas for criticality and Cauchy decrease, and prove only the lemma that is new to the structure (p. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] For a composite objective, interpolate every component on one shared sample set. The extra models cost memory and arithmetic but no extra evaluations (p. 2, p. 16–17).
- [D2] Switch the Hessian model by regime, with explicit thresholds: Gauss–Newton when the gradient is large, a regularization proportional to the residual when both gradient and residual are relatively small, and the full second-order model otherwise (p. 5–6).
- [D2] Do not shrink the radius after a failure that may come from poor geometry, and skip the function evaluation for tiny steps (the safety step) (p. 7–9).
- [D4] In the ratio lemma, separate the model error into residual norm × Jacobian error plus O(‖s‖²), so that structure enters the constants (p. 12–13).
- [D5] Moré–Wild protocol: set the budget in simplex gradients, use data profiles at four tolerances, add deterministic noise for reproducibility, and set each solver's final radius or finite-difference step to the noise level (p. 17–19).
- [D5] Report the memory and arithmetic overhead of exploiting structure as a formula next to the results (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "This is the essential cost of exploiting the structure." (p. 17)
- "Therefore, for a method to be both efficient and reliable for least-squares minimization, the polynomial interpolation-based methods which also exploit the problem structure are recommended." (p. 21)

### S066 · Bilevel derivative-free optimization and its application to robust optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2012, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2010.547579)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: L. N. Vicente (Coimbra); Conn at IBM T. J. Watson (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: A two-author follow-up that carries the CSV machinery into nested problems. The practical algorithm follows Fasano–Morales–Nocedal [12] and Bandeira–Scheinberg–Vicente [1] (p. 10). The file is a preprint dated 15 September 2010 (p. 1). **Read**: full text (p. 1–20) and the figure captions (p. 21–24). The plotted curves in Figures 1–4 are not in the text file and were not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is bilevel programming in which neither the upper-level nor the lower-level objective has derivatives (abstract, p. 1; problem (1), p. 2). The motivating application is robust optimization of simulation-based functions with implementation errors or uncertain parameters: the robust counterpart min_x max_{p∈P} f(x, p) is a bilevel problem (p. 1, p. 16–17). The paper works with the *reduced* formulation min_{x^u} f^u(x^u, x^ℓ(x^u)), assuming the lower-level solution is unique. It rejects the *all-at-once* formulation (3), because without lower-level convexity (3) only relaxes (1) (p. 2–3). It calls the reduced form a more direct starting point for a derivative-free, possibly nonconvex lower level (p. 3). General constraints are left out on purpose (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Treat the reduced upper-level function as an inexactly evaluated black box whose inexactness is controlled by the lower-level solve. Then set the lower-level effort from the accuracy the upper-level model needs (§4, p. 6–9). Two evaluation-saving devices:
  1. **Inexact lower-level solves** (p. 8–9). The lower-level gradient tolerance is tied to the upper radius and step: ‖∇_ℓm^ℓ‖ = O((Δ^u)²) with Δ^ℓ = O(Δ^u), or O(min(‖s^u‖², ‖s^u‖‖g^u‖)) for dynamic accuracy. In the code this is (11): ε_g^ℓ = max(min(10⁻²(Δ^u)², 10⁻²Δ^u‖g^u‖, 10⁻²), 10⁻⁵) (p. 14).
  2. **Reuse of lower-level evaluations** made at perturbed upper-level values, if ‖x^u − x^u_pert‖ = O((Δ^ℓ)³) (p. 9). In the code this is (10), a distance of at most min(10⁻²(Δ_0^ℓ)³, 10⁻²) (p. 14).

  Both levels use the same practical DFO algorithm (Algorithm 5.1, p. 10–12):
  - minimum-Frobenius-norm quadratic interpolation while |Y| < (n+1)(n+2)/2;
  - no explicit model-improving iterations, and no distinction between successful and acceptable iterations;
  - on success, the point farthest from the new iterate is discarded;
  - a weak criticality condition: when Δ < 10⁻³, discard points outside B(x; 100Δ);
  - the radius is never reduced while |Y| < n+1.

  Lower-level starting points come from a linear surrogate x^ℓ_m(x^u), obtained from a joint minimum-Frobenius-norm quadratic model of f^ℓ in both sets of variables (p. 12–13). Linear constraints are handled by switching to an ℓ∞ trust region, so that the subproblem is a QP. Stationarity is measured by a projected gradient, computed with an ℓ1 projection by LP when the constraints are general (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The lower-level minimizer is assumed unique (p. 2), and the reduced upper-level function C¹ for the convergence claims (p. 3). The lower-level models must be fully quadratic, so that the upper-level models can be fully linear (p. 8). The inverse-function-theorem bound needs ∇²_ℓℓ f^ℓ nonsingular near the solution (p. 8). Lipschitz constants are assumed in the upper and lower variables (p. 8–9). Inexact values follow a controllable-error model: for a requested ε_x one can compute f̄ with |f − f̄| ≤ ε_x (p. 5). The theory in §4 is unconstrained. The code handles linear constraints, but not upper-level constraints on the lower-level variables, which would become nonlinear in the reduced form (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: No new convergence theorem. The paper states that no analysis needs to be added to the existing DFO trust-region theory (p. 3). It derives sufficient conditions instead:
  - **Controlled inexactness** (from the CGT book, §10.6): max{ε_x, ε_{x+s}} ≤ η0′(m(x) − m(x+s)) with 0 < η0′ < η0/2 keeps the true ratio at least η0 − 2η0′ (p. 5–6).
  - **Theorems 4.1 and 4.2**: interpolation with value errors of order Δ³ (or Δ²) is still fully quadratic (or fully linear). They are adapted from [8, Thm 4.2] and [10, Thm 5.4] (p. 6–7).
  - **Error propagation** (p. 8): the inverse function theorem gives ‖x^ℓ(x^u) − x^ℓ_dfo‖ ≤ c_∇^ℓ‖∇_ℓf^ℓ‖ ≤ c_∇^ℓ‖∇_ℓm^ℓ‖ + c_∇^ℓc_g^ℓ(Δ^ℓ)², and a Lipschitz step carries it to the upper level (8). Together these give the O((Δ^u)²) value error that fully-linear upper-level models tolerate.
  - **Reuse criterion**: Lipschitz continuity in x^u, with an O((Δ^ℓ)³) displacement (9) (p. 9).

  Algorithm 5.1 itself, with no model-improvement steps, is not analysed (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: A Matlab implementation (p. 14), with constants listed (p. 10–11). The tests:
  1. Two synthetic bilevel examples with n^u = n^ℓ = 5 (p. 16). One has a quartic lower level, ‖H x^ℓ − x^u‖⁴ with H = QDQᵀ. The other is taken from Case's thesis [5] and has bounds and lower-level constraints. The second example is also run with n^u = n^ℓ = 20.
  2. A 2-D robust example from Bertsimas–Nohadani–Teo [3], with ‖p‖ ≤ 0.5 and starting points A and B (p. 17).
  3. Robust maximization of the Omega function for 8 assets: 7 upper-level variables, with the threshold L ∈ [0, 0.04] as the lower-level variable (p. 17–18).

  Three variants are plotted: basic, inexact lower level, and inexact plus reuse. The measure is the reduced upper-level value against lower-level evaluations, and for some figures also against upper-level evaluations (captions, p. 21–24). The text reports that inexactness gives a significant saving with the quartic lower level but not with the quadratic one (p. 16). On the robust example both starts reach a local minimizer, and in most other trials the run converges to the closest one (p. 17). There are no baselines, and the paper says so (p. 19). Figure values: not read (not in the text file).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper cites the bilevel books of Bard, Dempe and Shimizu–Ishizuka–Bard (p. 2). It recounts the DFO trust-region lineage, crediting Winfield and Powell, then CST's criticality step [7], CSV's fully-linear models [9] and Powell's minimum-Frobenius-norm models [16] (p. 4–5). The practical algorithm is credited to [1] and [12], with the differences stated (p. 10). The techniques are compared to truncated and inexact Newton and SQP methods (p. 14, p. 18). The application literature is Bertsimas et al. [3] and the Omega-function papers [4, 13, 14]. The paper calls itself a first contribution (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The claims are modest: the conditions follow the convergence theory only *as far as possible* (abstract, p. 1), and the paper calls itself *a first contribution* (p. 18). Choices are explained in the first person: the reduced formulation was felt to be the more direct starting point (p. 3). The costs are admitted: the total number of lower-level evaluations is still high (p. 19). The missing baselines are disclosed with dry humour (p. 19). Derivations take about three pages (p. 5–9); the rest is algorithm description and illustration.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and applied: a bilevel DFO framework, two evaluation-saving techniques backed by error-bound arguments, an extension to linear constraints, and robust DFO as an application (p. 1, p. 18–19). The limits are concrete:
  - no general constraints (p. 3);
  - upper-level constraints on the lower-level variables are excluded (p. 14);
  - point reuse is less robust than inexact solves, and is recommended only when lower-level evaluations are relatively expensive (p. 19);
  - the total lower-level effort is still high (p. 19);
  - the main difficulty is the nonconvexity of the reduced upper-level function, not the concave lower level created by the robust reformulation (p. 19);
  - the Omega model is a simplification (p. 18).

  Future work: a separate study of Omega-function portfolio optimization without derivatives (p. 18).

**Method links**:

- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the geometry error bounds are used as a *design calculus* for tolerances (Theorems 4.1–4.2, p. 6–9). **Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Recast a robust min–max problem as a bilevel problem with lower level min_p −f(x, p), so that bilevel machinery applies (p. 16–17).
- [D4] Propagate the outer accuracy requirement inward. A fully-linear outer model tolerates O(Δ²) value error. The inverse function theorem and a Lipschitz step then turn that into an inner gradient tolerance O((Δ^u)²) with Δ^ℓ = O(Δ^u) (p. 8).
- [D4] Accept inexact function values if the errors at both points are at most η0′ times the predicted decrease, with η0′ < η0/2. The true ratio then stays at least η0 − 2η0′ (p. 5–6).
- [D2] Reuse evaluations made at a perturbed parameter if the perturbation is O(Δ³) of the inner radius, which keeps the inner model fully quadratic (p. 9, p. 14).
- [D2] Warm-start every inner solve from a linear surrogate of the inner solution map, built from one joint quadratic model (p. 12–13).
- [D2] With linear constraints, switch to an ℓ∞ trust region so the subproblem is a QP, and measure stationarity by a projection computed by LP (p. 15).
- [D5] Measure effort in inner (lower-level) evaluations, and plot the basic, +inexact and +inexact+reuse variants as a cumulative ablation (p. 16, captions p. 21–24).

### S075 · A progressive barrier derivative-free trust-region algorithm for constrained optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10589-018-0020-4)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: C. Audet, S. Le Digabel, M. Peyrega (GERAD and Polytechnique Montréal); Conn at IBM Business Analytics and Mathematical Sciences (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The second Conn hybrid with the MADS group. The earlier one, Conn & Le Digabel 2013 [17], put quadratic models into MADS and is what NOMAD uses by default (p. 19). This paper goes in the opposite direction and brings MADS's progressive barrier into a trust region. The file is a preprint dated 28 June 2016 (p. 1). **Read**: full text (p. 1–27), including the reference list. Figure axes and legends were read. The plotted curves are not in the text file; the text's own description of them was used (p. 21–23).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem is min f(x) subject to c(x) ≤ 0 and l ≤ x ≤ u, where f and c come from a blackbox and may return +∞ (p. 1–2). Constraints are quantifiable, relaxable, simulation-based and known (QRSK) in the Le Digabel–Wild taxonomy, so infeasible points may be evaluated and there are no hidden constraints (p. 2). The entry is that nonlinear constraints remain a challenge in DFO (p. 2). Most methods wrap an unconstrained solver in a penalty or Lagrangian scheme. Direct treatments are few (COBYLA, trust funnel, NOWPAC, inexact restoration, and others; p. 2–3). The progressive barrier (PB) existed only inside MADS (p. 3). The goal is to adapt PB to derivative-free trust regions (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The base is an unconstrained DFTR algorithm (Algorithm 1, p. 6) that folds the criticality step into the radius update. After a success the radius shrinks if Δ_k > μ‖g_k‖ and grows otherwise (p. 6–7). This replaces both Step 5 and the criticality step of the CSV book algorithm (p. 7), using the criticality step of Bandeira–Scheinberg–Vicente [13] (p. 7). The model is certified well-poised at every iteration (p. 8). PBTR (Algorithm 2, p. 12) adds the following:
  - Two incumbents: the best feasible point x_F, and x_I, the infeasible point with the lowest f among those with h ≤ h_max (p. 8). One is primary and one secondary, decided by a trigger ρ = 0.1 that favours the feasible one (p. 9).
  - The primary sample set has (n+1)(n+2)/2 points with geometry improvement. The secondary set needs at least 2 points, and linear models are built from it (p. 12).
  - Models of f and of every c_i. Two constrained trust-region subproblems (model constraints ≤ 0, plus bounds) with separate radii Δ_F and Δ_I (p. 11–12). The subproblems are solved by Ipopt (p. 19).
  - Radius rules (4)–(7) use the ratios for both f and h when the trial point is infeasible, and only the ratio for f when it is feasible. The flags P_I and P_F require ‖∇model‖ ≥ μΔ before any increase (p. 13–14).
  - The threshold h_max is updated by the MADS classification of iterations as dominating, improving or unsuccessful (p. 9–10), with two retunings. The PB works on the filtered set of non-dominated points (p. 13). After an improving iteration, a revised rule sets h_max = 0.9·h_max^original + 0.1·h(x*) (p. 20). Both retunings are justified by one fact: a trust region spends more evaluations per iteration than MADS, so the original rule lowers the barrier too slowly (p. 13, p. 20).
  - A speculative line search from MADS, along x + 2^j s, after a dominating iteration (p. 10, p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and c are C², and f is bounded below. Assumption 1 asks for a fraction of Cauchy decrease: for f in the feasible subproblem and for h in the infeasible one. It also asks for uniformly bounded model Hessians (p. 14–15). The problem statement allows f and c to take the value +∞ (p. 2), but the analysis assumes smoothness. Three formulas in this preprint appear garbled as printed: the denominator of ρ̄_h uses x̂ (p. 13); Assumption 1 is written with h̃(x_I) − h̃(x̄) (p. 15); the proof of Theorem 3.2 speaks of a Cauchy step *for f* where the inequality is about h (p. 16). The journal version was not checked.
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The theory is minimal. Two theorems show that at least one radius sequence goes to zero:
  - **Theorem 3.1**: if a feasible point is ever found, Δ_F → 0 (p. 15).
  - **Theorem 3.2**: if none is ever found, Δ_I → 0 (p. 16).

  Both proofs use the same device. If a radius sequence does not tend to zero, there are infinitely many iterations where it is not decreased. At each of them the ratio is at least η0 and the flag gives ‖g‖ ≥ μΔ > με/γ_inc. The decrease is then at least C = η0(κ_Cauchy/2)·min{μ/κ_H, 1}·μ·ε²/γ_inc² > 0, which contradicts the lower bound on f (or h ≥ 0) (p. 15–16). The paper states the gap plainly: if both feasible and infeasible points are generated, nothing is known about Δ_I (p. 16). There is no stationarity or KKT result, and the fully-linear property is never used in these proofs. For the unconstrained base, the paper says the general analysis of [18] carries over (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**: Setup (p. 16–19):
  - A Python implementation (p. 16).
  - Tier 1: 40 small CUTEst problems (n = 2–9, m = 1–13). Table 1 lists the bounds and whether each starting point is feasible (p. 17–18). Unscaled problems are excluded because the code has no dynamic scaling (p. 17).
  - Budget: 100(n+1) evaluations (p. 17).
  - Data profiles at τ = 10⁻³, adapted to constraints by counting only feasible solutions, with h ≤ 10⁻¹⁴ counted as feasible (p. 17). Performance profiles are also given (p. 17–19).
  - Tier 2: two multidisciplinary design problems. AIRCRAFT RANGE has 10 variables, 10 relaxable constraints and 3 coupled disciplines. SIMPLIFIED WING has 7 variables and 3 constraints. Both start at the centre of the bounds (p. 17).
  - Baselines: NOMAD 3.7.2 and COBYLA (NLopt), with default settings (p. 19).

  Internal study first: six PBTR variants (quad_quad, quad_lin or lin_lin models × original or revised h_max rule) are compared, and quad_quad_revised is best (p. 19–21). Model-building time is not counted (p. 21). Results against the baselines:
  - On the smooth set PBTR is comparable to COBYLA and slightly better beyond 40(n+1) evaluations. Both model-based codes beat NOMAD (p. 22).
  - On AIRCRAFT RANGE, COBYLA stalls near −1600 while NOMAD and PBTR reach about −4000, and NOMAD improves faster than PBTR (p. 22–23).
  - On SIMPLIFIED WING the NOMAD and PBTR curves overlap (p. 23).

  The number of runs per MDO problem is not stated.
- **D6 Positioning**: The related-work survey covers both schools and describes each method neutrally (p. 2–3). Model-based: COBYLA, Conejo et al., Xue–Sun, Sampaio–Toint's trust funnel, Tröltzsch's SQP, NOWPAC (noted as needing a feasible starting point), and inexact restoration. Direct search: extreme barrier, filters, augmented-Lagrangian generating set search, PB and the progressive-to-extreme barrier. The claim of novelty is that this is the first algorithm after MADS to use the progressive barrier (p. 23). Conn's own earlier work is cited as building blocks: the 1991 augmented Lagrangian [16], Conn & Le Digabel 2013 [17], CSV 2009 [18] and the CSV book [19] (p. 25).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The paper states an explicit rule for choosing between the schools (p. 2) and repeats it when explaining the results (p. 22). Claims use the word *competitive* (abstract, p. 1; p. 3; p. 23). The discussion adds *preferable to* for one tier each (p. 23). Figure 2 illustrates the three iteration types (p. 10). A roadmap is given (p. 3). Theory takes about 2 pages (p. 14–16) and numerics about 7 (p. 16–23).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an algorithmic hybrid with numerical evidence; the theory is thin (p. 23–24). Limits stated:
  - nothing is known about Δ_I when both feasible and infeasible points appear (p. 16);
  - model-construction time is ignored (p. 21);
  - no dynamic scaling, so unscaled problems were excluded (p. 17).

  Future work (p. 23–24): dynamic scaling; other sample-set management, including overdetermined (regression) models; measuring how often geometry improvement is needed; penalty functions for the subproblems; adapting PB to other nonlinear algorithms.
