### B001 · Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization — table of contents (Conn, Scheinberg, Vicente; SIAM MPS-SIAM Series on Optimization 8, 2009)
**Role**: core · **Read**: full (every line of the three contents pages; the body of the book is not available) · **Coauthors**: K. Scheinberg, L. N. Vicente · **Voice**: the authors' own organisation of the field. It is stated methodology at the level of emphasis and order. · **Position in trajectory**: the book that closes the 1996–2010 model-based DFO line (CST 1997, CSV 2008 ×2, CSV 2009; SKILL.md Research Trajectory). Its geometry chapters share topics with S016 and S028, and its trust-region chapter with S012; the correspondence is by title only (see D6). It refines the S002 card (`a2-02`), which had only the chapter list from the publisher's description.

- **D1 Problem & entry** (Chapter 1, title only, p. 1):
  - The introduction has five sections: 1.1 Why derivative-free optimization; 1.2 Examples of problems where derivatives are unavailable; 1.3 Limitations of derivative-free optimization; 1.4 How derivative-free algorithms should work; 1.5 A short summary of the book (p. 1).
  - So the book enters through motivation, then examples, then limits, then a normative design section ("should work"), then a map. The limitations get their own section, placed before the design section.
  - Other items show what §1.4 (book pp. 7–11) contains:
    - The errata place a perturbed-quadratic example with Figure 1.1 on book p. 9, and a Nelder–Mead run on book p. 10 that "is trapped at a spurious minimizer" (B002 p. 1, items 1–2).
    - Nazareth reports illustrations on Rosenbrock's function and a perturbed quadratic (B005 p. 1).
    - Orban reports that the introduction holds the book's only comparison between methods (B006 p. 1).
    - Inference from these three items: the book's introduction shows a classical method failing.
  - Transferable technique: open a monograph or thesis with an explicit "limitations of the approach" section, placed before the section on how the algorithms should work (p. 1).

- **D3 Assumptions (item 3)**:
  - The corrected Theorem 2.13 is stated under Assumption 2.2, which the errata do not restate, for all y in B(y0; Δ) (p. 2).
  - The note to item 3 says when the printed statement would still be valid: for a model of the form m(y) = f(y0) + (y − y0)ᵀg, that is, one that interpolates f at y0. For such a model, g is the regression simplex gradient of book p. 33 (p. 1).
  - So the error was a mismatch between the theorem and the model form it was applied to.
- **D4 Proof device (item 3)** (pp. 1–2):
  1. Assume y0 = 0 without loss of generality, by the argument at the end of the proof of Theorem 3.16.
  2. Write the Taylor data as a residual equation: M (f(y0), ∇f(y0)) − f(Y) = r, with |ri| ≤ (ν/2)Δ².
  3. The least-squares coefficients are M†f(Y), so the coefficient error equals M†r. A footnote gives M† = (MᵀM)⁻¹Mᵀ when M has full column rank.
  4. Factor M† = diag(1, (1/Δ)I) M̂† (equation (2.9)). This makes the power of Δ explicit and leaves only the norm of the scaled matrix, ‖M̂†‖.
  5. Result (p. 2):
     - gradient error ≤ κeg Δ, with κeg = ν(1 + p^{1/2}‖M̂†‖/2);
     - value error ≤ κef Δ², with κef = 2κeg + ν/2.
  6. Exercise 10 now asks the reader to show (2.9) first (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when a theorem about a least-squares model borrows its proof from the interpolation case, check whether the model has a free constant term. If it does:
    - put the constant among the unknowns;
    - bound the coefficient error by M†r;
    - factor out the scaling.

### B004 · Derivation of Error Bounds for Quadratic Regression (01/27/2011) — addendum to Introduction to Derivative-Free Optimization
**Role**: core · **Read**: full (pp. 1–2) · **Coauthors**: K. Scheinberg, L. N. Vicente · **Voice**: the authors' · **Position in trajectory**:
- Dated 01/27/2011: two years after the book, and four years before the date on the errata lists (B002, B003).
- It supplies the derivation of the book's Theorem 4.13 (p. 1).
- Earlier, the CSV paper S028 had stated its regression error bounds without proof, as "straightforward adaptations" of the proofs in S016 (S028 p. 14, per the `c2-04` card). Whether Theorem 4.13 is the same statement cannot be checked without the body of the book.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The book states, as Theorem 4.13, error bounds for quadratic regression models that are "based on the condition number of a scaled version of the regression matrix". The addendum says they "can be derived as follows" (p. 1).
  - Why the book omitted the derivation, or why it was published separately, is **not stated**.
  - Two facts bear on it:
    - B002's item 11 replaces the three constants on book p. 69. That page lies in §4.4 (book pp. 68–70 in B001), where Theorem 4.13's constants appear to sit.
    - The constants derived here have the same form as the corrected ones. They are written with ‖M̂†‖ where the errata write ‖Σ̂⁻¹‖ (p. 2; B002 p. 3). The two are equal when M̂ has full column rank and Σ̂ holds its singular values, as in S028's reduced-SVD notation (S028 p. 11).
  - Inference from these facts: the addendum is the derivation behind the corrected constants.
- **D2 Key idea**: the device of the corrected linear Theorem 2.13 (B002 item 3), lifted to quadratic models.
  - The model's coefficients (c, g, vec(H)) are compared with the Taylor coefficients (f(y0), ∇f(y0), vec(∇²f(y0))).
  - The coefficient error equals M†r (0.1).
  - Scaling isolates the powers of Δ (0.2) (p. 1).
- **D3 Assumptions & model** (p. 1):
  - The Hessian of f is Lipschitz continuous with constant ν2 > 0.
  - The model is m(y) = c + gᵀy + ½yᵀHy, in the canonical basis φ̄ of the book's (3.1).
  - The regression matrix M = M(φ̄, Y) has full column rank, so M† = (MᵀM)⁻¹Mᵀ (footnote 1).
  - y0 = 0 without loss of generality, by the argument at the end of the proof of Theorem 3.16.
  - Ŷ ⊂ B(0; 1) is the scaled sample set, and p̄ = n(n + 1)/2.
  - Notation as extracted:
    - The residual bound is written |ri| ≤ (ν/2)Δ³, with ν rather than ν2 (p. 1).
    - After (0.2) the bounds are written with ‖M†‖, although the scaling in (0.2) implies the scaled M̂† (pp. 1–2).
    - Whether a hat was lost in extraction or is missing in the original cannot be decided from the text.
- **D4 Proof devices** (pp. 1–2):
  1. Shift to y0 = 0 by reusing an argument already proved (Theorem 3.16).
  2. Write a Taylor residual equation: M (f(y0), ∇f(y0), vec(∇²f(y0))) − f(Y) = r, with each |ri| bounded by a multiple of Δ³.

- **D1 How the reviewer frames the problem** (p. 1):

  - They are hard because derivatives cannot be supplied and evaluations are expensive or noisy, which makes finite differences prohibitive.
  - History as he tells it: Nelder–Mead was the "standard workhorse". In the mid-1990s, theoretical work on its convergence "or lack thereof", together with the needs of applications, spurred renewed research on DFO.

  - The introduction gives examples and shows the typical behaviour of key algorithms on Rosenbrock's function and a perturbed quadratic.
  - Parts I and II are each about 100 pages; Part III is brief.
  - Part I is "nicely organized and well presented". It is of interest in its own right, and applies interpolation techniques that students meet in a first course on numerical analysis. Its three regime chapters (determined, overdetermined, underdetermined) lead to a chapter on modifying a sample set to ensure well-poisedness.
  - The first direct-search chapter considers global convergence "for both continuously differentiable and nonsmooth cases" (p. 2).
  - Part II has two direct-search chapters and a brief implicit-filtering chapter "sandwiched in between" them and the trust-region chapters.
