**What the critique shows** [inferred]. (a) Around 1997–2004, second-derivative interior-point codes driven by AMPL beat an augmented-Lagrangian/bound-constrained trust-region code on the large, sparse, constrained problems that the rival groups themselves chose (COPS, their own AMPL models). (b) The critics' set-ups were not neutral to LANCELOT. It was run through AMPL, not SIF; its memory estimates could not be tuned; rival authors tuned their own codes; defaults were used everywhere. Toint's own 1997 methodology names exactly these risks ("A fair comparison between two pieces of software such as LANCELOT and MINOS seems extremely difficult"). (c) The same limitation appears in both camps: augmented Lagrangian is weak on linear programs and linear constraints (MINOS 1997; the "major defect" for linear constraints noted in the Conn cross-reference of note 01 §2 A).

## 8. Blind spots and limits of applicability (distilled; for the SKILL's "honest boundary" section)

Each item names the evidence it rests on. All are [inferred] unless tagged otherwise.

1. **Augmented Lagrangian + bound-constrained trust region at scale** loses to second-derivative interior-point and SQP codes on large constrained AMPL benchmarks of the late 1990s and early 2000s (§1.1). It is weak on LPs and linear constraints [stated by Toint's own group, 1997]. Its multiplier safeguard needs strong regularity and can slow dual convergence (§1.2, critic's claim). *Advice built on the LANCELOT design should not be carried over to LP-heavy or linear-constraint-heavy solvers without checking.*
2. **Test-set representativeness.** Evidence drawn from CUTE(st) performance profiles supports "tuning, comparison and standardization", in Toint's own words, not claims about industrial usefulness (Fletcher vs Toint, *Optima* 99). The standard precautions of COPS and Argonne (analyzers of solution quality, larger n, difficult problems) came from a rival school.
3. **Worst-case examples are one-dimensional and contrived**, by the authors' own admission (ICM 2018 p. 28; 2024 note p. 1). They do not capture dimension dependence. Constrained bounds exclude the cost of solving subproblems (ICM 2018 p. 28). *A complexity-optimal label says little about CPU time on a real solver* (Curtis 2021; Curtis & Robinson 2018).
4. **ARC-type regularization never switches off**, so it never takes pure Newton steps near a strict minimizer (TRACE p. 11). A solver designer who wants Newton steps locally should look at TRACE-style acceptance rules or trust-region variants.
5. **Evidence base for new theory-driven methods is small-scale and in Matlab** (zbMATH on ARC Part I; note 03 on deferred CPU comparisons; single-workstation experiments per note 01 §4). The critics' large-scale, CPU-time benchmarks are a kind of evidence the Toint record rarely supplies after 2002.
6. **Filter acceptance** needs SOC steps for fast local convergence. Inside an interior-point method, a pure multi-point filter may need a merit-function fallback (§3). The SOC-vs-Lagrangian-filter choice was made by stated preference, not by a published comparison.

## Contradictions (kept, not reconciled)

1. **LANCELOT's standing, 1997 vs 2001–02.** Toint's group (1997): LANCELOT "is a clear winner if the evaluations of the problem functions are expensive", and the two packages "complement each other". Benson–Shanno–Vanderbei (2001/02): LANCELOT is "not competitive with the above codes for the larger problems tested". The competitors differ (MINOS in 1997; LOQO, KNITRO and SNOPT in 2001), and so do the interfaces (SIF vs AMPL). Both statements are in the record.
2. **Benson–Shanno–Vanderbei's verdict vs Dolan–Moré's text.** BSV attribute the "not competitive" finding to Dolan–Moré [6]. The Dolan–Moré text I read never states it; it only omits LANCELOT from the list of solvers that are best on some interval. The verdict rests on figures.
3. **Filters: supported and qualified by the same independent study.** Benson–Shanno–Vanderbei find filter-based variants "superior in terms of efficiency" to a merit function alone. They also find the Fletcher–Leyffer multi-point filter can fail to accept any point and needs a merit fallback, and that a one-point "Markov" filter works best.
4. **Filter future.** The 2006 Brief History predicts filters "will continue to grow". Toint's own filter papers stop in 2007, followed by a method "without a penalty function or a filter". IPOPT's filter line search is widely used. The field-level outcome is not settled here.
5. **Worst-case relevance, same people on both sides.** Toint calls the bounds "typically very pessimistic" (2011, note 02) yet defends the sharp examples as a set of nonzero measure (2024). Curtis says "“Better complexity” has yet to mean “better performance”" (2021) yet built TRACE to match ARC's bound (2017) and thanked CGT for the inspiration.
6. **"Optimal" means different things.** CGT (ICM 2018): ARC is "optimal … within a wider class of second-order methods" and "Newton's method is suboptimal". Carmon et al.: CGT's results concern "important but specific algorithms" and "certain structured classes"; their own bounds are algorithm-independent but use functions of dimension polynomial in 1/ε, while CGT's are one-dimensional. Both are right about different classes.
7. **Books.** Cherruault praises the TR book as "very complete". Toint (podcast ASR) is "not so sure" a "massive book" was "a great idea".
8. **Reddi et al. example.** Toint describes it as depending on "the nonzero variance of the noise". The theorem itself is stated for a deterministic periodic online sequence (§5). This is a difference in framing, recorded as such.
