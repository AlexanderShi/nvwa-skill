## 1. The code as a research record (layer 4: execution)

### 1.1 filterSD: repository structure and history [practice, primary]

- **What is in it.** 19 Fortran 77 files, 13,832 lines, plus three PDF manuals (`README.pdf`, `filterSD.pdf`, `glcpd.pdf`), four example drivers on Hock–Schittkowski problems (`hs72.f`, `hs72d.f`, `hs106.f`, `hs106d.f`: sparse and dense versions of each) and one CUTEr driver (`driver.f` with `user.f`). The README says "This package does not require any Third Party software." Everything, including sparse LU updating, ordering and the QP/LCP solvers, is his own.
- **Modular, swappable linear algebra.** The same solver runs on dense (`denseA.f` + `denseL.f`) or sparse (`sparseA.f` + `schurQR.f` or `sparseL.f`) modules. README 2.0: "The file schurQR.f implements a Schur complement scheme for sparse matrix updates. This replaces an a previous file sparseL.f implementing Fletcher-Matthews updates, which is also included in the distribution. These files are interchangeable." He kept the superseded module in the release rather than deleting it.
- **Decades-old components carried forward.** Header dates (each file carries "Copyright" and "Current version dated" lines, a manual form of version control):
  - `denseA.f`: © 1996, version 21 May 1998; `denseL.f`: © 1996, version 16 April 2002 in release 1.0, 4 October 2011 in 2.0; `util.f`: © 1996, 06 January 1998 → 26 May 2011; `sparseA.f`: © 1996 / "Current version dated 31/01/07" and 9 December 2010; `sparseL.f`: "Copyright, University of Dundee (R.Fletcher), January 1998 / Current version dated 16/04/02".
  - `sparseL.f` still begins "Basis matrix routines for bqpd with sparse matrices" and describes workspace needed by "the current version of bqpd.f". These are the bqpd (1990s QP solver) modules reused in a 2011 package.
  - New in 2010–13: `filterSD.f` (© 2010, version 5 Oct 2011), `glcpd.f` (version 27 Mar 2013), `qlcpd.f` (18 Apr 2013), `schurQR.f` (© 2011, 17 Jan 2012), `checkd.f` (20 Jan 2011), `checkg.f` (11 Jan 2011), `l1sold.f` (© 2010).
- **Engineering claims inside the code are experience claims.** `sparseL.f`: "The use of a Tarjan+spk1 ordering algorithm to control the length of these spikes has proved quite effective. The factors are updated by a variant of the Fletcher-Matthews method, which has proved very reliable in practice. However the B matrix is re-factored every 30 updates to control growth in the total spike length." And: "So far 10^6 locations has proved adequate for problems of up to 5000 variables." [practice + stated, primary] His 1984 paper with Matthews (DOI 10.1007/BF02591933, "Stable modification of explicit LU factors for simplex updates") is here a working component 27 years later. The header cites his own background papers: "Dense Factors of Sparse Matrices" (Powell tribute volume, CUP 1997, pp. 145–166) and "Block Triangular Orderings and Factors for Sparse Matrices in LP" (*Numerical Analysis 1997*, Pitman Research Notes in Mathematics 380, 1998, pp. 91–110); both titles, years and pages also confirmed in the Beebe bibliography (entries Fletcher:1997:DFS, Fletcher:1998:BTO). Neither text read.
- **Commit history (who touched the code).** `4dc3016` 2011-10-29 "Initial import" (committer "coin"); `34f710c` 2012-01-27 "Set stable 1.0 and release 1.0.0"; `205fda2`/`8462d85` 2013-05-29 "Release 2.0"; `7a0f787` 2015-11-12 "Bug fix in schurQR.f"; 2019–2020 COIN-OR metadata commits by Stefan Vigerske. All release and fix commits are by Frank E. Curtis. Fletcher never appears as a committer. [practice, primary record] [inferred] Fletcher developed without version control and handed tarballs to a steward; the "Current version dated" headers were his versioning.
- **The only post-release bug fix** (2015, by Curtis) adds `double precision Q_` to two subroutines (`aqsol`, `eptsol`) in `schurQR.f`: an implicit-typing slip (variables beginning with Q are integers under his `implicit ... integer (i-q)` convention). [practice, primary] [inferred] a direct cost of the Fortran 77 implicit-typing style he kept.
- **Release 1.0 → 2.0 (Jan 2012 → May 2013)** [practice, primary; `git diff releases/1.0.0 releases/2.0.0`]:
  - Refactoring: about 800 lines removed from `glcpd.f` and `qlcpd.f` into a new `shared.f` ("subordinate routines shared by glcpd.f, qlcpd.f and ql1lcpd.f"). `ql1lcpd.f` is not in any release: an unreleased variant.
  - `sparseL.f` restored as an interchangeable alternative to `schurQR.f`.
  - Manuals gained parameter warnings learned after release 1.0: "The default value of mxmc=500 is not suitable for use when using schurQR.f or sparseL.f and should be reset to a smaller value (typically 25)." (filterSD.pdf 2.0, §4 "Common blocks", p. 5) and "When sparseL.f is used in place of schurQR.f, a similar number should be set (historically 30 has been used)." (glcpd.pdf 2.0).
  - Small fixes: `checkd.f` output label corrected from "c at x+h, c at h" to "c at x+h, c at x"; `denseL.f` initialises a counter (`nup=0`); `filterSD.f` returns early `if(ifail.ge.7)`.

### 1.2 The CUTEr driver: a working test harness left in the release [practice, primary]

`driver.f` (release 2.0.1) is not a tidy example; it is the harness he used, with its history visible:

- **Per-problem workspace settings kept as comments**: "c  this one for GASOIL" (`mxws=50000000`, raised from 35000000 in release 1.0), "c  this one for PINENE", "c  this one for MARINE and METHANOL" (active), "c  this one otherwise". These are the largest CUTEr/COPS-type problems; 5×10⁷ doubles is 400 MB of real workspace. [practice; the byte count is my arithmetic]
- **Parameter sweeps by overwriting**: `rho=2.D1`, `rho=25.D-2`, `rho=1.D-1`, `rho=1.D1`, `rho=1.D0` in sequence (the last assignment wins); likewise `maxit=50` then `maxit=999`, `mxgr=400` then `mxgr=100`. Release 1.0 had only `rho=1.D1` and `ubd=1.D1`. [practice] [inferred] tuning by editing and re-running, leaving the trail.
- **Hard cases pinned as starting points** (added between releases 1.0 and 2.0): "c  HEART6 with 4 near zero c/s", "c  HEART6 with 5 near zero c/s", "c  PFIT1 with 2 near zero c/s", each followed by a commented `data y/.../` vector with 7-digit values, and a commented loop copying `y` into `x`. He saved iterates where constraints are nearly active (near-degenerate) so the failure could be re-run directly. [practice] [inferred] linked to the degeneracy work published as the 2014 Wolfe's-method paper (DOI 10.1137/130930522), which went to press in the same period.
- **Robustness probe**: a commented loop `x(i)=x(i)+dble(i)*0.01` perturbs the starting point.
- **Variants run through the same driver**: `c     call filterSD2(...` and `c     call filter3(...` above the live `call filterSD(...`. Neither variant is in any release. [practice; status of the variants unknown]
- **Automatic retry**: `if(ifail.eq.4.and.h.gt.ubd)then / ubd=11.D-1*h / goto10`: when the run stops because the constraint violation exceeds the filter's upper bound, raise the bound by 10% and re-run. (Present in both releases.)
- **One-line-per-problem results log**: after the run the driver appends to unit 99 a fixed-format line `pname, n, m, f, h, rgnorm, k, itn, nft, ngt` and "fail" plus the code on failure. [practice] This is the raw material of the result tables in his papers (problem, sizes, function and gradient counts).
- **Derivative check before solving**: commented block setting `al(i)=1.D-2*x(i)`, `call checkd(...)`, `stop`, i.e. check derivatives first, then comment out and solve. The manual prescribes the same sequence: "A successful check is recognised by the messages 'entering checkd' followed by 'exiting checkd', whereupon the call to checkd can be commented out." (filterSD.pdf §5)

### 1.3 How he debugged (inside the code) [practice, primary]

- **Print traces kept, commented out.** About 744 commented-out `print`/`write` statements in 13,832 lines (e.g. 210 in `schurQR.f`, 204 in `sparseL.f`, 92 in `filterSD.f`). He did not delete instrumentation after use.
- **Iteration-triggered tracing.** `filterSD.f` passes a flag `iii` to the LCP solver, switched on at one chosen iteration: release 1.0 has `c     if(itn.eq.164)iii=1`, release 2.0 has `c     if(itn.eq.14)iii=1`. The number changed between releases: he was chasing a specific iteration of a specific run.
- **NaN trap.** `user.f` (2.0) contains a commented test `if(c(j)-c(j).ne.0.D0)then` that prints x and c and stops, the x − x ≠ 0 test for NaN or Inf.
- **Internal consistency checks with hard stops**, active in the release: `denseL.f` "malfunction in reset sequence in start_up", "malfunction in re_factor", "malfunction detected in eptsol", "malfunction detected in c_flma" and "... r_flma", each followed by `stop`; `filterSD.f` prints "malfunction in phase 1 LCP subproblem" / "phase 2 LCP subproblem" / "projection subproblem". `denseL.f` prints the factorisation residual "error in LA=U is ... growth in L =" unconditionally (a commented line shows it was once conditional on `emax.gt.tol.or.gmax.gt.1.D1`).
- **Checkers left for users.** `glcpd.f` contains `subroutine check` (index bookkeeping, "indexing error", "faulty free variable") and `qlcpd.f` `checkq`, `shared.f` `checkT`. glcpd.pdf: "The file glcpd.f contains a subroutine check which can carry out checks as the iteration proceeds. There are two calls to check from glcpd but these are normally commented out. In case of failure, further information as to the cause may be obtained by reinstating these calls."
- **Derivative checker with a bracketing test.** `checkd.f`: "If the difference quotient estimate lies between the derivatives at x and x+h (h is the perturbation stored in in al) then the derivative is assumed to be correct." [practice; "in in" is in the original] The test does not need a tolerance tuned to the difference interval, which is the usual weak point of finite-difference checks. [inferred]
- **Platform residue.** `util.f` keeps commented calls to Sun Fortran's `ieee_flags`/`ieee_handler` for overflow trapping, with a note that the call "should be removed" if the driver does not set the handler. [practice] Era marker: the 1990s–2000s Sun workstation environment of NA/181 ("the flag -r8 in the SUN f77 compiler").
- **Robustness devices written into the solvers** (glcpd/qlcpd header comments): iterative refinement "a fixed number nrep of such repeats"; restart "In case of any breakdown, the code is restarted in mode 0" (glcpd) / "usually in mode 2" (qlcpd), with a cap `nres`; periodic refactorisation `mxmc`; automatic loosening of the reduced-gradient tolerance "If at any stage the accuracy requirement rgtol < sgnf*rgnorm then rgtol is increased to sgnf*rgnorm". The degeneracy handler `l1sold.f` states: "A modified form of Wolfe's method is used to resolve degeneracy."

### 1.4 filterSQP and bqpd (1998–2000s) [practice, primary manual; secondary distribution page]

- **Manual with a changelog.** NA/181 is dated "April 1998 / Version 1, June 1998 / Updated, March 1999", and its §7 "Changes to earlier Versions" lists: June 1998 "Parameter istat now has 13 entries. Record crashes of bqpd ifail = 9. Facility for changing to double precision added."; September 1998 "Modified termination criterion (scaled)."; November 1998 "Added common ubdc for upper bound."; January 1999 "Added warm start for NLP solver."; March 1999 ifail indicator and statistics changed, a `readpar` routine, and an interface section. Five dated revisions in the eleven months after the first issue (April 1998), which itself followed the filter report NA/171 (Sept 1997) by seven months.
- **Heuristics instrumented in the iteration log.** "Finally XX is nonblank if any heuristics were activated during the step ( UB = upper bound, NW = North West corner rule, SE = South East corner rule)." The NW/SE corner rules are the ones the co-authored 2006 history later reports "were shown to be redundant in the subsequent convergence analysis" (see agent 01). He logged every firing of each heuristic before theory removed them. [practice + inferred link]
- **Crash handling and advice by symptom.** "Following a crash of bqpd the trust-region radius is reduced and the QP problem is re-solved in cold-start mode." "On some problems, the solver re-enters feasibility restoration many times. In that case it may be better to use a tighter upper bound of say, ubd = 10, tt = 0.0001." Crashes are also counted in `istat(14)`.
- **Single precision by default in 1998.** "It is possible to compile the code in double precision using the flag -r8 in the SUN f77 compiler." "Changing from single to double precision is recommended for large or ill-conditioned problems." This is the code behind the 2015 story (Optima 99, p. 5 [stated]): Powell's maxim that a good algorithm should work in single precision, which he "sort of believed ... until I started to solve LP problems", and "I used software to cut down my single precision version to half precision, and it works even more badly." filterSD (2010–13) is written `implicit double precision` throughout. [practice across versions]
- **Interfaces on request, not bundled.** "We can provide interfaces to AMPL or CUTE [1] upon request."
- **Distribution model.** Leyffer's "Dundee solvers" page (bqpd, filterSQP, MINLPBB, MIQPBB): "If you wish to take out a licence (or to discuss the matter further) please contact us"; "You might also wish to sample some of our software. This can be done using the NEOS server at Argonne National Laboratory."; the solvers are also listed in the commercial TOMLAB/MINLP interface. The bqpd blurb claims it is "effective in both single and double precision". [practice, secondary page hosted by his co-author] filterSD in 2011 went the other way: open source under the Eclipse Public License on COIN-OR. So the distribution practice changed from licensed (1990s–2000s) to open source (2011). Why is **not documented** anywhere I read.
- bqpd and filterSQP **source code was not inspected**: it is licensed, not public.

### 1.5 Harwell library routines, 1969–1973 [practice, primary record: HSL Archive specs]

- Nine current HSL Archive specification sheets give "Origin: R. Fletcher, Harwell" (or "ex-Harwell"): VE01 (Dec 1969, linearly constrained minimisation, "A generalization of Davidon's method"), LA02 and VE02 (July 1970, feasible point and QP), VE03 (June 1971), VA08 (Jan 1972, conjugate gradients), VA09 and VA10 (April 1972, quasi-Newton with and without derivatives), VB01 (June 1972, nonlinear least squares), VE04 (April 1973, bound-constrained QP, method of Fletcher & Jackson). Each ships a single- and a double-precision version (e.g. `VA09A`/`VA09AD`). The sheets are HSL's current text, possibly re-edited since 1972. [practice, primary record]
- **Paper → code → documented comparison → user recommendation**, within two years of the 1970 BFGS paper. VA09 spec: "The method used is a quasi-Newton method described by Fletcher (Computer Journal, 13, p.317 (1970))". It lists why it beats the older library routine VA01, including: "It uses a crude line search which has been shown to be more efficient than an accurate line search." and the LDLᵀ representation that keeps H positive definite under round-off (the subject of Fletcher & Powell 1974, DOI 10.1090/s0025-5718-1974-0359297-1; not read). The comparison evidence is a Harwell report: "some comparisons (R. Fletcher, AERE Report R-7125 (1972)) indicate that VA06 is marginally slower and more affected by round off error."
- **The competitor's code as the fallback.** VA06 is Powell's routine (its own spec: "The method used is described by M.J.D. Powell in a Harwell report R.6469, 1970"). The VA09 spec: "As VA06 is more difficult to use, it is suggested that VA09 should be used in the first instance on any problem. If VA09 fails then VA06 should be tried as it is guaranteed to converge if the effect of rounding errors can be neglected." [practice; whether Fletcher or HSL editors wrote this sentence is not known]
- **Improving his own 1964 method in code.** VA08: "The program differs from the ALGOL program given by Fletcher and Reeves in that a more efficient linear search is used requiring on average about two evaluations of F(x) per iteration. See A.E.R.E. Report R-7073 (1972)."
- The Harwell reports R-6370 ("A Fortran subroutine for general quadratic programming", 1970, 14 pp.) and the 1972 "Fortran subroutines for minimization by quasi-Newton methods" (29 pp.; R-7125 per the VA09/VA10 specs) are listed in the Beebe bibliography; **not read**.

### 2.2 How test problems were chosen or built [practice, primary]

- **Build a library when none exists.** NA/210 §4 introduces MacMPEC ("over 100 MPECs"; 137 used in Table 2), written in AMPL and hosted at Dundee, with a classification string for every problem and a table of size quantiles. Stated motive: "The motivation for collecting this test set is two-fold. Firstly, we are interested in testing our own ideas on a large collection of test problems." (p. 7) The random generator for the flp4-* problems is published as a Matlab file. [stated + practice, co-authored] The MacMPEC page itself is Leyffer's ("Aug. 1, 2000; last update August 5, 2005"), so [inferred] Leyffer did the curation.
- **Select by the structural property the method targets.** NA/223 p. 15: "The results in this section are sampled from CUTE test problems in which the dimension d of the null space at the solution is a significant proportion of n." Where d is unknown he estimates it from the baseline run: "In the table an approximate value of d is given based on the size of the QP reduced Hessian on termination of the filter2 run." (p. 16)
- **Generate random instances with controlled difficulty.** PBB 2005 (p. 27): "We have generated 15 random box constrained SPD test problems with n = 1000", with active constraints at the solution "ranging from 14 to 885" and condition numbers "from 10⁴ to 10⁶"; 50 random problems for Tables 2–3; for indefinite problems each setting "10 times and so have obtained 120 groups of function values" (p. 44). Cheap runs for design choices: "Nevertheless, to save time we use random positive definte test problems with smaller dimensions in §2 to compare different choices for the steplength." ("definte" as printed) [co-authored]
- **Construct a problem whose answer is known, to see the mechanism.** ERGO 09-014: quadratics "with λ1 = 1 and the other eigenvalues in geometric progression with ratio √2" (p. 5) and ratio 2 (p. 6); for the non-quadratic case a Trigonometric instance where "A solution x∗ is designated as a vector of random numbers, uniformly distributed in ±π" and the start is x* perturbed by ±10⁻⁵ (p. 11). His own 1963 test function (Trigonometric, Fletcher & Powell) and his own Laplace2 problem sit next to Toint's Chained Rosenbrock, Raydan's Convex 2 and CUTEr (p. 13).
- **Standard sets for breadth.** Nonmonotone filter (2009): "all 411 CUTEr ... problems with less than 100 variables or constraints that are available in AMPL" (p. 21) [co-authored]. filterSD: "Extensive testing of the code indicates that a high percentage of test problems (mostly from CUTEr) have been solved effectively." (filterSD.pdf §8). The shipped examples are Hock–Schittkowski HS72 and HS106; filterSQP's example is from Duran & Grossmann (MINLP).
- **An application user's problem as motivation.** NA/223 p. 2: the Yagi–Uda antenna "shown to me by Martijn van Beurden" with measured runtimes and memory on NEOS (agent 02 §2.2). The 2017 box-QP paper reports "a range of practical problems of up to 10⁸ variables from various sources" (abstract, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drx002; text not read).

### 2.3 What he did first, and in what order [practice, primary]

- **LMSD (2009), single-author.** (1) Does the idea beat its m = 1 special case on a clean quadratic? "Firstly it is important to establish whether or not the sweep method improves on the BB method (m = 1) as the number of back vectors m is increased." (p. 5; agent 02). (2) Compare against the obvious alternative built from the same parts (m steps of CG per sweep, Table 1). (3) A diagnostic on a non-quadratic problem with the exact Hessian spectrum supplied, tracing each eigencomponent of the gradient step by step (Table 2). The trace shows where the design breaks: "Thus the last non-monotonic step is a disaster as regards providing local convergence." (p. 11). The fix follows from the trace: replace that step by a line search, and end the sweep when ‖g‖ rises. (4) Generalisation choices justified by observed numerics: "In practice I have observed that, due to ill-conditioning in R, the product with R⁻¹ in (15) most seriously effects [sic] the upper triangle of T. Therefore my approach has been to construct a symmetric tridiagonal matrix" (p. 12); line search: "I have chosen the latter as it ensures that the resulting ~T has at least one positive eigenvalue." (p. 12). He also states "None of these issues admits a single obvious solution, and various possibilities might be followed up." (p. 12). (5) Only then the standard-problem tables and timings.
- **Low-rank QN for SQP (2005).** Theory first (hereditary and conjugacy theorem, §7, p. 14), then "some preliminary practical experience" (p. 15) on selected problems, with the code itself not finished: filterQN "is currently under development, and indeed has been so for some time" (agent 02).
- **Filter (1996–2002).** Talk (May 1996) → report with numerical results (NA/171, Sept 1997) → user manual and code (NA/181, April 1998) → convergence theory with Toint, Gould and Wächter (2002) → heuristics dropped (agent 01 §2.4). The numerics and the software came before the proofs.
- **MPECs (2002, co-authored).** Build the library and an AMPL interface that passes the MPEC to the NLP solver as an NLP ("filtermpec works both as an NLP and as an MPEC solver", NA/210 p. 6), then run every NEOS solver, then diagnose failures. A local-convergence theory preprint with Ralph and Scholtes appeared three months earlier (Optimization Online 2002/05/476; *SIAM J. Optim.* 17:259–286, 2006, DOI 10.1137/S1052623402407382). So here theory and numerics ran in parallel, not numerics-then-theory.
- **PBB (2005, co-authored).** Test the unmodified method on random problems; observe that failure "would appear to be very unlikely in practice" (p. 27); still construct a 2-D counterexample in which it cycles (§3); then design a line search that keeps the unmodified steps; then compare with GPCG.
- **Harwell (1969–73).** Publish the method (1970), code it into the site library with single and double versions (1972), compare against existing routines in an internal report (R-7125), write the recommendation into the spec.

### 2.4 Performance measures he used [practice, primary]

- Counts of gradient calls (#g) and function calls, sweeps, line searches (ERGO 09-014 Tables 1–6; NA/223 Tables 1–2: "One gradient call includes the evaluation of both the gradient of the objective function and the Jacobian"). QP solves "roughly proportional to CPU time" (ANL/MCS-P1679-0909 p. 21, co-authored).
- Storage in "long vectors" (ERGO 09-014 p. 14; agent 02 §4.2).
- Both iterations and CPU in the MPEC study, with a reason: "We use both number of iterations and CPU time as performance measures, since the former allows comparison between similar solvers excluding the effect of fast linear algebra." (NA/210 p. 12, co-authored)
- **Performance profiles** (Dolan–Moré) appear only in the co-authored Leyffer papers (2002, 2009). His single-author reports I read (NA/223, ERGO 09-014) use per-problem tables. [practice] [inferred] the profile habit came from the Leyffer side.
- Machine and compiler are stated: "COMPAQ Evo N800v laptop (clock speed 1.3 GHz) under Linux, with optimized code from the Intel F90 compiler" (ERGO 09-014 p. 13).

## 3. Judging results (layer 5)

- **Classify failures before counting them** [practice, co-authored]. NA/210 pp. 9–10 separates "Catastrophic failures" (iteration limit, trust region or step too small at a non-stationary point) from "Soft failures" (local infeasibility, "ordinary exits"), then: "In the context of evaluating the suitability of NLP solvers for solving MPECs, we view only the catastrophic failures as problematic." In the performance profiles a catastrophic failure is scored #iter = 10⁶ and CPU = 10⁸ so that the failure rate shows as the right-hand asymptote; infeasibility exits are not penalised (p. 12).
- **Test the popular explanation of a failure, and correct others' attributions** [practice, co-authored]. On earlier studies: "Previous numerical studies of MPECs and complementarity problems have sometimes made erroneous conclusions, attributing the failure of solvers to the failure of MFCQ." (p. 10) For LUBRIF: "However, examining the model it turns out that this model does not have a complementarity constraint. Instead, complementarity is minimized and is therefore not responsible for this failure." (p. 3) Then a controlled test on their own data: replace complementarity by z₁ᵀz₂ ≤ 10⁻² and rerun LOQO and KNITRO: "This experiment will give an indication as to whether the failures are due to the absence of MFCQ or not." (p. 10). Result: LOQO "still fails on 11 out of 20" (p. 10), so its failures "are not solely due to the fact that the MPECs violate MFCQ" (p. 11).
- **Size claims to the design of the comparison** [practice, co-authored]: "Since the random problems differ from [20], any conclusion based on these instances is by necessity tentative." (NA/210 p. 15); "All three comparisons, while tentative by design, point to the potential for NLP solvers when solving MPECs." (p. 17). PBB: "it is difficult to read too much into this comparison since different methods may find different local minimizers." (p. 44)
- **Say when something is unexplained** [practice, co-authored]: SNOPT's poor results on flp4-*: "It is not immediately clear, why this should be." (NA/210 p. 15). Single-author parallels are in agents 01/02 (Chained Rosenbrock, "no conclusive outcome either way").
- **Mark where the baseline fails, not only where the new code fails** [practice]: NA/223 Table 1 flags HS90 and HS92 with "* filter2 finds a locally infeasible point".
- **Attribute each bad number to a mechanism** [practice]: "For HS101 and HS102, filterQN spends about 200 and 180 iterations respectively in feasibility restoration, which accounts for the poor performance." (NA/223 p. 16); the nonmonotone filter's gain is attributed, hedged, to not invoking SOC steps far from the solution: "We believe that some of this improvement can be attributed to the fact that FASTr does not invoke SOC steps far from the solution." (ANL/MCS-P1679-0909 p. 21, co-authored)
- **Declare the known weak spots of released software** [practice]: filterSD.pdf §8: "Extensive testing of the code indicates that a high percentage of test problems (mostly from CUTEr) have been solved effectively. However some negative features have been observed and were indeed expected." It names the weakness (many gradient calls when the null space is not small) and the workaround (`mxgr`, relaxing `rgtol`). On a parameter he could not settle: "Clearly this is a matter for experimentation." On local infeasibility: "Otherwise the only advice I can offer is to re-run with different starting points or parameter values. One way of doing this (not guaranteed to work, of course) is to relax the bounds on all the nonlinear constraints by a smallish amount such that filterSD returns a feasible solution. Then re-run from this solution with the bounds restored."
- **Publish an anomaly early with a tentative explanation, return later with theory** [practice, primary abstracts]: 1981, educational testing: "some difficulties over slow convergence are observed in some cases and a possible explanation is given" (*SIAM J. Sci. Stat. Comput.*, DOI 10.1137/0902021). 1985: "Semi-Definite Matrix Constraints in Optimization" (*SIAM J. Control Optim.* 23:493–513, DOI 10.1137/0323032; not read). His 2015 account of this episode (three M.Sc. students, nondifferentiable eigenvalue constraints) is in agent 01 §2.6.

## 4. Problem choice and idea generation as they show in practice (layers 2–3)

- **Ideas come out of a code that does not work yet** [stated, primary]: the 2017 box-QP method: "It came about because I was trying to work out why I couldn't get MINRES to work on the Rockafellar's augmented Lagrangian, and I was messing about doing things to see why it wasn't working and just came across it." (Optima 99, p. 4). Same pattern as LMSD (CG unsuitable inside his SLP code) and the filter (SQP steps thrown away by penalty functions), both in agents 01/02.
- **Iterate on one subproblem for decades, replacing his own earlier answers** [practice + stated]: degeneracy. 1988 "Degeneracy in the presence of roundoff errors" (*Linear Algebra Appl.*, DOI 10.1016/0024-3795(88)90026-2); 1993 "Resolving degeneracy in quadratic programming" (*Ann. Oper. Res.*, DOI 10.1007/BF02023102); 1998 abstract: "In contrast to a previous method of the author, this method works throughout in the primal space." (*SIAM J. Optim.*, DOI 10.1137/S1052623494277470); 2014 Wolfe's method paper (DOI 10.1137/130930522), whose abstract says "heuristics are described that have proved to be very reliable in practice"; in code, `l1sold.f` uses "A modified form of Wolfe's method". His summary: "There are a lot of different methods for doing degeneracy. But eventually I settled on Wolfe's method" (Optima 99, p. 4). About 26 years from the first paper to the settled method.
- **Enter a new field by first finding what is wrong with its standard algorithm** [practice, primary abstract]: SVM training at about age 70: the standard dual-based algorithm "is shown to be deficient in certain aspects, and we develop a new primal-based SQP-like algorithm" (Fletcher & Zanghirati, *Acta Numerica* 2010, DOI 10.1017/S0962492910000024; text not read).
- **Tailor the interface, not the algorithm** [practice, co-authored]: for MPECs the solver was left a general NLP method; the new work was an AMPL interface adding slacks and one aggregated complementarity equation, "at negligible additional overhead" (NA/210 pp. 5–6), plus a small perturbation heuristic (3.4) whose "Properties ... are currently being investigated".

He still wrote every line, including competitors' implementations for comparisons (ERGO 09-014). ## Contradictions (kept, not reconciled)

1. **Finite differences.** 1972: he wrote and shipped VA10, a quasi-Newton routine "in which derivatives are estimated by differences" (HSL spec). 2010–13: "users are advised not to use finite difference approximations to the gradients as an alternative to providing exact formulae" (glcpd.pdf §9; agent 02). Different codes and 40 years apart; recorded as a dated change, not reconciled.
2. **Initial trust-region radius.** filterSQP manual (1999): "The initial trust region radius is usually not critical to the success of the algorithm." filterSD manual (2011): choice of initial rho "can be important" (§8), and his own `driver.f` contains five successive `rho` settings. Different algorithms (SQP vs SLCP), so possibly both true.
3. **Test problems.** Stated (2015): "I'd rather see half a dozen real industrial problems" than "500 test problems from CUTE" (agent 01, C6). Practice (2009–13): his harness is a CUTEr driver; filterSD's claim rests on "test problems (mostly from CUTEr)"; the 2009 co-authored paper runs all 411 small CUTEr/AMPL problems. Counter-evidence in practice: the antenna problem (2005), SVM data (2010), 10⁸-variable practical problems (2017 abstract).
4. **Single precision.** The bqpd description on the Dundee/Argonne page claims it is "effective in both single and double precision"; the 1998 manual recommends double "for large or ill-conditioned problems"; in 2015 he says LP work changed his mind about single precision. The dates of the page text and of his change of mind are unknown.
5. **Author order.** Agent 01 treats his author lists as alphabetical, with Shen, Leyffer & Fletcher (2012) as an exception. The preprint of that same paper (2009) is alphabetical; only the journal version is not.
6. **Numerics before theory?** The filter record fits "code and numbers first, proofs later" (agent 01). The MPEC record does not: the local-convergence theory preprint (May 2002) came three months before the numerical report (Aug 2002). Both are co-authored with Leyffer.
7. **Failures "atypical" but guarded against.** PBB 2005: failure "would appear to be very unlikely in practice", yet the paper builds a cycling counterexample and adds a line search. NA/223 2005: lack of global QP solutions shows "little evidence that this is a serious difficulty in practice", yet the design guarantees them (agent 02, C3). His practice guards against cases he states are rare.
