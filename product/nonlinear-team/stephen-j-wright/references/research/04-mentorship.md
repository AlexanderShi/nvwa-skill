## 1. Research taste passed on (layer 1)

**1.1 "1/√T is a negative result."** [observed, S, recurring saying]
- According to Ben Recht (co-author and former UW colleague), "Regretfully Yours", argmin.net, 2023-09-07: "Steve Wright, inspired by one of his colleagues at NC State, always said that 1/√T is a negative result. I agree! It's hard to find an algorithm that converges more slowly than 1/√T."
- "always said" marks this as a repeated saying, the kind of standard students absorb without seeing it in a paper. The source is his NC State period (1986–90).
- I did not find the saying in Wright's own writing this run (checked the PCMI notes, the NIPS 2010 slides and arXiv:2510.15734). It stays [observed] only.

**1.2 Methods are tools in a tool chest; matching method to problem is the skill.** [observed, S] and [stated, P]
- According to Recht, "There is no optimum", argmin.net, 2023-08-01: "Steve Wright described optimization methods as implements in a tool chest. Each has a rough guideline, but you never know which will be right for the job until you dig in."
- According to Recht, "Modeling Dystopia", argmin.net, 2024-09-19: "Steve Wright thinks that optimization is best approached like an old tool chest in the garage. You're going to have a wide assortment of formulations and methods. Keep that assortment well organized in your head. Learn about what all of the tools are, how to use them, and when they are best applied."
- Wright's own words confirm this [stated, P], NIPS tutorial slides, 6 Dec 2010, slide 2: "there is a growing emphasis on "picking and choosing" algorithmic elements to fit the characteristics of a given application — building up a suitable algorithm from a "toolkit" of components. It's more important than ever to understand the fundamentals of algorithms as well as the demands of the application, so that good choices are made in matching algorithms to applications."
- What he said (2010) and what a colleague reports (2023, 2024) agree, so this is a transmitted value.

**1.3 A complexity-guaranteed method should still look like the textbook method and work in practice.** [observed, S] plus [inferred]
- According to Clément Royer (postdoc 2016–19), a news item on his Dauphine homepage (now commented out in the HTML) about the work with Curtis, Robinson and Wright (SIAM J. Optim. 31 (2021) 518–544, doi:10.1137/19M130563X): "We were able to design a variant of the Newton-trust region algorithm that achieves the best known complexity guarantees, looks a lot like the textbook method, and works well in practice!"
- In his 2025 HDR, Royer frames his own later programme the same way: "By introducing tweaks to the original algorithm, one can equip them with complexity bounds that reflect their original empirical behavior." (Royer HDR, ch. 3 intro)
- [inferred] This taste matches what Wright states in 02-methodology.md: complexity-first modifications do not pay off unless the method stays practical. It looks like a value the postdoc took with him. Royer does not attribute it to Wright, so the link is my inference.

**1.4 What he values in a senior colleague.** [stated, P, a quote inside a secondary article]
- On Michael Ferris (UW CS news, 2026-06-19): "His ability to work with domain scientists and engineers to leverage the power of optimization in their own research areas is unparalleled." And: "a person whose advice I value very highly."
- On younger researchers (SIAM profile Q&A, reproduced by UW CS news 2024-09-04): "The skill level of younger researchers working in optimization is incredible, and both daunting and inspiring."

## 2. Choosing problems, and how students join (layer 2)

**2.1 Students are recruited from his graduate courses.** [stated, P]
- "the students that I've recruited have typically come from graduate classes, they're not students that are new as undergrads. So they've typically taken the classes and then said, "Well, I'm doing well, in this class, I'm really interested in doing a PhD in this area."" (OH22 [35:54])
- Supporting evidence [observed, S]. According to Sangkyun Lee, thesis 2011, p. ii: "We had a list of superb courses offered, where I had practiced the craft of optimization, and also great minds who were always open for discussion." According to Douglas Allan (ChemE), thesis 2020, p. xiv: "Professor Wright's optimization class was excellent, and he was always willing to discuss my research with me after class."
- Era and resource context [practice, P]: his homepages list no course between CS524 (Spring 2023) and CS730 (Spring 2026). He was department chair from 2023 to 2025. The old UW page's teaching line reads "Current: Not these days, baby!" (undated), and its research-interests list begins "Being Department Chair." Caveat: the teaching list is incomplete. It leaves out CS730 in Spring 2022, which the old page mentions in a comment, and the Fall 2022 graduate course he describes in OH22 [30:53]. [inferred] While he was chair, his main recruiting channel was probably narrowed.

**2.2 Postdocs are hired for theory and algorithms first, applications second.** [stated, P]
- From a postdoc advertisement on his UW homepage. It is commented out in the HTML, so no longer shown, and dates from about 2018 ("Positions are available from the present time until August 31, 2019"): "Several postdoctoral research positions are available ... to work on funded projects involving nonconvex optimization, parallel/distributed optimization, and other optimization paradigms and algorithms that are motivated by data science applications. Prior experience in developing optimization theory and algorithms is required. Experience with data science applications is desirable." Applicants send "a description of relevant prior work and the names of several references."
- Resource context [observed, S]. According to Royer's homepage (Feb 2018 item): "the MACSER: Multifaceted Mathematics for Rare, High-Impact Events in Complex Energy and Environment Systems annual meeting, held at Argonne National Laboratory ... This project and its predecessor have been supporting my appointment in Madison". So postdocs were paid from DOE/Argonne projects. From 2020 some were paid through IFDS (NSF): Alacaoglu was "partly supported by IFDS".

**2.3 Staying alert for the next big topic, which is the advice he gives.** [stated, P]
- "you just want to kind of have an early warning system, keep your antennae out there and see, if something's interesting, something that tickles your interest happens, it might blow up into a major research topic. You just have to sort of keep your finger on the pulse, I think" (OH22 [58:55]).
- [practice] His advisees' topics follow the waves he names. 2000s students worked on IMRT, SQP and active-set methods (Ólafsson, Oberlin). Around 2011 the topic was sparse and online learning (S. Lee). Around 2014 it was asynchronous parallel methods (Liu, Sridhar; Hogwild with Niu). From about 2017 it was nonconvex complexity (O'Neill, Royer, Xie), and in the 2020s robustness and learning theory (Shuyao Li) and graph-learning systems (Waleffe). Identifiers are in §0 and §6.1.

## 4. Execution: how he works with students on proofs, code and experiments (layer 4)

**4.1 He works through the students' proofs.** [observed, S]
- According to Brett Stewart (ChemE, advisor Rawlings), thesis 2010, p. ii: "I was also fortunate to have the constant help of Stephen Wright. He was always willing to devote time to my optimization problems. Because of his effort, the proofs in this thesis are immeasurably better."
- According to Aswin Venkat (ChemE), thesis 2006, p. iv: "Steve's understanding of optimization is unparalleled, and his quickness in comprehending and critically analyzing material has constantly amazed me."
- Tacit rule [inferred from the two above]: when a student from an application field brings an optimization question, Wright engages at the level of the proof and critical analysis, not only by pointing to a method.

**4.2 Students are trained to program an algorithm and also prove things about it.** [stated, P]
- "I'll ask them to go and write a program to implement a particular algorithm computationally and use it to solve problems and to make observations about how it performs in practice. And then I will also ask them to prove things about how that algorithm works, and what kinds of problems it can solve" (OH22 [40:30]). Students learn to ask "How much computer time will it take ...? Is it possible to give it a really bad problem that will confuse it ...? ... are these bad problems rare? Or are they common? ... we ask those questions in research. And we also teach students to think about questions like that." (same)
- Teaching aim [stated, P]: "teaching students not just to learn things, but also to learn how to think about things ... if someone gives them a problem from an application, hopefully, they'll have the skill to sort of distill it down to an optimization problem that they can solve" (OH22 [35:54]).

**4.3 Software was a group product at Argonne.** [stated, P] plus [practice]
- PCx (released 1997) "grew out of my research and interior point methods ... I had several postdocs, and students work on it as well" (OH22 [9:02]). Paper: Czyzyk, Mehrotra, Wagner, Wright, doi:10.1080/10556789908805757. OOQP: Gertz and Wright, doi:10.1145/641876.641880.
- Era context [stated, P]: at Argonne there was no teaching, "they had a lot of students come and visit in the summer" (OH22 [6:36]). According to Matthew Tenny (thesis 2002, p. i), his own first real contact came through that summer channel (quote in §7.2).
- [inferred] After 2001 the software role moved to short research codes such as GPSR and SpaRSA, "pretty short codes" released freely (OH22 [9:02]). Students' theses at UW are proof- and algorithm-centred, not package-centred. I found no UW-era student-maintained solver package.

## 8. Tacit knowledge (what the students know that the papers do not say)

4. Proofs get checked hard. Expect them to come back "immeasurably better" (Stewart 2010).
5. A complexity-guaranteed variant should still look like the textbook method and work in practice (Royer; §1.3; the link to Wright is inferred).

7. Papers are small-author-list journal papers, student first, Wright last (practice; §6.1).

Lee (2011; §7.3) names Ferris, Linderoth, Miller and Robinson, and his committee included Recht. Co-advisors elsewhere include Linderoth and Luedtke (Sridhar) and J. Diakonikolas (S. Li).
